ACT I · THE WRONG SEASON

The ground was keeping a list of its own.

01 · The Box at the Gate

An upland health subcentre near Sogeri · morning

The vaccine case was holding at four degrees. The generator outside the health subcentre was not.

It coughed twice, recovered, and returned to its thin metallic whine. Nara Okafor kept one finger against the insulated case until its thermometer completed another reading. The nurse had the receiving log open on her knee. Beside the log was a drawing of a truck with twelve wheels and no driver.

RuthHe always gave them too many wheels.

Nara looked up.

NaraFor bad roads, perhaps.

RuthFor making sure it couldn't get stuck.

The drawing belonged to Samuel Laku, seven, missing since Monday afternoon. Under his name someone had written THREE NIGHTS in thick marker on a sheet pinned to the clinic wall. The photograph beside it showed a narrow boy in a school shirt, one hand behind his back, smiling as if he had been told to and was waiting for permission to stop.

It was Thursday. Heat had settled over the plateau despite a night of rain lower down. Beyond the screened window, the air over the track shimmered above wet stones. An insect worked patiently against the glass. Every thirty seconds the generator's exhaust blew its body a little sideways.

Nara checked the vaccine seals, recorded the temperature and counted the vials with Ruth. It was small, honest work. She had driven up from Port Moresby with two cool boxes, three cartons of dressings and a replacement temperature probe. The road had taken longer than the schedule allowed; another clinic was already waiting for her call.

She liked the mathematics of it. Every place represented a person she had not met who would suffer if the chain broke. A late delivery could be forgiven. Spoiled stock could not be argued back into use.

A man in a dust-streaked shirt appeared in the doorway, carrying a paper map protected inside a plastic sleeve. He was broad across the shoulders, but the way he moved around the children's posters was careful.

VeleYou're the woman from the supply truck.

NaraDepends who's complaining.

He did not smile.

VeleSergeant Daniel Vele. Criminal investigations. Ruth says you noticed something unusual on the road up.

NaraI noticed a strip of grass with the wrong colour. That isn't a missing-child lead unless someone saw him there.

VeleSomeone did. Or says they did.

He laid the map over the counter without moving the vaccine log. His pencil marks followed the creek, the school path and a ruined house above an old orchard. Twice his search lines had approached the orchard. Twice men had reported the scrub too thick to cross, though the local woman who owned the next plot said the path had been clear a week earlier.

VeleHis father says the boy went to his aunt's. The aunt says he never arrived. One neighbour saw him below the road on Monday. Another says she heard him crying from the direction of the old house on Tuesday. That's not the same thing as seeing him.

RuthHe didn't come to his last two follow-up appointments.

She turned a folder so the sergeant, not Nara, could read the information inside. It was a clinical document. Nara kept her eyes on the map.

RuthHe was losing weight. I reported the missed visits. We were going to send someone to the house before all this happened.

VeleThen why didn't someone?

Ruth closed the folder.

RuthAsk the list of people who had a vehicle.

The sergeant absorbed that without answering. His glance passed to Nara, then to the picture of the twelve-wheeled truck.

VeleYou came up this road recently? Before today?

The surname on the clinic sheet had made Nara remember a blue-painted gate. She could picture the left hinge, badly fastened with wire. She could picture the delivery app giving her thirty-one minutes to reach the next stop. A parcel in her hands.

NaraI've made runs here before.

It was a technically adequate answer. She felt its inadequacy at once.

Vele tapped the orchard on his map.

VeleTwo men went into that scrub yesterday because a visitor told them a vision put the boy in a cave. They lost six hours and one of them broke his ankle. I don't need another person telling me the land is speaking.

NaraGood. I don't know whether it is. But I have photographs. If you want, we can mark where each one was taken.

Ruth gave her a look: Your other clinic. The vaccines were signed over. The sealed boxes were safely inside. Nara called her dispatcher and arranged for another driver to cover the next run. She did not claim there was no cost to the change.

Outside, Vele walked to the truck with his phone held sideways, comparing her photographs to the ridge on his map.

The first showed a meadow. On the left, the grasses were brown and combed flat; on the right, they stood almost waist-high, dark and wet-looking. The dividing line crossed a gentle slope in a way water should not have done. The second photograph, taken fifty metres higher, showed the same division around a stump. Above the stump, the green narrowed. Below it, the brown broadened.

VeleDifferent soils.

NaraMaybe. Show me the soil boundary that moves uphill around a rock and then straightens again.

He zoomed into the photograph. She did not watch him change his mind. She watched him check whether he had reason to.

VeleBring your phone. No taking charge of my search.

NaraI wasn't planning to.

It was another technically adequate answer.

They drove in the sergeant's vehicle to the end of the passable track. Halfway there, Nara saw the blue gate. Someone had painted over the rust with a colour too bright for the old timber. One hinge still sagged under a twisted strand of wire.

She thought of telling him to stop. Instead she made herself memorise where it stood. The decision, she knew later, was a second delay. The first was already two months old.

02 · The Meadow That Lies

The creek grassland · midday

The grass moved before the youngest searcher put his foot down.

Not all of it. Six thin blades bent away from the empty patch where his boot was about to land. He froze with one leg raised, ridiculous and frightened, while the rest of the meadow remained perfectly still.

PhilipDid you see that?

Vele put out his hand for everybody to stop. Nara crouched without crossing the patch.

VeleGo back where you were. Slowly. Don't step on it.

Philip retreated. A second later the six blades straightened, one after another, the delay too orderly to feel like a gust.

The sergeant had recorded the movement by accident while filming the bank. He played the short clip twice. Each time, the grass bent in advance of the boot.

NaraIt may be responding to shadow or vibration. Or there's something else just outside the frame. We have one event, not an explanation.

PhilipYou don't have to explain it. It's wrong.

Nara agreed with that. It was one of the few things she was confident about.

The valley smelled of warm mud and crushed citrus leaves. Across the creek, trees formed a dense wall of green. Where there should have been a cloud of flies over the wet bank, there was almost nothing. The insects were loud behind them; ahead, their sound ended at a line Nara could mark with the edge of her shoe.

They moved in a long curve rather than marching straight toward the orchard. Vele assigned two searchers to the outer path, another to watch the creek, and told everyone to call their position before stepping off the marked ground. His radio held for most of the traverse. At the point where a dead fern lay on top of a green one, it filled with static.

Nara made a small map in her notebook. A strip of fresh growth. A ring of exhausted grass. Three bushes whose leaves had turned glossy at their tips while their stems went dull. None of those observations proved a common cause. Yet on the paper they seemed to face inward, like people in a waiting room turning towards a door.

A narrow framework of gold lines arrived in her mind—the way a route graph did when she stared at a loading schedule for too long. She could see possible dependencies, the next line after the next. She knew no one else could see them. She put actual arrows on the page only where she had a photograph, a slope or a measured position to justify one.

VeleYou draw faster than I can count.

NaraThen check me slowly. I might be drawing a beautiful mistake.

He took the notebook and looked for a compass mark. When he found none, he drew one himself. The two of them disagreed by almost fifteen degrees about which way a particular patch faced. A searcher walked to the ridge to check the bearing. Vele's was closer.

Nara crossed out her line.

They reached a ring of shrubbery that had dried from the outside inward. The leaves at its centre were unexpectedly fresh. When Nara looked back, she could still see the blue gate far down the slope, a bright speck beside the road. The disappearance, the carton she had left at that gate and the ring of plants wanted to become one story in her head. She resisted because one of the three was something she had not yet told the sergeant.

Philip lifted a fallen twig and held it beside a live branch. Both had the same tiny oval leaves, but one was so brittle it powdered against his thumb. From farther up the hill came a soft series of taps, five beats followed by a pause. Vele raised the radio. The other search teams reported no movement.

PhilipPerhaps there is a person hitting wood.

VeleThen that's where we go. Not because the grass told us. Because there's a sound.

He sent two men along the safe outer track and kept Nara with him. The taps stopped when they moved. They resumed behind them, though nobody had crossed the path.

Nara tried to keep listening in terms of duration, direction and distance. But the silence around the insects had changed her sense of scale. It felt as though they were moving across the skin of something enormous, and whatever it was had not decided if it would allow them closer.

She wrote possible common signal in the margin. Under it, in smaller letters: We have not found Sam.

03 · The Hollow Between Branches

An old hedge above the creek · afternoon

The opening was no wider than a child's shoulder. Two branches crossed over it, and between them darkness ran deeper than the hedge should have allowed. Someone had tied a strip of orange fabric to a thorn a few days earlier. The fabric was caught between living stems now, although no growth Nara knew could have closed around it that quickly.

Vele held the fabric aside. Philip, determined to be useful, pushed the blunt end of his walking stick into the gap.

The hedge folded.

It did not spring back from the stick. Its leaves turned inward in a passing seam, first near Philip's hands, then above his head, then ten metres along the bank. Branches trembled against branches with a dry, close sound. A bead of pale sap pushed out through a split in the bark and hung there while the last leaves curled.

Philip dropped the stick.

PhilipI've hurt it.

He said it with the shocked conviction of a man who had struck an animal by mistake. His hand went to the bush knife on his belt, perhaps because a blade was what he understood.

NaraDon't cut it.

PhilipIf the child is behind there—

VeleHand off the knife. Now.

Philip gave it to him. Nara stayed clear of the folded branches. She had felt an almost irresistible urge to touch the wound, to apologise to the thing that had recoiled. That urge was not evidence either.

NaraThe response was real. Pain is an interpretation. We don't need to injure it again to learn which way it moved.

Vele reviewed the clip with his thumb just above the pause control. They saw the near leaves turn, then the ones farther up the bank. The wave travelled away from the trespassers—towards the orchard.

NaraI thought it was a barrier. Something pushing us out.

VeleAnd now?

NaraIt might be a warning running inward. Or a coordinated response with no intention behind it. We can follow the direction without pretending to know who received it.

He marked the point on his map and made Philip photograph the small wound from a distance. The bead of sap darkened as they watched. No one tried another test.

The path lifted away from the creek. The trees on either side carried new shoots in strange places, one branch flowering beside another already bare. Vele had his phone in his pocket now, recording audio. When Nara spoke, she knew the line would be there if anyone later needed to know exactly what she had said.

VeleIf something can organise this many plants, would you call it intelligent?

NaraI'd call it organised. Intelligence needs more tests. A flood can route a valley. It doesn't mean the flood wants anything.

VelePeople at the subcentre keep saying an outsider came to this hill. Something from the sky.

NaraA visitor from another world might be older than us, more capable than us, and still make mistakes we recognise. If it had never been forced to ask a weaker thing what it wanted, it could be very intelligent and very bad at care.

VeleYou already decided there's a visitor.

NaraNo. I'm thinking about what we might ask if we meet one.

There was another stretch of path and heat. The leaves overhead twitched without moving enough to shade them.

NaraI think one of the hardest faults to correct in a highly intelligent being is hiding an action because it believes the action is good. The better its reasons sound to itself, the longer it may be willing to conceal the cost.

Vele gave her a look she could not read. He was not charmed by sentences. Good, she thought. Neither should she be.

VeleAnd humans?

Nara looked down at her notebook. She had written check north where he had corrected her bearing. The page had enough space for another correction.

NaraEspecially humans.

They rounded the last wall of scrub. For an instant Nara thought the ground beyond it was burning.

It was not fire. It was leaves.

ACT II · THE KEEPER

Something had been spending the valley to keep one child alive.

04 · Leaves in the Heat

The old house and its garden · late afternoon

The house stood beyond a low stone wall, half-hidden by fruit trees. Its roof had rusted into several colours of its own; a veranda post had fallen and been left where it lay. All around it, from the broken gate to the steps, leaves lay so deep that the stone path had disappeared.

Copper, amber, wine-dark red. Their colours belonged to photographs of northern autumns, not this hot hillside. Green branches stood outside the wall. Inside, a mango tree shed red leaves between ordinary fruit, and an old taun had a crown of bronze where Nara expected glossy green. Small guavas fell unpicked into the carpet. Some had split, but there was no smell of rot. Only hot bark and something sharp, like crushed stems after rain.

A leaf came down in front of Nara's face. Then another. There was no breeze. She could hear each one settle.

VeleTrees can shed at odd times in this country.

NaraYes. But these should not be doing it together. Not like this.

He picked up a red leaflet, turned it over and let it fall. Its underside was the colour of thin paper held against light. Near the wall, saplings had died in narrow fans whose points all seemed to face the same place beneath the old veranda.

Before she could stop it, Nara saw the valley as a map of deliveries: a dozen routes cut short, stock stripped from the outer shelves to keep one refrigerator cold. She had run weeks like that, when every road bent toward the one clinic that could not lose power. Every gold line she had drawn since the meadow bent toward the veranda. But a plant was not a depot. No one had sampled the soil, measured flow or tested the roots. She told Vele exactly that.

NaraIf the lines really converge here, something is concentrating resources. I can't tell you what. The pictures aren't a laboratory.

VeleIs the boy underneath those leaves?

NaraI don't know.

He radioed the searchers to hold the outer perimeter and approached the gate. The metal latch had been polished by repeated friction. A gap between two stones was plugged with a tuft of pale roots, as if the ground had mended a crack.

The leaves to their left shifted.

It was too deliberate to mistake for a breeze. A shallow ridge travelled through the dry carpet towards the veranda, then stopped. Two pieces of loose wood tapped together. Nara heard a sound that might have been a word and knew, at the same instant, how badly she wanted it to be one.

Vele took out his phone.

VeleSay it again.

The tapping came slowly, irregularly. Leaves raised their edges as if little breaths were passing through them, but the air above stayed still. In the spaces between taps they heard three rough syllables, then a pause, then a sentence assembled from a child's small vocabulary and a rhythm that belonged to no child.

The gardenHe is not hungry here.

Neither of them moved. The sergeant's screen showed the recording had continued through the words. He played them once at low volume. The sentence emerged again, quieter and flatter.

VeleIf somebody is making this, come out and show yourself.

The carpet lifted a finger's width, then fell.

The gardenThe hot place is not for him. Here he eats. Here he sleeps.

Nara looked at the unopened windows of the house. The speaker could have been hidden there. It could have been someone using a device. Neither possibility explained the way a ring of leaves drew itself tight around the veranda, but impossible appearances did not make a preferred explanation true.

NaraWe're looking for Samuel Laku. Is he alive?

The reply came after a long scrape of wood.

The gardenAlive. Warm. Not hungry.

Something small shifted beneath the steps. A thin human hand appeared between two roots, caught the edge of a stone, then withdrew.

Vele was moving before Nara could speak. He stopped only because the leaves ahead of him rose into a tight, shivering line.

05 · The Case for Keeping Him

The garden · dusk

The hollow began under the veranda and extended into the tangled roots of the taun. It was not a cave. It was a low chamber held open by living timber and interlocked branches, with spaces through which air moved and a patch of earth dry enough for a child to lie on. Small fruit skins lay near the entrance. The ground below them was damp.

A boy sat where the last light reached. He wore one sock. His knees were drawn up against his shirt, and his hair had been flattened on one side by sleep. He looked at the sergeant without the surprise Nara expected. He looked tired of being looked for.

VeleSamuel? My name is Daniel. I'm a police officer. We've been searching for you.

SamEveryone is loud outside.

The reply was distinctly a boy's: quick, small, slightly cross. The rustling carpet did not move with it.

VeleAre you hurt?

SamMy foot was sore. It got better. I'm thirsty now.

Vele glanced at Nara. She could see him judging the distance to the boy, the thickness of the roof and the room his shoulders would need to enter. He took the compact folding spade from his bag.

NaraDon't dig yet.

VeleHe needs to come out.

NaraThose roots are holding the roof open. Cut the wrong one and the veranda drops into him. The ground is loose under your boots.

He tested the stone with his heel, saw it move, and put the spade back. It cost him something to wait, and he paid it.

The gardenThere was no water for him. He cried when the light went away. The door stayed shut.

VeleWhich door?

A branch knocked three times against the veranda post. The dry leaves stirred around the boy's feet.

The gardenHis door. They did not hear. He was little and called and called.

Nara glanced at Sam. Ruth's records gave reasons to worry, but not the private nights in that sentence. They would have to ask without making him relive more than he could bear.

VeleSam, you don't have to tell me everything now. Did somebody leave you on your own?

He picked at the loose thread of his sock.

SamDad goes to work. Mum said she'd come when the sun went down. Sometimes she did. Sometimes she went somewhere first. I knew how to find the biscuits. Only there weren't any left.

His voice grew even smaller.

SamI don't want them in trouble because I talked.

VeleYou haven't done anything wrong. We'll work out what happened. That is my job, not yours.

A band of roots behind Sam flexed. One crossed over the opening, leaving only space for his head and arms. Nothing touched him. Nothing needed to.

The gardenI came for the shaking. I stopped the shaking. I fed him.

NaraHow did you find him?

The gardenHe lay where the water runs. He listened. His chest was quick. Then he came into the soft place.

Sam closed his eyes.

SamIt was raining under the leaves. Not on me. I heard it in here.

He touched the centre of his forehead as though trying to locate the sound, then let his hand fall.

NaraDid you come because you wanted to?

SamAt first. There was fruit. It made me warm. When I went to sleep I was in the tree too. We could hear the water far away. I thought it was morning, but it was another dark.

VeleDo you know how many nights you've been here?

SamOne. Maybe two. We don't count them like that.

He frowned at the word we, apparently hearing it after he had used it.

SamI don't. I don't count them.

Nara's throat tightened. She did not know whether he was describing a dream, a way of coping with isolation, or something that neither her medicine nor her vocabulary could contain. He had eaten and been sheltered. He was also losing days.

NaraSam, would you like to come with us?

His eyes went to the root across the opening.

SamNot back to the house with the blue gate.

VeleNot tonight. I'm not taking you there tonight.

The gardenNot tonight becomes another night. Another door. Another leaving.

VeleHe cannot stay here.

The gardenHe has stayed. He has slept without fear.

The sergeant bent to look beneath the crossing root. It tightened, making the branches above creak. A handful of leaves spilled from the roof into Sam's hair. He flinched and put his hands over his head.

Nara saw it. The creature did not seem to.

For one dangerous instant she felt gratitude towards the thing that had acted while people had filed notes and driven on. It had believed Sam when he was hungry. It had not asked him to make a neat report of his terror. In that instant she almost wanted the detective to be silent and let the garden keep him until human beings had earned the right to say otherwise.

Then Sam spoke from under the root that could close his only way out.

SamI want to come out. Just a bit. To see.

Nara pulled her notebook from her pocket. Her hand had begun to shake. She folded the paper over the trembling thumb so the pencil would mark a line, and she wrote his words and the time before her gratitude could talk over them.

NaraDaniel, keep everyone outside the roof's fall line. I'll talk from here. You stay where he can see you.

VeleI'm not letting a voice in the leaves conduct this rescue.

NaraNeither am I.

He held her gaze, then moved a step back into the open. Behind him one of the searchers switched on a headlamp. The red leaves on the roof turned the colour of embers.

06 · Plant Time

The garden · night, under two work lamps

Nara made her first offer before she had earned it.

NaraWe can take him somewhere safe. We can feed him, keep him warm and make sure this never happens again.

The leaves rose as one, an entire carpet briefly standing on its edges.

The gardenYou can say that. Saying is not bringing.

NaraI know. We can show you—

The gardenYou brought the box.

She felt the words in her stomach before she understood the sound. The leaves on the path pulled into a thin line pointing down the hill, towards the blue gate. Somewhere along the wall, wood knocked once, twice, then stopped.

The gardenThe box is still there. Paper in the wet. Nothing came for it.

For a moment Nara saw not the garden but the blue gate two months earlier. She had knocked. She had called out once. The address was not on her main route. It was a shelf-stable nutrition parcel Ruth had asked her to hand over if someone was home. Nobody answered. The dispatcher wanted her at the next clinic before the vaccine packs warmed. She had set the carton inside the gate, marked left at address; recipient not confirmed and driven away.

No one had signed for it. She had not called Ruth back. Whether the garden had sensed the parcel or heard about it from Sam, the claim could be checked. Vele radioed a searcher to photograph the gate and anything still lying beneath it, without moving the scene.

The gardenIt sat in the wet. The boy saw the truck. He waited for its driver to come back.

SamYou had the white cooler. The one with the blue strap.

Nara looked at him, and recognition moved through his face before she found a way to answer it.

SamYou left the food by the gate. I couldn't open the door. Later Dad shouted when he found the note.

Vele lowered his lamp until the beam caught Nara's hands. She wanted to correct the boy about what the carton contained, to say it was not medicine or that she had logged the uncertainty. Every explanation that came to her was true in some part. None changed the gate.

NaraYes. I left it. I marked it as unconfirmed and drove on. I could have called Ruth, or stayed long enough to get a handover. I didn't.

VeleYou knew that before we walked in?

NaraI recognised the surname this morning. I thought it wouldn't help us find him. I also didn't want you to know I had been there.

Vele did not offer her a gentler version.

VeleWrite down the date and what you did. Give it to me. We need a record that doesn't improve itself because we're embarrassed.

She nodded. The shaking in her fingers had spread to the pencil. She knelt on the flat stone and wrote slowly. When she passed him the page, he photographed it and gave the notebook back.

The gardenPeople put food outside closed doors. Then they make a mark and go.

It was not a fair description of all human care. It was a fair description of hers that afternoon. A leaf dropped onto the stone by her knee. The sound was a dry tick. A radio crackled from the outer path: the parcel's torn cardboard was still visible inside the blue gate, trapped against a tangle of roots. A searcher had photographed the label and left it in place.

NaraYou're right to reject my promise. I don't have custody powers. Daniel cannot choose his next home alone. Ruth has the health information; I don't. You shouldn't take my word for any of that.

The gardenAnd if you are wrong again?

She wanted to say that she would not be. Instead she swallowed and looked at the child.

NaraThen someone needs to be able to stop us.

The answer did not soften the roots.

Near the gate, the last light disappeared from the road. The sergeant had sent Philip to fetch Ruth and arranged for another searcher to stay on the outer track with a radio. He also asked a constable to bring lights and a covered vehicle as far as the creek crossing. Nobody entered the inner path without being told where the ground had moved.

Ruth arrived after dark, having left her colleague to cover the subcentre. She carried a bag of medical supplies and a bottle with a measured amount of clean water. She saw the boy, the root arch and the fallen leaves. She did not ask Nara for a theory.

RuthSam, it's Ruth. Do you think you can drink a little water? Slowly.

A narrow gap opened above the stone. Ruth slid the bottle into the boy's reach with one hand, then backed away. He drank once, stopped at her instruction and tried again. She watched how he swallowed. It took time. The garden seemed to watch as well.

RuthHe needs an examination. I can't tell you from here how dehydrated he is or what else he needs.

The gardenHe drank from the roots. He slept in the cool. I know his breathing.

RuthYou may. I know what I haven't checked yet.

Her voice was neither frightened nor reverent. Nara was grateful for its exactness.

The sergeant called the district desk on the radio. The first attempt went nowhere. The second reached a duty officer who could pass a message to a Child Protection Officer and could promise nothing about when. Ruth found one bar of mobile signal by the wall and sent the child's name and the few clinical facts she was allowed to share.

Then they waited.

Nara had set her phone face up on the flat stone. Sam watched its light through the gap in the roots.

SamWhat time is it?

NaraNine forty-one.

He said the numbers to himself. A minute later he asked again, and she told him, and he said those too. By the fourth time he was not asking. He was reading the screen through the roots, his lips moving.

The gardenWhat is he doing?

SamCounting.

The gardenWhy?

The boy thought about it with his chin on his knees.

SamSo I know it's still the same night.

At nine fifty-two the radio crackled and went quiet again. Sam said the time aloud anyway.

The garden's voice returned in small phrases: sleep, water, not the blue gate. It showed them what it had learned. Under the work lamp a tendril lifted a fallen piece of fruit and set it near Sam's hand. The boy accepted it, chewed twice and left it on his knee.

Nara stared at the red leaves dropping behind the veranda. Across the wall, a live branch had gone pale since they arrived. She saw what might happen when the last outside trees had no water left to give. That was a deadline, but not a lever she was willing to use.

NaraI think this place is being exhausted to hold him. I may be wrong about how. If I'm right, it still doesn't tell us whether you intend to let him go.

The gardenWhat is left can hold.

A root closed around a stone, pressed until it cracked, then relaxed. It had answered the question. It had not yielded.

The call came a little after eleven. Vele put it on speaker and set the phone on the flat stone, close to the roots. The leaves around it lay still.

Grace, on the phoneSergeant, this is Grace, Child Protection. I'm taking the district's referrals tonight. You can act in the emergency. Get him out and get him checked. The Office is being told. I have a safe place for him after the check, and it is not his parents' house. Nothing past that gets decided tonight, and nobody decides it without checking first.

The line broke. Grace called back from another number and said it again, more slowly, because Vele asked her to. He did not say who else was listening.

Ruth told Grace what she would examine and what she would not decide. Then the sergeant crouched by the phone.

VeleHere is what I can do. I can take him out of danger tonight. I report it within a day. I can't give him a new family. I can't send him anywhere because someone asks me to. The people whose job that is will check before they decide.

He looked at the hollow rather than at Nara.

VeleAnd I'll put my name on it.

The root over Sam's head shifted, but it did not open.

The gardenYou are a voice outside. The boy is warm inside. Why should outside win?

That was where Nara's prepared argument ended. She sat down beside the stone, far enough from the root arch not to shift the ground, and rubbed the soreness out of her hand. The lamplight smeared at its edges. Ruth offered her water too; Nara took it without pretending she didn't need it.

NaraMaybe it shouldn't win. That's not the question I ought to ask you.

She turned to Sam.

NaraYou've heard what people want for you. I haven't heard enough from you. What was good about coming here?

SamNobody yelled when I was eating. I could sleep without listening for steps. When I was cold, the leaves got warm.

Nara waited. After a while he added something as small as the falling leaves.

SamSometimes when I dreamed, we were all the trees. The river took so long to move. I wasn't scared in the dream. But when I tried to wake up, it was still there.

NaraWhat was not good?

He looked past her at the sergeant, at Ruth, at the headlamps below the wall.

SamIt doesn't let me look out when I want. It says the dark means sleep. I want to decide when it's morning.

The gardenOutside is where the fear came from.

SamI know.

That was all he said at first. Nara let the silence stand. Sam could see the lamps and the people. A line of roots rested loosely around his ankle, not hurting him and not allowing him to go farther.

SamAunty Rose has a door with a hook down low. She lets me open it. Can I see her?

Vele knelt where Sam could see his face.

VeleI'll write down her name and ask the Child Protection Officer to check whether you can stay with her. I'm not promising before they do. Ruth is coming with you for your health check. You'll be told what happens next.

The leaves did not move for a long while. Nara could hear Vele breathing through his nose and Ruth shifting a bag away from the wet stone. Somewhere beyond the hedge, a night insect started calling and stopped.

NaraYou were the first to believe him when he was hungry. He says he isn't hungry now, and we can check that. He also says he wants to open a door himself. I don't know what you'll decide. I need you to hear both things.

The gardenHe could return to hunger.

NaraHe could. That's why the Office must check the next place, and why Daniel has put a report on record. The risk doesn't end when he leaves your roots. We can't make it end by locking him inside them, either.

A branch tapped once. The leaves made no words.

The gardenIf the door opens, he may not come back.

Nara thought of the drawing at the health subcentre, the truck with too many wheels to become stuck. She had imagined the boy would like more wheels. Now she wondered whether he had drawn them because he wanted the truck to stop.

NaraHe might not. He might want to come back when it's safe, with somebody he trusts. I can't promise he will, and I won't ask him to promise. A visitor isn't a prisoner you can count on.

She stopped herself. It was becoming too easy to speak on behalf of the child again.

NaraSam, shall I stop talking for you?

SamPlease.

Nara closed her notebook.

Sam did not look at the adults. He turned his head toward the roots beside his shoulder, the way a child turns toward someone lying next to him in the dark.

SamI'm not cross with you.

The leaves did not move.

SamYou can't come to Aunty Rose's. You're too big. But I can come here. In the day. Not to sleep.

Wood knocked against wood, very softly.

The gardenIn the day.

SamAnd I want to see when it's morning. When it is. Not after.

He said nothing else. Neither did the garden.

When he asked to try a few steps, Vele did not seize on it. He went with Ruth to look at the ground: how far the boy could stand, which stones were level, what would make them stop. Sam listened while they set a short clear path, a minute to try it, and a way back if he wanted one. The garden heard all of it.

Some time before the sky began to change, the root over the opening lifted a little and rested. There was room for a child to stand. Not yet enough for him to leave.

ACT III · THE DOOR

The space between staying and leaving was scarcely wider than a footstep.

07 · One Minute

The garden threshold · before dawn

It took twenty minutes to clear a path of six metres.

Vele did most of the work himself, lifting loose stones out of the child's way rather than disturbing the roots. Ruth set a folded blanket on the level ground beyond the wall. Nara stood off to the side with the notebook and her phone, where she could see both the gap under the veranda and the marked stone at the threshold. Philip kept the others back. Nobody had given him a speech; he had been told what to watch and where to shout if the ground moved.

The opening in the root arch was still narrow. Sam could stand under it if he bent his head. He tried once, sat down again, and asked for another moment. Nothing pulled him upright. Nobody counted the hesitation as a failure.

VeleWe can stop here, Sam. If you feel dizzy, sit down. Ruth is just past the stone.

The gardenHe is tired.

RuthI can see that. We won't hurry his body to settle an argument.

A leaf slipped into the hollow. Sam caught it before it landed. He turned the red side towards the dim sky.

SamI told it I can come in the day. Can I? With someone.

Vele did not offer a promise that belonged to the Office.

VeleI'll tell Grace you asked. They'll decide what visit is safe, and you will be part of that conversation.

The garden said nothing.

Then, above the hollow, the roof began to move.

It was slower than anything it had done all night. Leaves slid against leaves. Twigs that had been knitted together drew apart one by one, the way fingers come apart when a hand that has held something too long finally opens. A seam widened over Sam's head. Through it there was sky: grey, then the colour of water in a basin, then paler.

Sam looked up at it for a long time.

SamIt's morning.

Nobody corrected him.

The garden could still have tightened. Nara saw a living cord lift from the stones, arch over the route and hang there, thick enough to stop a grown person. Then it lowered itself along the wall, out of Sam's path. The roots under the veranda held the roof as before.

Nara looked at the timer on her phone but did not start it. Sam was still holding the leaf. He touched the edge of the root nearest his shoulder, almost the way a child might touch the doorframe of a familiar room.

SamMi laik go. I want to go.

Vele nodded once. Nara started the minute.

Sam stepped onto the first stone, then the second. One foot shook. A pale root followed his ankle as far as the third stone and stopped, still close enough to wrap him. He looked back. The leaf remained in his hand.

At the fourth stone the roof creaked. Vele lifted his arms but did not lunge. Ruth kept the blanket open. The cord that could have blocked the path pressed into the soil instead.

On the last stone Sam's weak leg folded.

He did not fall. The pale root that had followed him rose under his heel and braced there. It was not around his ankle but beneath it, a step where there had been no step. It held him upright for the length of a breath, exactly long enough for Vele's hands to close on his shoulders.

Then it let go.

Vele received him with both hands, careful of the boy's ribs and the weak leg. Sam leaned against the sergeant as if he had forgotten what an ordinary human shirt felt like. Ruth closed the blanket around him and began checking his breathing and alertness while they moved back from the roots. The timer read forty-three seconds.

The garden did not call him back.

Along the wall the leaves shivered. A narrow channel opened through the hedge, showing the safe path to the waiting vehicle. Above the veranda, one final crimson leaflet let go, turned slowly and settled on the empty stone where Sam had stood.

No further leaves fell.

08 · What Goes in the Report

The health subcentre · morning

Ruth did not use the word fine when she examined Sam. She checked his temperature, listened to his chest, examined his foot and measured how much he could drink without being sick. He was dehydrated and tired. He needed food in careful portions, observation and further clinical review. He also needed someone to explain each step before touching him.

The boy asked whether the treatment-room door opened from the inside. Ruth showed him the handle and let him try it twice. Then he asked for the paper truck from the wall. She gave it to him.

Vele sent the emergency notification to the Office with the time of the first call. Grace rang to confirm the safe place and the transport. Aunty Rose's name went on a form as someone the Office would contact and check. Sam's parents had been found and were being interviewed separately, at the police post down the road. None of it was simple yet, or finished.

Nara's own statement began with the date she had left the parcel at the blue gate. Vele asked her to include the status she had entered in the delivery record and the call she had not made. She wrote both.

At the back table, on the clinic's one laptop, the sergeant drafted the location where the boy had been found. His first wording was ordinary enough to pass without a question: Child located in shelter at abandoned rural property; recovered without injury from third-party intervention.

Nara read the sentence over his shoulder. The words made the roots, the falling leaves and the boy's account vanish into an absence of detail.

NaraThat isn't all you saw.

VeleIf I write what I heard, it will go through hands I cannot control. Somebody will take a television crew to that child's hiding place. Somebody else will go looking for a specimen.

His concern was legitimate. She remembered her own line on the path above the creek: concealing actions because one believes they are good. At the time she had meant a being more intelligent than herself.

NaraThen separate the records. A protected attachment with the recording, the photographs and who witnessed what. A public account that protects Sam's identity and the location. But don't turn your fear about what people will do into a false account of what happened.

Vele stared at the draft. After a while he deleted the last clause.

VeleWe can write that the environment moved in ways we couldn't explain. We can record what sounded like speech. We cannot write 'alien' as a finding.

NaraNo.

He gave the restricted attachment an evidence number, identified who would receive it and marked the limits of their knowledge. Nara supplied her photographs with their original times. They listed Sam's words separately from the recorded leaf-and-wood sounds. No one signed a theory as a fact.

On the desk between the forms, Nara found a red leaf caught in the cuff of her shirt. It must have travelled out with her unnoticed. She set it beside the photographs. Vele turned it over, checked it for moisture and made a small evidence envelope. Neither of them could say what its colour proved.

Nara wrote the time and her name at the bottom of her statement. Vele sealed the envelope and put her statement beside his own, not beneath it.

Ruth called from the treatment room. Sam wanted to open the door by himself again, and he wanted the sergeant to see.

Vele went. After a moment Nara followed. Sam made them both come inside and waited until the door had swung shut behind them.

He stood in a borrowed shirt with the paper truck under one arm. He pressed the handle down with both hands and pulled. The door swung open. Beyond it was the corridor, the screened window at its end, and the hills in full sun. At that distance the orchard was a dark patch among other dark patches, and the road climbed towards it with no sign of copper leaves.

He looked out for a while. Then he went back to the bed and left the door open.

09 · In the Day

The old garden · daylight, twelve years later

At nineteen, Sam's feet covered the stones he had once crossed on the way out. He stepped over the last one and put his lunch beside the taun.

SamOnly a short visit today.

The leaves beside him stirred.

The gardenSit in the cool.

He sat. Through the open gate he could see the road.

After the first safe placement, he had moved to Aunty Rose's, once the Office had checked her household. She had kept the low hook on her door even when he grew tall enough to reach the other one. When he woke frightened, she came. When he asked her to leave the light on, she left it on.

His parents came for visits. They accepted help with meals, work arrangements and caring for a child without frightening him. Someone from the Office checked the house, spoke with each of them, and sat with Sam alone at Rose's table. There were visits he ended early. Once, his father began raising his voice over a spilled drink. Sam reached for Rose's hand. His father stopped, cleaned the floor and said he had been wrong.

They kept coming after Sam said he wasn't ready to go home.

His mother began arriving when she said she would. If work changed, she called Rose and waited for an answer before changing who would collect him. His father learned to ask what had happened before getting angry. Sam watched them do these things through months of afternoons, meals and longer visits.

Eventually, with the Office's agreement and the checks continuing, he asked to try staying a night. Later he chose to take his school things home. Rose helped him pack, and kept his bed ready. The Office visited after his return too.

His parents' care held through ordinary days: food after school, someone there when he woke ill, arguments that ended without him hiding. By the time he outgrew his school shirts, his mother no longer needed to ask where he kept a spare. She had washed and folded it herself.

Today she had packed more lunch than he needed. His father had asked whether he would be back for supper. Sam had said yes.

He opened the container.

The gardenYour mother made that?

SamYes. Too much again.

A leaf scraped over the stone beside his elbow.

The gardenYou can leave some.

SamI know.

The first visits here had been short, in daylight, with Rose beside him, after the adults responsible for his safety had checked the route and agreed how to leave. Sometimes he had stayed at the gate. Sometimes he had changed his mind before they left Rose's house. Nobody made him explain it to the garden. Their visits ended before dusk. No one asked him to sleep beneath the roots again.

Later he brought school stories, then stories that had nothing to do with school. He had missed whole months. Each time he returned, the path was clear. The garden remembered Rose complaining, on her last visit, that the writing on things kept getting smaller.

The gardenDoes Rose see now?

SamEverything. Especially what I'm trying to hide.

He laughed, and the leaves rattled beside him. They often did that when he laughed.

Before leaving, he asked about a photograph. The garden rustled its assent. He framed his own face and the trunk, leaving the house and road outside the picture. A copy went privately to Nara with a short message: Still friends. Mum packed lunch.

He put away his phone and stood.

SamSee you when I can.

The gardenBring a story.

SamI'll have one.

He walked out into the sun. Behind him the open path ran back to the stone where he had been sitting.
