# Ordinary Light

## Nara — Episode 01

The cold-chain alarm had been blinking red for seven minutes when the first mooring line began to sing.

Nara heard it beneath the diesel engines, police radios, and the rain striking the metal roof of Wharf Three: a rising wire-note pulled too tight.

Everyone else was looking at the ferry.

The **Kumul Dawn** sat against the quay with her vehicle ramp half raised and her bow angled a little too far from the fenders. Floodlights turned the rain white. A line of armed port police waited behind plastic shields. Above them, on the bridge, three crew members had blocked the windows with signal flags and hung a handwritten sheet over the rail.

**NO SAILING. NO MORE DEAD.**

Nara looked at the mooring line.

A young constable stood inside its recoil path, shouting into a radio. A dock porter had crouched beside a pallet to retrieve a dropped scanner. Neither of them saw the fibers beginning to separate.

Nara dropped her manifest.

“Move!” she shouted.

The constable turned toward her instead of away.

The line snapped.

It crossed the quay faster than sight, a black rope becoming a weapon. Nara hit the constable at the waist and drove him behind a steel bollard. The whipping end missed his face and struck the porter’s thigh with a sound like a bat hitting wet cloth.

He went down screaming.

For one second the police line broke shape. Rifles lifted toward the ferry.

Nara was already beside the porter.

“Don’t pull him,” she said to the two officers reaching for his shoulders. “His leg may be broken.”

Blood spread through his work trousers. Nara stripped off her faded blue jacket, folded it hard against the wound, and put the constable’s hands over it.

“Press here. Both hands. Do not look at the ship.”

The constable stared at her.

“Here,” she repeated.

He pressed.

The rifles lowered by degrees as the emergency medic ran across the quay. The ferry struck the rubber fenders with a deep metal groan.

A woman in a dark port-authority coat pushed through the police line. Commander Selai Duma was compact, gray-haired, and moving with the controlled anger of someone whose plan had just lost thirty seconds.

“Who are you?”

“Nara Okafor. Island Reach Medical.” Nara pointed toward the refrigerated container chained to the ferry’s starboard vehicle deck. “That cargo is mine.”

“Then you should have stayed behind the barrier.”

“So should he.” Nara nodded at the constable.

Duma looked at the snapped line, then at the blood soaking Nara’s jacket.

A loudspeaker on the ferry crackled.

“No one boards,” a man said. His voice was tired, not theatrical. “We have shut down propulsion. If police enter the vessel, we disable the remaining pumps.”

Duma raised her radio. “Rako Vele, you have detained the captain and a government surveyor. Release them, lower the ramp, and surrender the engine-room keys.”

“We release them when the maintenance record is outside company control.”

“You have nine minutes before I send a team.”

“You send them, Commander, and this ship becomes part of the harbor.”

The speaker went dead.

Nara picked up her wet manifest. The top page had torn beneath a police boot. At the bottom of the loading list, in her own handwriting, was the shipment that had kept her awake for twenty-two hours:

**ARUA CLINIC — INSULIN / BLOOD PRODUCTS / ANTIBIOTICS — PRIORITY LIFE-SUSTAINING.**

A message lit her phone.

**CHIDI:** Eat the sandwich. This is not advice.

She had forgotten the sandwich existed.

Dr. Amara Kila arrived from the outer gate, breathless beneath a yellow rain shell. She took in the injured porter, the snapped line, and Nara without her jacket.

“Nara.”

“I’m fine.”

“You say that like an administrative code.”

“How long does Arua have?”

Amara’s face changed.

“Five hours of insulin at normal use. Less if the refrigerator fails again. The storm closes the harbor in thirty-eight minutes. If the Dawn does not sail, the next weather window may be two days.”

Commander Duma heard her. “That is why this ends now.”

Nara looked at the ferry. The vehicle deck lights leaned slightly with the ship. A loose plastic bucket rolled toward starboard, stopped against a tire, then rolled again.

“Who is Rako Vele?” she asked.

“Chief engineer,” Duma said. “Twenty-one years at sea. Six formal safety complaints in eleven months. Tonight he locked Captain Maru and the surveyor in the chart room, took control of the engine room, and refused departure.”

“Any passengers?”

“Evacuated before the ramp came up. Six crew are with him. No reported injuries aboard.”

Amara said, “Rako called Island Reach twenty minutes ago. He asked for the person who signed the medical manifest.”

Nara looked at her. “That was you.”

“I approved the shipment. You assembled the load.”

Duma’s radio hissed. “Boarding team ready.”

Nara watched the ferry move against the remaining lines.

“Give me six minutes.”

Duma almost laughed. “For what?”

“To find out why the chief engineer asked for the manifest instead of a lawyer.”

“He is holding two people.”

“Yes. Which means you can stop him. It does not mean you understand him.”

“I do not require understanding to enforce a boundary.”

“No,” Nara said. “But you require it if you want the ferry intact afterward.”

Duma studied her. Rain ran from the commander’s hair to the collar of her coat.

“Four minutes.”

“Six.”

“You are in no position to negotiate.”

“Then call it arithmetic. Your team needs three minutes to board. I need six to make boarding unnecessary.”

Amara closed her eyes briefly, as if filing a complaint with the universe.

Duma raised the radio.

“Rako. The medical coordinator is here. She comes alone, unarmed, for six minutes. You show the captain and surveyor alive at the bridge window first.”

For several seconds only rain answered.

Then two men appeared behind the glass. Captain Maru looked furious. The port surveyor looked frightened but uninjured. Rako stepped beside them, one hand raised and empty.

“Send her,” he said.

Duma pointed at Nara. “Six minutes. If he closes the bridge blinds, if you say the word *red*, or if I lose your signal, we board.”

Nara picked up the damaged manifest.

Amara caught her wrist.

“You do not have to be the person who goes onto every broken ferry.”

Nara looked at the ship, then at the jacket soaked dark beneath the medic’s hands.

“My parents died on one.”

“I know.”

“That is not why I’m going.”

Amara’s grip tightened.

It was not true enough for either of them.

Nara pulled free and walked toward the gangway.

---

The Kumul Dawn smelled of wet steel, old cooking oil, and overheated wiring.

The deck tilted under Nara’s shoes. Not much. Enough that her body corrected before her mind admitted it.

Rako Vele waited at the top of the gangway. He was in his early forties, broad-shouldered but thinned by fatigue, with grease embedded in the lines of his hands. He carried no gun. A heavy wrench hung from his belt.

“You saved Toma,” he said.

“The porter?”

“My sister’s boy.”

Nara glanced back toward the quay. The medic team was lifting Toma onto a stretcher.

“I did not know.”

“That is why it counts.”

He took her radio, inspected it, and handed it back.

“You have five minutes now.”

“Commander Duma said six.”

“You spent one climbing.”

Despite herself, Nara almost smiled.

Rako led her through the vehicle deck. The refrigerated container occupied the forward starboard bay, white sides beaded with rain that had blown through the open ventilation panels. Its small control display flashed red.

Nara touched the screen.

**RETURN AIR: 9.8°C.**

The insulin limit was eight.

“We have to move this cargo now,” she said.

Rako stopped.

“That sentence is why I asked for you.”

“You asked because I know the load.”

“No. I asked because you would arrive already convinced urgency had made the decision.”

He kicked a loose steel nut on the deck. It rolled toward the starboard wall and stayed there.

“The ballast pump on this side failed during the last three crossings,” he said. “The company bypassed the alarm. We found metal in the intake yesterday. Captain Maru received an order to sail before the storm because your cargo is marked humanitarian priority.”

“The surveyor cleared the ship.”

“The surveyor read the certificate he was given. He did not enter the pump room.”

“That is why you locked him upstairs?”

“That is why I stopped the ship.”

“You also confined two people and threatened the remaining pumps.”

Rako’s face hardened. “If I lower the ramp and hand over the keys, the Dawn sails. They have done it before.”

“Then let the captain and surveyor go. Keep the engine disabled. I will ask for a twenty-minute independent inspection. If it passes, a volunteer crew takes the medical load. If it fails, we move the cargo.”

Rako stared at her.

“That is your bridge?”

“It protects the patients and gives you a documented inspection.”

“A ballast system cannot be inspected in twenty minutes.”

“It can identify whether departure is immediately unsafe.”

“It is immediately unsafe.”

“You are asking me to take that on your word while a clinic runs out of insulin.”

“And you are asking me to put twelve sailors on a failed ship because sick people make better witnesses than dead workers.”

The words struck cleanly.

Nara felt anger rise—at him, at the clock, at the red temperature display, at the use of patients as if they were pieces on a board.

Then she saw what her anger was protecting.

She had come aboard to move the medicine.

She had not come aboard to decide whether the ship should leave.

Rako pointed toward the deck above them.

“Your first question was how to make us release the ferry. You did not ask whether the ferry deserved release.”

Nara looked at the nut against the wall.

“You’re right,” she said.

Rako’s expression did not soften.

“I asked the wrong question.”

Her radio crackled.

“Okafor,” Commander Duma said. “Two minutes.”

Nara crouched beside the container and examined the seal plate. The manifest listed total gross weight as 7,460 kilograms. The customs weigh-stamp fixed beside it read 19,880.

She read both again.

“Rako.”

“I know.”

“What is inside besides medicine?”

“You tell me.”

Nara pulled out her phone and opened the load photographs from the cooperative warehouse. Foam cartons. Cold boxes. Pallets of saline. At the rear of the image, half hidden by thermal blankets, was a dark metal frame she had assumed belonged to the refrigeration unit.

It did not.

She enlarged the photograph.

A generator housing. Industrial, not clinical. At least ten tonnes with its mounting frame.

Nara called Amara.

“Tell me exactly what was added after I left the warehouse.”

There was a pause too long to be static.

“Nara—”

“Exactly.”

“A donor supplied a backup generator for Arua. Their power fails twice a week. We could not get a separate cargo slot before the storm.”

“So you placed it in the medical container.”

“It is medical infrastructure.”

“Did you update the weight?”

“The operator said the Dawn had reserve capacity.”

“Did you update the manifest?”

Rain hammered the vehicle deck roof.

“No,” Amara said.

The word changed the room.

Rako watched Nara without triumph.

Nara looked at the overloaded container, high on the starboard side, directly in front of a yellow hatch marked **MANUAL BALLAST ACCESS — KEEP CLEAR**.

The humanitarian shipment had not merely been used to justify an unsafe departure. It was helping make the departure unsafe.

Her own work was inside the problem.

“Commander,” Nara said into the radio. “Do not board. The manifest is false.”

Duma answered immediately. “Whose manifest?”

“Ours.”

Amara said, “Nara—”

“The container is more than twelve tonnes over declared weight and blocks manual ballast access. I need the boarding clock stopped.”

“You have detained officials aboard and an unstable vessel against the quay,” Duma said. “I am not giving control of this port to a man who threatens sabotage.”

“She is right about that,” Nara said, looking at Rako.

Rako’s jaw tightened.

“You are right about the ship,” Nara told him. “You are wrong about the people locked upstairs. Their release is part of any next step.”

“If I release them, Duma boards.”

“If you keep them, she should.”

Rako looked away.

The ferry shuddered.

A low alarm sounded below deck—three slow pulses, then three more.

Rako ran.

Nara followed him down a narrow ladder into the machinery space. Water sprayed from a ruptured cooling line near the hull, silver in the work lights. A crew member fought with a dead control panel.

“Bilge circuit is gone,” he shouted. “The impact pulled the backup cable.”

Rako opened a cabinet, struck the reset, and got nothing.

“How long?” Nara asked.

“Before the lower compartment floods? Twenty minutes. Before she tears the second line and swings into the fuel pier? Less.”

Commander Duma’s voice came through the radio. “I heard alarm. Report.”

Rako reached for Nara’s radio.

She stepped back.

“Tell her.”

“She uses it to board.”

“Then give her a reason not to.”

He looked at the water spreading over the deck plates.

Nara heard the alarms, the rain, the pump clicking uselessly, the cold-chain warning above them, Amara’s breathing over the open call, Duma issuing orders in the distance.

For an instant they stopped arriving as separate sounds.

The crisis became a shape.

A failed pump. A blocked hatch. A heavy generator. A refrigerated container with modular batteries. Three rescue launches visible beyond the police line. Six crew members who knew the ship. Two confined men whose release could stop an assault. Medicine that needed cold, not necessarily this ferry. Evidence that needed witnesses, not hostages.

The shape did not tell Nara what anyone thought. It did not provide a hidden fact. It showed relation—pressure lines, dependencies, doors that existed only because everything had been held together at once.

The sensation was beautiful for less than a second.

Then it hurt.

Every voice pressed against her skin.

Nara gripped the ladder rail until the metal edge bit her palm.

“Rako.”

He looked at her.

“Will you give me six minutes to build a route that gets the medicine off this ship, keeps the Dawn at the quay, and puts your evidence beyond the company’s reach?”

“You already had six.”

“I used them to discover I was wrong.”

Water reached the soles of his boots.

“What do you need?” he asked.

The question was the opening. Not surrender. Not trust. Willingness bounded by a clock.

Nara raised the radio.

“Commander Duma, I need six minutes. No boarding if the captain and surveyor are brought visibly to the open ramp. Prepare both rescue launches and the fisheries patrol boat for medical transfer. I need the dock crane and a portable cable to the bilge pump.”

Duma said, “You are describing a port operation, not a negotiation.”

“Yes.”

“What is the power source?”

Nara looked up toward the container.

“The medical refrigeration bank.”

Amara cut in. “That gives the cold boxes perhaps eight hours after disconnection.”

“Arua is three hours by the patrol route.”

“In this weather?”

“Four.”

Duma said, “And the detained men?”

Nara looked at Rako.

He closed his eyes once.

“Bring them to the ramp,” he told his crewman. “Door open. No restraints.”

Nara relayed it.

Duma was silent.

Then: “Six minutes. My team holds unless a weapon appears or either man signals distress.”

Rako took a breath that seemed to come from somewhere below his ribs.

“What keeps the company from deleting the logs when I open the engine room?”

Nara called Kelechi Nwosu.

He answered on the fourth ring with the voice of a man who had been asleep only in theory.

“If this is another missing customs stamp—”

“Wharf Three. Kumul Dawn. Bring your camera and every storage device you own.”

“I am not your publicity department.”

“Good. I need a witness who dislikes me.”

That woke him.

“What happened?”

“False medical manifest. Failed ballast pump. Crew confinement. Possible company suppression of maintenance records.”

A pause.

“I have three months of complaints from Dawn engineers that the operator called ‘resolved.’ I’m nine minutes away.”

“You have six.”

“Then keep something from exploding.”

He hung up.

Rako looked at her. “A journalist is your protection?”

“No. Copies are.”

---

The next minutes moved too quickly to feel real and too slowly to forgive mistakes.

The ramp lowered until it formed a steep wet bridge between ferry and quay. Captain Maru and surveyor Peter Lau stood at its upper edge with a crew member behind them, hands visible. Maru had a bruise along one cheek.

Commander Duma saw it.

Her boarding team shifted.

Nara climbed halfway up the ramp and stopped where both sides could hear her.

“Captain, were you struck?”

Maru pointed at Rako. “He shoved me into the chart table when I tried to retake the bridge.”

Rako did not deny it.

“You will answer for that,” Nara said.

Rako’s mouth tightened. “After the ship does not sink.”

“After,” she agreed.

She turned toward Duma. “No immunity. No disappearance. Medical checks, counsel, and recorded surrender after the transfer. The captain and Mr. Lau come down now.”

Duma said, “Rako hands over the engine-room keys first.”

“No,” Rako said from above.

The false binary returned at once: keys or force, surrender or storming.

Nara felt the new pressure gather. She could almost reach toward it—almost push the moment into the only shape that kept everyone alive.

The temptation was immediate and intimate. Not a voice. A possibility.

She could make Duma feel Rako’s terror of erased evidence. Make Rako feel the commander’s responsibility for the fuel pier, the officers, the city. Press each truth until refusal became unbearable.

She stopped herself.

No.

She could not manufacture the opening and then call it choice.

Nara looked at Captain Maru.

“Captain, will you carry the engine-room keys down yourself after Rako releases you?”

Maru stared at her.

“They are my keys.”

“Then reclaim them without a boarding.”

Rako said, “He sails the moment he has them.”

Maru looked back at his chief engineer.

“You locked me in my own chart room.”

“You ordered us out in a storm on a ship you knew was sick.”

“I had a clinic waiting for insulin.”

“You had a company waiting for an invoice.”

Maru flinched.

There it was—something factual, not merely emotional.

Nara said, “Captain, did the operator tie your departure bonus to sailing before harbor closure?”

Maru’s silence answered before he did.

“Yes.”

Rako laughed once without humor.

Nara kept her eyes on Maru. “Will you state, on camera, that the Dawn does not sail until an independent inspection clears the ballast system?”

“If the medicine leaves another way.”

“It will.”

“If the ship is stabilized.”

“It will be, if your engineer gives you the route.”

Maru looked at Rako. For the first time, not as a mutineer or subordinate. As the person who knew the machinery beneath both their arguments.

Rako pulled the key ring from his belt.

He did not hand it to Nara.

He held it out to Maru.

“Say it first.”

Captain Maru faced the quay, where phones had begun to rise behind the police line.

“The Kumul Dawn will not depart,” he said, voice rough, “until the ballast and bilge systems pass independent inspection. I received a financial incentive to sail before closure. I should have refused it.”

The sentence was not redemption. It was evidence.

Rako placed the keys in his palm.

Maru and Lau walked down the ramp.

Commander Duma let them pass and did not give the order to board.

The quiet window held.

Kelechi arrived running, shirt half buttoned beneath a raincoat, two cameras hanging from his neck. He took one look at Nara and said, “You look terrible.”

“Document first. Insult later.”

He raised the camera.

Amara climbed onto a loading pallet so the crew, police, and phones could see her.

“My name is Dr. Amara Kila. I approved the Island Reach shipment.” She swallowed. “A generator was added without an updated weight declaration. I signed the release. The manifest is false.”

Nara watched the admission cost her.

Duma said, “Dr. Kila, remain available for investigation.”

“I will.”

No excuse. No appeal to patients. The bridge became more real because someone on Nara’s side accepted consequence.

Rako’s crew opened the container.

Cold vapor spilled across the deck. Behind the cartons of medicine sat the dark generator frame, strapped where a clear access path should have been. The refrigeration bank was disconnected and carried below by two engineers. A dock electrician ran cable from it to the emergency bilge pump.

The pump coughed once.

Twice.

Then caught.

Water began to retreat from the machinery-space floor.

On the vehicle deck, Amara and the port medics packed insulin and blood products into six portable cold boxes. Each box had a green temperature lamp no larger than a coin. The rescue launches came alongside one by one, rising and dropping in the rain.

The crane operator lowered a hook toward the generator.

Rako stood beside Nara, giving hand signals.

“Lift ten centimeters,” he called. “Hold. Shift port.”

The generator frame rose from its mounts.

The ferry answered with a slow roll toward level.

For one breath, everyone watched the waterline straighten.

Then the lifting strap began to tear.

Nara heard the fibers before anyone shouted.

The same rising wire-note as the mooring line.

“Rako!”

She drove into him.

The strap failed.

The hook swung across the deck. It caught Nara along the ribs and threw her into the container wall. The impact emptied the air from her lungs.

Something impossible happened inside the pain.

For a fraction of a second, the steel hook seemed to pass through the edge of her body rather than through bone. Warm gold light moved beneath the torn skin of her side, thin as sunlight under a door.

Then the world returned all at once.

Rako was on his knees. Amara was shouting her name. The crane operator had killed the lift. The generator hung crooked but secured by the second strap.

Nara pressed one hand to her side.

Light shone between her fingers.

Not brightly. Not enough to turn the rain holy. Enough that Amara stopped speaking.

Nara looked at her own hand.

“I don’t know,” she said before anyone could ask.

The words were small and true.

Rako reached for her.

“Do you want help?”

The question mattered.

“Yes.”

He pulled her behind the container while Amara checked the wound. There was blood, but less than the impact should have made. The gold faded as if it had never been there.

“You need an ambulance,” Amara said.

“The boxes—”

“Are moving.”

Nara looked past her.

The crew had not stopped.

Captain Maru was on the quay directing the first cold box into the fisheries boat. Commander Duma had put two police officers on the line, not to board but to steady the transfer. Kelechi photographed the maintenance logs page by page while Rako’s second engineer copied them to three phones and an external drive.

The bridge no longer depended on Nara standing inside it.

That was the part she had never learned to trust.

Rako climbed onto the ramp.

His crew looked at him from the ferry. Duma’s officers waited below.

He could still retreat into the engine room. He could still use the vessel as leverage. The logs were copied, but copies were not guarantees. The company might survive. The charges would not disappear.

Nara could feel the moment narrow around him.

She did not reach.

She waited.

Rako looked toward the three cold boxes already aboard the patrol boat. Then at Captain Maru holding the engine-room keys. Then at Toma’s blood on Nara’s jacket beside the medic van.

“Open the bridge,” he told his crew. “All of it.”

The signal flags came down from the windows.

One by one, the engineers left the ferry with empty hands.

Rako descended last.

Commander Duma met him at the foot of the ramp.

“You are under arrest for unlawful confinement, assault, and interference with port operations,” she said. “Your crew will be questioned individually. Medical care and counsel will be provided. The safety evidence is now under independent seizure.”

Rako held out his wrists.

“Do not call it mutiny before you read the logs.”

Duma placed one cuff around him.

“Do not call confinement safety because your cause was just.”

He met her eyes.

“Fair.”

She closed the second cuff.

No one applauded.

That was right.

---

The first rescue launch cleared the harbor seventeen minutes before closure.

The second followed with blood products and antibiotics. The fisheries patrol boat carried the insulin, two medics, and a portable refrigerator powered from its own engine. Arua Clinic confirmed the route by radio.

The Kumul Dawn remained against the quay, pumps running, generator removed, crew dispersed into interviews and medical checks. Captain Maru gave a formal statement. Amara surrendered her access badge pending investigation. The ferry operator’s maintenance archive was seized before sunrise.

None of it made Rako innocent.

None of it made him wrong about the ship.

Nara sat on the rear step of an ambulance while a medic dressed her side. A silver thermal blanket covered her shoulders. The tropical night had not become cold, but her body had. Her teeth clicked hard enough to hurt.

Every sound arrived at the same distance.

A radio three meters away. A gull above the fuel pier. Amara answering an investigator. Rako’s crew speaking in low voices. The pump inside the Dawn. Someone crying near the medic van. Nara could not sort the sounds into foreground and background. She could not tell which fear belonged to her.

She tried to stand.

The medic put a hand on her shoulder. “No.”

“I need to check the transfer temperatures.”

“They have three logisticians doing that.”

“They might miss—”

“Nara.”

Chidi stood beyond the ambulance doors, soaked through, holding the sandwich she had forgotten.

Amara must have called him.

He looked at the bandage, the thermal blanket, and the blue jacket in a clear evidence bag.

His face did not show panic. That was worse.

“You do not get to make me your emergency contact,” he said, “and then treat me like a person who should only hear afterward.”

“I was working.”

“You were bleeding light.”

Nara looked at Amara.

Amara, ten steps away, did not pretend she had not told him.

“I don’t know what it was,” Nara said.

“Good. Keep not knowing until a doctor looks at you.”

“I need—”

“No.” Chidi unwrapped the sandwich. “You needed to make a route. You did that. Staying until you disappear is the extra part you always add.”

The sentence found the place she protected most aggressively.

Nara wanted to argue. To explain the clinic, the logs, the ferry, the way every loose end felt like a person she had abandoned.

Her hands were shaking too hard to hold the sandwich.

Chidi sat beside her and held it while she took a bite.

The bread was damp at one corner. The egg filling had too much pepper. It was the best thing she had tasted all day.

“Three things,” he said.

She closed her eyes.

“That is my exercise.”

“You left the notebook open on the table.”

Despite herself, she laughed once. It hurt.

“The blanket is scratching my neck,” she said. “The sandwich is too spicy. My left foot is numb.”

“Your foot?”

“Pins and needles. It is coming back.”

“Good.”

He gave her water. She drank.

Across the quay, two officers led Rako toward a transport van. He stopped when he saw Nara.

“Did they leave?” he asked.

Nara looked toward the harbor mouth.

Three small vessels moved across the black water. Each cold box showed a green point beneath its clear lid.

“They left.”

Rako nodded.

Then Duma guided him into the van.

Kelechi lowered his camera and came to the ambulance.

“What is the headline?” Nara asked.

“Not ‘mysterious woman saves ferry,’ if that is what you are afraid of.”

“It is.”

“False manifest. Failed pump. A confined captain. Patients used to justify risk. Everyone gets their own uncomfortable paragraph.”

“Good.”

He glanced at her bandage. “The light?”

“I don’t know.”

“For once, that is the correct quote.”

He walked away before she could object.

The rain weakened near dawn.

Chidi stayed beside Nara while the patrol boats passed the breakwater. The green indicators grew smaller, separated by dark water, then steadied into a line toward the islands.

Nara did not hold them there.

She let them go.
