01 · Eight Days

The spillway at Marren Dam had been built for a river that no longer existed. Forty years of silt had narrowed it, and the forecast for the autumn flood put the crest eleven percent above what the gates could pass. Joss Marrin, the chief operator, laid the numbers on the table of the Wardens' hut and did not dress them. "Eight days. After that the water goes over the top, or it goes where it has always wanted to go, which is down the old floodway and through the lower stands of the Oren Wood."

Nobody in the hut needed the second half explained. The Compact of the Wood was a hundred and forty years old, and its first clause said that the dam stood by the leave of the forest. The Wardens signed for the forest; the valley signed for itself. Leave was asked once a generation. The last question, whether the dam might stay and the floodway be used in emergencies, had been put to the roots eleven years ago, and the forest had not yet answered.

Illuminara had come at dusk with Wren Odira because Tamsin Oke, the senior Warden, had written to her. She had been a court interpreter before she was anything else, and the habit had outlasted everything that came after it. Before she spoke she asked what the other party would have to believe for the sentence to be true.

Dorrit Hale, who ran the ferry and sat on the valley council, had no patience for that. "Cut the crest. Drain Marren to half and be done. We have spent eleven years asking a wood for permission to keep our own water."

"A half-drained Marren is seventy thousand people without taps by February," Joss said.

"And a forest that learns its veto was theatre," said Tamsin, "will not ask again."

Illuminara let them finish. Then she asked Tamsin what the forest did between a question and an answer.

"Waits. It answers in water. We ask it with water, and it takes as long as it takes to be sure."

"Waiting is what you can see from outside. What does it need to meet before it is sure?"

Tamsin thought about it longer than the question deserved. "Everything. It has never accepted a claim it has not been rained on by. A flood has to arrive in its roots before it believes in the flood."

"One flood at a time?"

"One season at a time. The dam's future is two hundred years of seasons."

Wren, who kept the clock for her because someone had to, said quietly, "So it cannot decide in eight days."

"I do not think it is slow at thinking," Illuminara said. "I think it is slow at being shown. The Wood is forty thousand hectares. Tamsin, you told me it has eleven thousand stands that sense separately and agree among themselves. Every question we have ever asked it arrived in one place, in order, a page a year. We have been handing a body that can read in parallel a single page at a time."

02 · The Counting Field

The plan took one night to say and two days to build. Joss's model ran the valley forward two hundred years under the proposed arrangement: the dam stays, and the floodway opens when a crest exceeds the gates. Each simulated year produced, at most, a few events a forest would care about, a crest or a drawdown or a dry spell. Tamsin was sure of that part. "It counts the pulses. The quiet between them it already knows."

Illuminara proposed to stop making the forest meet the years in sequence, and she was exact about what that meant. Two hundred sentinel plots would be staked across the lower slopes, one plot for each simulated year. Into each plot would go only that year's pulses, at the strength the model gave them (the volume, the temperature, the load of silt), in the order the model gave them, and in their proper proportions to one another. What would not be kept was the quiet between pulses. Where the model had a long dry summer, the plot would get a single marked interval of rest, the same for every year, because Tamsin had said the forest already knew the quiet and counted only the pulses. Nothing in a plot lasted a year. Each took a day's irrigation, which meant that the forest, which read the whole slope at once, could meet two hundred years in a week, laid side by side instead of end to end. What each plot's roots would feel was a short, distinct signature of pulses, and the two hundred signatures together made a record that the roots could compare with each other the way they compared every neighbouring stand.

Joss objected first, and well. "My model is honest to about thirty percent on sediment after year ninety. If you show it only the flattering years, the dam will pass."

"Then it must not pass on flattery. Put in the worst years the model can produce, and mark them as the worst." She saw him look at the table. "Including the year the dam fails it."

"That is not a plan for winning."

"It is a plan for being believed."

Wren asked the question Illuminara had been waiting for someone to ask. "You can open a window to it. You have done it for stranger minds. Why walk two hundred plots through mud?"

"Because a window would show the forest what I understand. It has not asked what I understand. It has asked for water." She looked out at the dark line of the Wood. "Anything I could place inside it, it could not check, and a veto it cannot exercise on checked evidence is not a veto. Every plot will be labelled with its year. Anyone can walk the Field with Joss's model in hand and confirm that plot one hundred and fourteen carries the pulses of year one hundred and fourteen, in the model's order and at the model's strength. If a plot lies, the forest will be able to say so."

By the third dawn the Counting Field stood in the rain, two hundred stakes in a grid across the slope, the irrigation lines laid, Joss's crews at the pumps and Tamsin's Wardens at the sap-gauge pillars that stood among the trees like a congregation. The pumps started at noon. Wren wrote the time in her book. Five days remained.

03 · The Field That Stayed Dry

The forest's reply came on the fourth morning, and it was silence.

Tamsin brought the pillar readings up from the trees herself. She laid them on the table without comment, because she did not trust her own voice. Not one stand had reached across its boundary toward a plot. The roots along the Field's edge had sealed, closing in a way the Wardens' ledgers described as something that happened after a poisoning. Eleven thousand stands, and all of them had turned away.

"It said no," Dorrit said, not unkindly.

"No looks different. No has a shape. A no is a pattern of drawing down in the plots it dislikes." Tamsin pressed her knuckles together. "This is a door shut on the whole conversation."

Illuminara did not argue about what it meant. She asked what water they had used. Tamsin said river water, as in all the old watering letters. Joss said no, and the room changed. The model's numbers had come from the reservoir outlet, so the pumps had drawn from the outlet: forty metres down, four degrees, clear as glass, with almost no oxygen in it. Every old letter had been watered from the river above the lake. This was the first time anyone had drawn from the dam's own depths.

"It has met rivers for ten thousand years," Illuminara said. "A river is warm at the top and brown in a flood. It carries mud, and it smells of the hills it came from. We showed it water that is not a river at all." She turned to Tamsin. "There are two questions here and I will not let them run together. Why did it never answer you in eleven years? Because you handed it the future one page at a time, and that I think is still true. Why did it seal against us this morning? Because of the water. The first is an old fault in how we asked. The second is a new fault in what we carried. The forest did not refuse the dam. It refused a lie about what water is."

Joss was already looking at the spillway drawings pinned to the wall. "Clear water. The dam sends the Wood clear water because the mud stays behind the wall."

"And the mud is what is choking your gates." She said it slowly, so he could take it as his own. "I do not yet know what the forest wants from silt. I know that it will not be asked with water it cannot recognise. Make the water a river's, and then we will see what it does with it."

They rebuilt in a day. Joss drew surface water from the upper layers, where the reservoir was warm, and dosed it with silt dredged from the delta at the head of the lake, the same silt that was narrowing his gates, mixed to the load the model gave for each simulated flood. At Joss's insistence one plot was left on clear outlet water as a control. Wardens carried the new water out to the plots in drums when the lines would not reach. Wren stood at the edge of the Field with her book open and said the time aloud at every hour, because nobody else wanted to. The second pumping began at dusk on the fifth day. Three days remained.

04 · What the Forest Wrote

The roots opened on the sixth morning. Tamsin heard it before she read it; the Wardens said the pillars made a sound when a stand woke, a faint tick like cooling metal. By noon the sap gauges were climbing in ranks down the slope.

The forest did not simply accept. It edited. In the plots for the early years, when the floodway opened under the proposed arrangement, the roots drew water in hard and held it. In others they drew it down and let it go. They drew hardest in the plots whose water carried the most silt, and least in the one plot left on clear water, the control Joss had insisted on. Tamsin plotted the pattern with her pencil crooked in her hand, and the shape that came up was a calendar. The forest wanted the water in spring, in the first weeks of the sap rise, and it wanted it to reach the Maren terraces below the dam.

The Maren terraces were Ines Valk's orchards, and those of three hundred and ninety other families.

She came to the hut that evening and read the plot herself. She was seventy, with the hands of a woman who pruned in winter and picked in summer, and she did not raise her voice. "Every spring. You want four hundred families to drown their blossom on the word of a wood."

"I want nothing of the kind," Illuminara said. "I do not decide this. The forest has told us what it needs. Whether the valley can give it, and how, is a question about your land. You know the terraces and I do not."

It would have been easy to say more. She said nothing, and she let Ines look at the plot until Ines's mind, which was a farmer's and therefore practical, began to move. "A flood that reaches is not a flood that reaches everything. The terraces sit at three heights."

"They do," said Joss.

"So the crest only needs to climb one step a year." Ines set her finger on the lowest terrace. "One terrace floods and two rest. Each of us loses one spring in three and gains the silt on ground that has been going thin since my father's time. That I could live with. Four hundred families drowning together I could not."

It was a better design than anything Illuminara had in her head, and she said so. Then she said, "The forest has to read it. We cannot decide for it that this is enough."

They staked sixty plots that night for the revised calendar, rotating, with the dosed warm water. The forest took a day and a half. On the seventh evening the stands woke again, and this time Tamsin did not trust her pencil. She held the paper up to the lantern and read it twice. The rotation was accepted. The roots had also marked every ninth year in the plots for a double pulse, an extra step of water to seed the alder stands on the far bank.

"It is bargaining," Joss said.

"It is answering," Tamsin said. "It has just done in a week what usually takes it a generation."

Dorrit stood with his arms folded at the back of the hut. "I still say a wood ought not to have a vote."

"It had one before there was a valley to complain," Ines said. "We are only late to the reading."

He did not sign. He said he would watch the first release. It was the best that could be hoped for, and Illuminara did not push for more.

05 · The First Release

The crest reached Marren Dam on the eighth night, thirteen percent over what the gates could pass.

Joss's crews opened the spillway, and the third gate jammed. Silt had packed the guide channels, and the gate stuck at a third of its travel. Under the weight of the rising water its steel frame had begun to bend. Joss's engineers gave it twenty minutes before the frame went, and it would take forty to cut the silt out by hand. Wren, standing in the control room with her book, said the numbers aloud so that everyone could hear them.

Illuminara walked out onto the gate apron in the rain. She was not in a window and she was holding no one's mind. The work was physical, and so she kept it exact. She laid her attention along the frame, found the line where the load was failing, and moved it. She took the weight off the bending members and passed it across the apron to the two concrete piers on either side, which had been built for more than they had ever been asked to carry. The frame stopped flexing. She held it there. It was not strength she needed so much as precision, and the knowledge, which she had taken from Joss's drawings that week, of which piers could take it.

"Forty minutes," Wren said into the radio, and then again at thirty, and again at twenty, and in the end she said only the numbers, one after another, in a level voice.

Joss's people cut the silt out of the guides in thirty-four. The third gate went to full travel. The floodway sluice at the foot of the dam opened for the first time in nine years, on the forest's terms, and the brown water that had been the dam's enemy for forty years went down the cut and onto the lowest of the Maren terraces.

Ines had asked to be first. "Mine floods first. I will not ask the neighbours to trust a thing I have not stood in myself." She stood at the edge of her orchard with the lantern at her feet, and the warm, silted water came across her old terrace and went around her boots. Beyond the terrace, along the slope, the sap pillars among the alders began to tick one after another in order, the way they had done in the Field.

The dam held. Marren came down seven percent, no more, and stopped. The valley's taps ran. The gates were clear for the first time in years, and the floodway ran its brown water down the cut for six days.

The amendment to the Compact was signed in the Wardens' hut on the seventh day after the crest. Tamsin signed for the forest, with the Counting Field as the instrument, and the valley signed for itself. Its central clause tied three things together so that none could be kept without the others: the valley would dredge the delta every autumn, the dredged silt would be dosed into warm surface water at the load the Field had tested, and that water, and no other, would carry the spring pulse onto the terraces, the rotation and the ninth-year double pulse included. The Wardens would draw a sample at the sluice at every release and set it beside the Field's readings, and the Field itself would be staked again every five years with the new data. Joss had sworn at the dredging bill until the clause was read back to him, and then he asked only that the survey crews be named in it. A second clause, which Illuminara had insisted on, said that the forest could refuse. If a pulse drifted from the water it had accepted, the Wardens would stake the plots again, and the forest would answer in its own time. Ines lost the next spring's blossom on her east terrace, and so would her children's families in their turn. Dorrit signed under protest, and then, to his own surprise, asked to carry the first sample bottle down to the sluice himself.

Wren found Illuminara on the dam wall at dawn on the morning of the signing. The last of the floodway's brown water was still going down the cut, and the lowest trees were dark with it.

"You did not make it faster," Wren said.

"No." She watched the water reach the trunks. "I stopped making it wait for us."

01 · Osem dni

Prelivni kanal jezu Marren je bil zgrajen za reko, ki je ni bilo več. Štirideset let naplavin ga je zožilo, napoved jesenske poplave pa je kazala, da bo vršni pretok za enajst odstotkov presegel zmogljivost zapornic. Joss Marrin, glavni upravljavec, je številke položil na mizo v koči Varuhov in jih ni olepševal. »Osem dni. Potem bo voda prelila jez. Ali pa bo stekla tja, kamor je že od nekdaj hotela: po stari poplavni strugi in skozi spodnje sestoje gozda Oren.«

V koči nikomur ni bilo treba pojasnjevati drugega dela. Sporazum z Gozdom je bil star sto štirideset let in njegova prva določba je pravila, da jez stoji z dovoljenjem gozda. Varuhi so podpisovali v imenu gozda, dolina v svojem. Za dovoljenje so prosili enkrat na generacijo. Zadnje vprašanje — ali jez sme ostati in ali se poplavna struga lahko uporablja v sili — so koreninam zastavili pred enajstimi leti. Gozd še ni odgovoril.

Illuminara je ob mraku prispela z Wren Odiro, ker ji je pisala Tamsin Oke, glavna Varuhinja. Preden je postala karkoli drugega, je bila sodna tolmačka; ta navada je preživela vse, kar je prišlo pozneje. Preden je spregovorila, se je vprašala, kaj bi morala druga stran verjeti, da bi bil stavek zanjo resničen.

Dorrit Hale, ki je upravljal trajekt in sedel v svetu doline, za takšno previdnost ni imel potrpljenja. »Odrežimo vrh vala. Izpraznimo Marren do polovice in končajmo. Enajst let že prosimo gozd za dovoljenje, da obdržimo lastno vodo.«

»Če Marren izpraznimo do polovice, bo do februarja sedemdeset tisoč ljudi brez vode iz pip,« je rekel Joss.

»In gozd, ki ugotovi, da je bila njegova pravica do veta samo predstava,« je rekla Tamsin, »ne bo več pristal na pogovor.«

Illuminara jih je pustila govoriti do konca. Nato je Tamsin vprašala, kaj gozd počne med vprašanjem in odgovorom.

»Čaka. Odgovarja z vodo. Sprašujemo ga z vodo, on pa si vzame toliko časa, kolikor ga potrebuje, da se prepriča.«

»Čakanje je tisto, kar vidite od zunaj. Kaj mora izkusiti, preden se lahko prepriča?«

Tamsin je premišljevala dlje, kot bi si vprašanje zaslužilo. »Vse. Nikoli ni sprejel trditve, dokler ga ni dosegla kot dež. Poplava mora priti do njegovih korenin, preden ji verjame.«

»Po ena poplava naenkrat?«

»Po ena sezona naenkrat. Prihodnost jezu pomeni dvesto let letnih časov.«

Wren, ki je za Illuminaro merila čas, ker ga je nekdo pač moral, je tiho rekla: »Torej se v osmih dneh ne more odločiti.«

»Ne mislim, da razmišlja počasi,« je rekla Illuminara. »Mislim, da mu dokaze kažemo prepočasi. Gozd obsega štirideset tisoč hektarov. Tamsin, rekla si mi, da ima enajst tisoč sestojev, ki zaznavajo vsak zase in se med seboj usklajujejo. Vsako vprašanje, ki smo mu ga kdaj zastavili, je prispelo na eno mesto, po vrsti, kakor ena stran na leto. Telesu, ki zna brati vzporedno, smo ves čas izročali le po eno stran.«

02 · Števno polje

Za razlago načrta so potrebovali eno noč, za izvedbo dva dni. Jossov model je prikazal prihodnjih dvesto let doline po predlagani ureditvi: jez ostane, poplavna struga pa se odpre, kadar vršni pretok preseže zmogljivost zapornic. Vsako simulirano leto je ustvarilo kvečjemu nekaj dogodkov, ki bi gozdu kaj pomenili: poplavni vrh, znižanje gladine ali sušno obdobje. O tem je bila Tamsin prepričana. »Šteje sunke. Tišino med njimi že pozna.«

Illuminara je predlagala, naj gozdu let ne kažejo več zaporedoma. Pri tem je bila povsem natančna. Po spodnjih pobočjih bi zakoličili dvesto preizkusnih ploskev, po eno za vsako simulirano leto. Vsaka ploskev bi prejela samo sunke tistega leta, z močjo, ki jo je določal model — količino vode, temperaturo in vsebnostjo mulja —, v pravem vrstnem redu in v pravilnih medsebojnih razmerjih. Izpustili bi le dolge premore med sunki. Kjer je model predvidel dolgo sušno poletje, bi ploskev dobila en označen čas mirovanja, enak za vsa leta, saj je Tamsin pojasnila, da gozd tišino že pozna in šteje samo sunke. Na nobeni ploskvi se dogajanje ne bi raztezalo čez celo leto. Vsako bi namakali en dan. Gozd, ki bere vse pobočje hkrati, bi tako v enem tednu spoznal dvesto let — položenih drugo ob drugo, ne drugo za drugim. Korenine posamezne ploskve bi začutile kratko, značilno zaporedje sunkov; vseh dvesto zaporedij skupaj bi tvorilo zapis, ki bi ga korenine lahko primerjale med seboj, kakor primerjajo sosednje sestoje.

Joss je ugovarjal prvi — in upravičeno. »Moj model ima po devetdesetem letu pri naplavinah približno tridesetodstotno negotovost. Če mu pokažemo samo ugodna leta, bo jez dobil dovoljenje.«

»Potem dovoljenja ne sme dobiti zaradi olepševanja. Vključite najhujša leta, ki jih model lahko ustvari, in jih označite kot najhujša.« Opazila je, kako je pogledal proti mizi. »Tudi leto, ko jez popusti.«

»To ni načrt za zmago.«

»To je načrt, kako doseči, da nam bodo verjeli.«

Wren je postavila vprašanje, za katero je Illuminara čakala, da ga kdo izreče. »Lahko mu odpreš okno. To si storila tudi pri še bolj nenavadnih umih. Zakaj bi se torej prebijali skozi blato med dvesto ploskvami?«

»Ker bi mu okno pokazalo tisto, kar razumem jaz. Gozd pa ni prosil za moje razumevanje. Prosil je za vodo.« Pogledala je proti temni črti dreves. »Česarkoli bi mu položila neposredno v zavest, ne bi mogel preveriti. Pravica do veta, ki je ni mogoče uveljaviti na podlagi preverjenih dokazov, pa ni prava pravica do veta. Vsaka ploskev bo označena s svojim letom. Vsakdo bo lahko prehodil Števno polje z Jossovim modelom v roki in preveril, da ploskev sto štirinajst nosi sunke leta sto štirinajst, v vrstnem redu in z močjo, ki ju določa model. Če bo katera ploskev lagala, bo gozd to lahko povedal.«

Ob tretji zori je Števno polje stalo v dežju: dvesto količkov v mreži po pobočju, položene namakalne cevi, Jossove ekipe pri črpalkah in Tamsinini Varuhi pri stebrih za merjenje drevesnega soka. Med drevesi so stali kakor zbor, zbran k obredu. Črpalke so pognali opoldne. Wren je čas zapisala v beležnico. Ostalo je pet dni.

03 · Polje, ki je ostalo suho

Odgovor gozda je prišel četrto jutro. Bil je tišina.

Tamsin je iz gozda sama prinesla odčitke merilnih stebrov. Brez besed jih je razložila po mizi, ker ni zaupala lastnemu glasu. Niti en sestoj ni segel prek svoje meje proti kateri od ploskev. Korenine na robu Polja so se zapečatile, zaprle so se tako, kot so dnevniki Varuhov opisovali odziv po zastrupitvi. Enajst tisoč sestojev, in prav vsi so se obrnili stran.

»Rekel je ne,« je rekel Dorrit, ne brez sočutja.

»Ne je videti drugače. Ne ima obliko: vzorec umikanja vode iz ploskev, ki mu niso všeč.« Tamsin je stisnila členke. »To pa so vrata, zaprta pred celotnim pogovorom.«

Illuminara ni ugovarjala razlagi. Vprašala je, katero vodo so uporabili. Tamsin je rekla, da rečno, kakor v vseh starih vodnih pismih. Joss je odvrnil: »Ne.« In razpoloženje v prostoru se je spremenilo. Podatki za model so prihajali iz iztoka akumulacije, zato so črpalke črpale od tam: štirideset metrov pod gladino, voda s štirimi stopinjami, bistra kot steklo in skoraj brez kisika. Vsa stara pisma so zalivali z rečno vodo nad jezerom. Tokrat so prvič črpali iz globin akumulacije za jezom.

»Reke pozna že deset tisoč let,« je rekla Illuminara. »Rečna voda je na površju topla, med poplavo pa rjava. Nosi blato in diši po hribih, iz katerih priteče. Mi smo mu pokazali vodo, ki sploh ni podobna reki.« Obrnila se je k Tamsin. »Tu sta dve vprašanji in ne bom dovolila, da ju pomešamo. Zakaj vam v enajstih letih ni odgovoril? Ker ste mu prihodnost podajali po eno stran naenkrat; mislim, da to še vedno drži. Zakaj se je danes zaprl pred nami? Zaradi vode. Prva napaka je stara in zadeva način vprašanja. Druga je nova in zadeva to, kar smo mu prinesli. Gozd ni zavrnil jezu. Zavrnil je laž o tem, kaj je voda.«

Joss je že gledal načrte preliva, pripete na steno. »Bistra voda. Jez pošilja gozdu bistro vodo, ker se mulj zadržuje za pregrado.«

»In prav ta mulj maši vaše zapornice.« Izgovorila je počasi, da bi do spoznanja lahko prišel sam. »Ne vem še, kaj gozd potrebuje od mulja. Vem pa, da ga ne moremo spraševati z vodo, ki je ne prepozna. Pripravite vodo, kakršno nosi reka, potem pa bomo videli, kaj bo storil z njo.«

V enem dnevu so vse postavili na novo. Joss je črpal površinsko vodo iz zgornjih, toplejših plasti akumulacije ter ji dodajal mulj, izkopan iz delte na začetku jezera — isti mulj, ki je ožil njegove zapornice —, za vsako simulirano poplavo v količini, ki jo je določal model. Na Jossovo vztrajanje so eno ploskev kot kontrolni poskus pustili pri bistri vodi iz globinskega iztoka. Kjer cevi niso segle, so Varuhi vodo do ploskev nosili v sodih. 01 · Six Hands

The vote was already six to one when Illuminara entered the council ring.

Above the table, a bright crescent of debris moved toward the hull. Forty-three minutes remained before the impact window opened. Below it, seven columns showed pressure, chemistry and power. The damaged bus could run six pump-and-scrubber trains through impact. The station needed seven.

Director Mara Venn kept both hands flat on the table. Human, Naari, Rekk, Sile, Oth, Qel and Vey delegates surrounded her: fronds, pressure hoods, cooling veils, water skins and golden throat fans beneath one failing ceiling.

The Vey wore no masks. Their translucent bodies braided and unbraided around black breathing reeds. Beside them, their physician, Eres, held the single red voting light.

Mara did not soften the argument. To keep the circulation ring from cracking, they had to cut one life-support train. The Vey used the smallest volume and fewest berths. Their chamber could be detached before impact. Rescue might come in nine hours; its reserve would last five.

'If we do nothing,' Mara said, 'the thermal spine fails and all seven environments collapse.'

Qel Speaker Ahn folded two of his six hands against his chest. The golden fans at his throat trembled. 'We did not vote that Vey lives are worth less. We voted that twelve deaths are fewer than eight hundred and seven.'

'Twelve expected deaths,' Eres said. Her translator rendered the Vey voice as a chord with one human sentence inside it. 'Probability is not mercy merely because it has decimals.'

Mara looked at Illuminara. 'Can you hold the station together?'

Illuminara felt the force paths gathering through overstressed braces and imperfect welds. 'I can keep the ring from opening during the worst strikes—for eleven minutes, perhaps twelve. But the field will carry force. It will not remove heat, make oxygen, or teach seven chemistries to share one pipe.'

Rekk Marshal Daro's pressure hood had fogged around the edges. Three hundred of his people waited behind him in a habitat that would become an oven if the thermal spine failed. 'Then the vote stands. We detach in thirty-seven minutes.'

Illuminara saw that the columns treated them as seven sealed claims on diminishing power.

'Show me the air,' she said.

02 · The Wrong Average

With thirty-four minutes left, they entered the Qel chamber. A sweet metallic trace called halene kept the throat fans conducting in unison. Without a dose every nine minutes, the membranes lost coordination; after twenty, a Qel lost consciousness.

Halene was harmless to humans, Naari, Rekk, Sile, and Oth. To the Vey it was lethal.

Chief Engineer Toma Reyes raised the life-support diagram. 'One damaged bus. Seven sealed environments mean seven trains drawing power. Open the ring and we can share pumps, but halene spreads everywhere.'

Illuminara watched a maintenance valve breathe. It opened for three seconds, pushed a measured silver haze into the Qel chamber, then closed. The Qel throat fans brightened together.

'Oth membranes absorb metal-bearing gases,' she said. 'Route the Qel return through their water skins, then dilute it below the Vey threshold.'

Oth Envoy Pell rolled nearer in her water skin. 'We can accept the burden. Our membranes regenerate.'

Eres did not move. 'Test the return.'

Reyes drew three samples. All read below the Vey line. Eres touched a black reed to the third; violet opened along it.

'Bound halene,' she said. 'Your sensor counts only the free molecule.'

Oth salts had bound the gas in a form the sensors ignored. Vey lung chemistry would release it at once.

Illuminara had treated toxicity as room concentration. Eres had treated it as history inside a body.

The Qel valve clicked shut after three seconds. Pell's water skin held for ninety. The violet reed needed three minutes and twelve seconds. Three bodies had answered with clocks, but the display had filed them under different sciences.

'How long before the bound form clears?' Illuminara asked.

'It does not clear by dilution. Our blood dismantles it during the dark phase of repair.' Eres touched three points on the black reed. 'Three minutes and twelve seconds without a second exposure. Interrupt that phase and the damage compounds.'

Ahn opened one pair of hands. 'Three minutes without halene is safe for us. Nine is the limit between doses.'

Twenty-nine minutes remained. Illuminara turned the diagram sideways so its concentration columns became intervals. 'For the rest of you: stop giving me your preferred atmosphere. Give me the shape of what you can survive over time.'

Seven kinds of air acquired one axis.

03 · The Eighth Column

With twenty-eight minutes left, they drew the new table on the deck because the council display had no language for it.

Sova laid a frond across the first chalk line. 'Fourteen minutes dark. Then high-carbon light.' The Sile marked six minutes at human warmth. Pell traced her water skin's ninety seconds. Daro added a shorter low-pressure interval for charged hoods.

Eres set the violet reed at three minutes and twelve seconds. Ahn placed the Qel dose valve at nine minutes. Seven bodies became seven curves against one line of time.

No atmosphere served all seven, but their survival intervals overlapped while the station changed around them.

Reyes projected fourteen inhabited cells, seven pressure-rated service cells, two main pump trains and the thermal spine. Ordinarily the service cells held ducts and drones.

'They are not certified for people,' Reyes said. 'Our model treats atmosphere as a property of a room. It never asks where empty air can wait.'

'Do not put people in them,' Illuminara said. 'Put atmospheres in them.'

She moved the marks. Five chemistries needed isolation at once: Qel halene, Rekk pressure, Sile cold, Oth humidity and Vey-clean return. Human and Naari air could share the trunk during safe intervals. Two trains would move five parcels through service cells instead of seven trains holding seven rooms. A sixth cell could absorb delay; the seventh was excess.

Mara watched the pattern travel around the deck. 'A breathing clock.'

'A clock with bodies inside it,' Eres said.

'So we test it with bodies outside it.'

Drones supplied mass, sensors acted as lungs, and Vey reeds guarded every return. The first sequence failed when Oth exhaust frosted the Sile intake; Illuminara shifted it two cells ahead and used Naari waste heat. The second failed when Rekk pressure stole flow from the Qel dose. Daro connected his reserve tank to the Qel branch.

The third sequence completed the ring. Five cells carried parcels, one waited as a moving spare, and one remained unused. Two shared pump trains left four percent power for structural cooling.

The six delegates who had voted white suspended the detachment order for the live trial. Daro kept a local cutoff: if the route lost its structural-cooling margin, the order returned without another debate.

Illuminara pointed through the diagram to the empty docking spine. 'Clear it. Open every clamp.'

'The spine is not life support,' Reyes said.

'No. It is somewhere a failed load can go without passing through a living cell.'

At twenty-two minutes, an early fragment struck beyond the wall. The unused service cell turned amber and sealed itself. Six remained: five for the atmosphere parcels and one moving spare.

Illuminara studied the narrowed pattern. 'The route can still work. Now it must be able to change while it is running.'

04 · The Right to Stop the Clock

They returned to the council ring with eighteen minutes left.

Illuminara showed the failures first, then the successful sequence and the narrower route around the amber cell. A late valve could poison the Vey; an early release could suffocate the Qel; a stalled door could trap the Oth. Her field would be at the outer ring. She could not stand inside every chamber and make the plan true.

'Who controls the sequence?' Daro asked.

'The central system proposes each move,' Reyes said. 'Engineering authorizes.'

Daro's hood cleared as his body pressure rose. 'Then no.'

'Your delegation voted to detach the Vey chamber,' Mara said.

'The old plan placed danger where we could count it. This one surrenders every species' air to one console. The last host government lowered Rekk pressure to end a strike. I will not put three hundred lives beneath one human hand.'

Ahn's throat fans tightened. 'A distributed veto will miss the storm window.'

'Then find a design that does not require trust to arrive before evidence.'

Illuminara looked from Daro to the six lights that had outvoted the Vey. The mechanism had solved chemistry while preserving the old politics. She let the clock lose four seconds.

'What can the Rekk hold that engineering cannot override?' she asked.

Daro touched his reserve coupling. 'A local latch. Our tank, our hands, no station network.'

Illuminara erased the single authorization line. 'Build seven local latches. A transfer begins only when the groups leaving and receiving both authorize it. A refusal returns the parcel to its safe cell.'

Reyes shook his head. 'A refusal can break the sequence.'

'Only if the sequence is irreversible.' She split the route into seven short loops, each ending safe. The system could suggest; no species could open another's chamber. The sixth service cell followed the active parcel as a moving spare, able to absorb one delay without collapsing the five-cell schedule.

Eres traced three cycles. 'The Vey are always exposed first.'

'Yes,' Illuminara said.

'Then our authorization must come last, after every upstream valve reports closed.'

That amendment cost eleven seconds per cycle. It reduced the thermal margin to less than two percent.

Mara looked at the debris clock. 'Can we afford it?'

Illuminara included the cost no instrument priced: twelve people ordered to trust the six groups that had voted to abandon them. 'We cannot afford the plan without it.'

At thirteen minutes, seven colors appeared around the table, each linked to a separate pair of valves and to a local latch the central system could not open.

Eres raised the Vey chord last. 'Begin.'

05 · What the Hull Remembered

The twelve-minute impact window had begun when the station started to breathe in colors.

Blue opened into green. Gold waited for two black Vey reeds. Oth water crossed a transparent passage; Naari fronds darkened next door; Rekk hoods charged like distant rain.

Illuminara stood where ring met thermal spine, hands on two exposed braces. Reyes's crew had painted the force path: carrier through the brace, load around the ring, destination in four shock sinks.

'First major impact in fifty seconds,' Mara said over the common channel.

Ahn's delegation received halene. Their throat fans flashed gold. As the pumps drew the gas away, the Rekk transfer came due. Daro's console showed one hood bank at ninety-six percent charge. He held his local latch closed.

The system asked again. Daro answered no; the valve stayed shut. Rekk pressure returned safe, halene entered the sixth cell, and four parcels advanced. Ahn's predicted cascade did not happen.

Seven seconds later the hood bank reached full charge. Daro opened the latch himself. 'Now.'

The moving spare emptied back into schedule. Eres waited for six upstream closures, watched the black reeds, and authorized the Vey transfer. The twelve small bodies crossed single-file, each pair of limbs touching the same bright guide rail.

The debris arrived.

The first strike rang through Illuminara's bones. Metal tried to twist apart. She took the force offered by the braces and carried it into the shock sinks. The ring stayed round. A fragment punctured the cleared spare cell; it sealed and went dark. No further delay could be absorbed.

'Sile loop heating faster than model,' Reyes said.

The Sile veil had taken moisture during the Oth crossing. Three minutes remained before Sile needed cold; two before Qel needed halene.

Naari fronds brightened under emergency lamps. Their waste heat had cleared frost in the trial, and their photosynthesis lowered carbon pressure early. Humans could accept Oth humidity; one active cell could be released.

'Naari, forty seconds at full light?'

Sova spread her fronds. 'It will scar, not kill.'

'Humans, accept the Oth return for one interval?' Mara checked her curve. 'Authorized.'

'Sile, your cold can move now, with a rough pressure step.'

The Sile delegate raised one hand. 'Show us the peak.'

Illuminara sent the measurement, not reassurance. The Sile authorized.

The route folded. Oth water entered the human return; the released cell took Sile cold; Qel received halene with four seconds left. Daro's refusal had been obeyed without sacrificing anyone. Consent at each local latch kept the sequence real.

Then a fragment larger than the station's prediction struck the outer ring.

One shock sink went dark. The load slammed back. The ring screamed around her. She found its next honest destination: not the failed sink or inhabited cells, but the empty docking spine with every clamp open.

She turned the load. The docking spine folded outward in a long silent arc and vanished into the debris. The ring held.

'Final Vey closure confirmed,' Eres said.

The last dangerous atmosphere entered its cell. The thermal spine dropped below red. Eight hundred and nineteen lives remained inside their curves.

The impact-window clock reached zero and began counting upward.

06 · A Common Breath

The station lost a docking spine, two service cells, and the fiction that common air meant identical air.

No delegation was lost. Naari fronds bore silver scars; three Sile needed treatment; the Qel slept while their membranes recovered. The Vey chamber was never detached.

Nine hours later, rescuers found an intact station and the delegations beside the engineers, making the emergency route permanent.

They added cells until every dangerous atmosphere had two refuges. Valve keys left engineering for seven consoles at seven heights. No species' ideal became default; every proposal had to show all seven curves, and the narrowest set the margin.

Power stayed rationed and movement scheduled. Some Rekk refused human-run corridors; some Vey waited for Eres's tests. Survival had not erased the six white votes.

But survival gave the argument a future.

On the third day, Illuminara found the council table replaced by a circle of valves. A human child painted seven colors while a Vey held the cup and a Rekk technician lowered one console to Vey height.

Mara stood in the doorway. 'We called it a shared atmosphere when we meant one atmosphere controlled by whoever built the station.'

Illuminara watched halene, cold and water move separately around the ring. The system did not ask seven bodies to become alike. It made room for their differences in time.

'What will you call it now?' she asked.

Mara considered the question, then shook her head. 'They have not voted.'

At the lowest console, Eres touched the final key. Six upstream closures turned green. Only then did the Vey valve open.

The station took its next breath with no master hand on the air.

01 · The Keel Room

The sun was dying at the speed of a long marriage: no single moment anyone could point to, only a growing list of things that were no longer said aloud.

Eld had been a stable yellow star for four billion years and an unstable one for the last two hundred. In that time the Tenders of Kerrin Wake had built the Hearth-Keel around it, a braided ring of twelve field-engines that damped the star's flares so that ten thousand settlements downstream could keep their winters. At Pell, the nearest, seed-wheat went into the ground at the toll of the sun-bell, because the Keel promised eleven days of steady thaw; one flare in germination week would cook a year. The same bell let the schoolchildren out. The Keel did not make the sun healthy. It made the sun predictable, and everyone downstream had arranged their lives around the difference.

Illuminara stood in the control gallery with her feet bare on the cold deck, because she disliked arriving anywhere in a way that looked like arrival. Beside her, Wren Odira held a cup of the Tenders' bitter tea and was visibly deciding not to like it.

“Three hundred of them,” Wren said. “All looking at you.”

“They are looking at the window.”

“They're looking at you. The window is a window.”

It was true. The collective sat in tiers below, the families who had kept the Keel in shifts for six generations. Their speaker was an old woman with ink on her fingers named Ketu Anwar, who had designed the Keel's governor and had spent nine years watching it lose its argument with the star.

“We vote today on shutting the Keel down,” Ketu said. “Not a maintenance cycle. Down.”

Illuminara had read the figures on the way in, and they were honest in both directions. Run on, and the Keel gave four orderly decades and then failed with its reserves spent. Shut down now, and Eld would storm for eleven years before settling cooler and harder, and the settlements would have to move or adapt inside that decade, using the Keel's last reserves for ships instead of flame. Nobody on either side was lying. That was what made the room so quiet.

“We've asked you as a witness,” Ketu said, “so the record says someone watched us do it. And then.” She stopped, and began again, the way people do when a rehearsed sentence no longer fits the mouth. “I would like to know what you think.”

Wren put down her cup.

Illuminara had wanted to be asked this for a very long time. She knew the exact shape of the conversation in which she would say I don't know and the other person would remain. She had not pictured it with three hundred people and a sun.

02 · A Question With No Neutral Answer

“Don't answer her.”

The man in the third tier was broad and tired and wore the grey sleeves of the Pell relay. Dov Tarrant kept the signal station that told eleven towns when Eld would rise. He did not shout. He had the manner of someone who has been right in small rooms and ignored in large ones.

“She is not downstream,” Dov said. “And think what each answer is. Yes, shut it down: a verdict. No: a verdict. I don't know: we hear that shutdown is not clearly right, and that is a verdict. Silence: we hear that she thinks we are wrong and is too polite to say. Whatever she says, three hundred of us will vote on what she said instead of what we think.”

Illuminara tried each branch, quickly, the way she tried the paths of a collapsing boundary. He was right on all four. She also saw what he had not said: his argument assumed the answer would be heard once, at one moment, by people who had already half-decided and did not know it. That was not a property of her opinion. It was a property of the instrument. An instrument that disturbs what it measures is not thrown away. Its disturbance is measured, and for that the disturbance has to be the only thing that changes between two readings.

“You're right,” she said, “about the question as you've built it. May I change the question?”

Dov folded his arms. “You can propose.”

“Four changes. First, you vote before you hear me, sealed, with a number beside it for how sure you are. Second, you get everything I would have learned before I say what I think: the logs, the figures, any option I find. You absorb that, and you vote again, sealed, before my opinion enters the room. That second sheet is the baseline, because it already contains the evidence. Third, I don't give a verdict. I give a dial: what I would change my mind about, and by how much, so anyone can put their own values into it and get their own answer. Fourth, you vote a last time, and Ketu reads the difference between the second sheet and the third aloud. That difference is the weight of my opinion and nothing else. If my words alone carry the motion over two hundred, it does not pass. It goes to review, and Dov chairs the review.”

“Why me?”

“Because you will do it properly, and because you disagree with me.”

Ketu was already writing. “The changed voters should have to say why,” she said. “Not ‘I was persuaded.’ A reason that someone else could check.”

“Yes. Better than mine.”

Dov looked at Illuminara for a long moment. “And I may still vote against whatever comes out.”

“That is what the structure is for,” she said. “If it only works when you agree with me, I have built a throne with extra steps.”

The tokens went into the slot in silence. The first count took twenty minutes. A hundred and seventy-one for shutdown, a hundred and twenty-nine against, most of them unsure. Two hundred were needed.

03 · The Logs

“Before I say anything,” Illuminara said, “I want what I don't know.”

Ketu brought the governor's failure logs, nine years of them, and Illuminara read them in the time it took the old woman to sit down. She did not read them for failure. She read them for shape, and the shape was not the one Ketu had been staring at.

“Every flare,” she said, “braids nine through twelve drop out of the governor's lock. They sulk for ninety seconds and re-lock. The other eight do not notice. Four hundred and six times.”

“That is the fault,” said Ketu. “It is the thing I have spent nine years trying to repair.”

“It is a joint.” Illuminara put the chart on the rail where the tiers could see it. “You coupled the ring as a ring because the flares came as one blow. But the Keel is not failing from the flares. It is failing from its reserves, and the reserves are spent by the whole ring burning together. The vote everyone has been dreading assumes the Keel is a switch. The logs say it is twelve levers.”

Nobody spoke. Then Dov, grudgingly, in the voice of a man doing his own job: “What does Pell lose first?”

“You told me. Your signal tail. Which braid crosses Pell's sky?”

“Eleven.”

“Then eleven is not retired with the others. Retire every braid except eleven, in stages: twelve first, then ten and nine, the rest as each tripwire clears. Each stage is reversible until the last. Eleven stays lit under Pell's keeping as a tributary arm. It costs the fleet's reserve nine percent. It stretches the storm decade to sixteen years. It turns a catastrophe on one date into a schedule you can move inside, and the evacuation, nine days slower than the old plan, stops being a disaster and becomes a number the schedule can carry.”

The young engineer in the second tier was on her feet before anyone could stop her, checking, then checking again, then saying aloud that the arithmetic held and that she was sorry she had not seen it. “Ketu built the machinery,” Illuminara said. “Every part of it that matters is hers. I read a joint off her own fault log and worked out what it allowed. I noticed what nine years had already written down.”

Ketu said nothing, but she took the chart off the rail and held it as if it had changed weight.

“Now,” Illuminara said, “the rule I proposed. You have all of it. The logs, the joint, the plan. Vote again, sealed, before I give you anything of mine. If I speak first, I will never know what I added.”

The second ballot took thirty minutes. On the original question, shutdown fell from a hundred and seventy-one to a hundred and fifty-two; a hundred and forty-eight against. “That is the evidence,” Ketu read, “not her. She has said nothing.” And on the staged plan, which no one had been able to vote on that morning because no one had known it existed, two hundred and twenty-one were already for. It had carried before Illuminara spoke a word about it.

Then she turned to the sun, as the custom required, and said it.

“On the question you first voted on, I would shut it down, about six in ten. The figures are close, and a good argument could move me four points either way. The fear underneath is mine: that in thirty-eight years someone will stand where you stand with no reserve and no choice. That fear is not evidence. Treat it as something to check, not something to follow. On the staged plan I am nine in ten, and here is the lever that would move me. The first stage retires braid twelve. If braid eleven's lock holds under that stage less than nine flares in ten, I fall to four in ten on the plan, and you should believe the falling number, not this one. I am not a Tender and I do not carry Pell. That is your dial. Turn it.”

She turned around. The faces were the same faces. They looked like people who had been handed a heavy thing and a way to carry it, and who were deciding what to do with their hands.

04 · The Delta

The third ballot took twenty minutes. Ketu read the numbers in her flat, careful voice. On the original question: a hundred and sixty-four for shutdown, twelve more than the second sheet, and nothing but Illuminara's words between the two. It did not cross the threshold. No review was triggered. The room breathed out, and Dov, who held the chair for exactly this, did not sit down.

“I'll read the reasons anyway,” he said. Nine of the twelve had written the same thing in different hands: she told us what would change her mind, and it is checkable, and it is checkable by us. Three had written: she said six in ten.

“Those three are not wrong to be moved,” Dov said. “But their reason is her, and her reason is not theirs yet. They will check the lever and vote again, or they will leave the sheet as it stands.” Two checked the lever against the logs and changed their sheets. One did not, and Dov let it stand without comment, which was its own kind of respect.

On the staged plan: two hundred and thirty-nine for, eighteen more than the sheet taken before she spoke. It had not needed Illuminara's authority to carry. It had needed her question, and Ketu's logs, and Dov's relay.

Dov cast his sheet against it and said why. “The tripwire at the third stage is too late. By then Pell has lost its margin.”

“Then write the tripwire you would trust,” Ketu said.

He did, in four lines. Any ten Tenders could halt a stage. Pell's relay would be the first sentinel and its keeper the first to speak. And because he had been listening: if braid eleven's lock failed in more than one flare in ten under the first stage, the plan halted automatically and came back to the floor. Illuminara's lever had become the collective's law. The amendment passed by two hundred and ninety. Dov still voted against the plan. He signed the sentinel's seal anyway.

“I can hold the load on eleven,” Illuminara said quietly to Ketu. “As long as the relay needs.”

“You can't, and you won't,” said Ketu. “It is theirs to carry. If you carry it, the shutdown never cost them anything.”

“It costs the fleet nine percent.”

“Yes. We will pay it.”

She closed her hands and left the arm unheld. It was the right discipline, and she noticed that it did not feel like a loss. The room had not needed her to be sovereign. It had needed her to be useful, and she had been.

05 · The Bell at Pell

Three weeks later braid twelve went dark, then ten, then nine, each stage a slow wave like a field of lamps put out by a patient hand, and Eld began to storm at the edges.

Illuminara stood on the relay platform at Pell at the hour of the sun-bell, in a wind that smelled of hot iron, with Wren eating a sour apple beside her and not offering her any. Down the street behind fogged windows someone was singing badly. Overhead braid eleven drew its thin white line across a star that was darker than it had been and much more alive.

“You didn't win the vote,” Wren said. “You built the room the vote could happen in.”

“Ketu built half of it.”

“And you got to say what you thought, and nobody had to obey it.”

“Dov voted against it.”

“Dov is guarding the tripwire.” Wren chewed. “Those are not different things.”

A child came up the platform steps at a run, small and out of breath and holding a paper boat folded from a school worksheet. She stopped in front of Illuminara with the confidence of someone not yet told what the stranger is. Behind her, Dov Tarrant had come out of the relay house and stood in the doorway with his arms folded.

“Will the bell ring tomorrow?” the girl asked.

Illuminara looked at her, at the man in the doorway who had heard her say nine in ten and had said no to it, at the dark, living sun.

“I don't know,” she said. “I know what to watch. Braid eleven held its lock through the first stage nine flares in nine, so the number I gave stands. If it holds through the next flare, it rings. Mr. Tarrant reads the sentinel at dawn. He's the one to ask, and he may tell you something different from me.”

The girl turned to the doorway.

“It'll ring,” said Dov, not unkindly. “Probably I'll be annoyed that she was right.”

“Okay,” said the girl, and went to put her boat in the gutter, where last night's rain was still running toward the drain.

It was not a conversation in which no world depended on the answer. It never would be again. But a child had asked, and the answer had come as a dial and a name instead of a verdict, and the man who disagreed with her was the one holding it.

Above them the bell began to ring, a little late, as the star allowed.

01 · Dvorana Keela

Sonce je umiralo s počasnostjo dolgega zakona: ni bilo enega samega trenutka, na katerega bi lahko kdo pokazal, le vse daljši seznam stvari, ki jih nihče več ni izrekel na glas.

Eld je bil štiri milijarde let stabilna rumena zvezda, zadnjih dvesto let pa nestabilna. V tem času so Skrbniki Kerrin Wakea okoli njega zgradili Hearth-Keel, prepleten obroč dvanajstih generatorjev polja, ki so blažili zvezdne izbruhe, da je deset tisoč naselbin niže po toku lahko ohranilo svoje zime. Na najbližjem Pellu so semensko pšenico sejali ob zvonjenju sončevega zvona, saj je Keel zagotavljal enajst dni enakomerne odjuge; en sam izbruh v tednu kalitve bi uničil pridelek celega leta. Isti zvon je oznanjal konec pouka. Keel sonca ni ozdravil. Naredil ga je predvidljivega, vsi niže po toku pa so svoja življenja uredili okoli te razlike.

Illuminara je bosa stala v nadzorni galeriji na mrzlem krovu, ker ni marala nikamor priti tako, da bi bilo videti, da prihaja. Ob njej je Wren Odira držala skodelico grenkega čaja Skrbnikov in se očitno odločala, da ji ne bo všeč.

»Tristo jih je,« je rekla Wren. »Vsi gledajo tebe.«

»Gledajo okno.«

»Gledajo tebe. Okno je samo okno.«

Res je bilo. Skupnost je sedela na stopničastih klopeh pod njima: družine, ki so šest rodov v izmenah skrbele za Keel. Njihova govornica je bila stara ženska s črnilom na prstih, Ketu Anwar, ki je zasnovala Keelov regulator in devet let opazovala, kako izgublja svoj spor z zvezdo.

»Danes glasujemo o izklopu Keela,« je rekla Ketu. »Ne o vzdrževalnem ciklu. O izklopu.«

Illuminara je med prihodom prebrala številke in bile so poštene v obe smeri. Če bi Keel deloval naprej, bi jim dal štiri desetletja urejenega življenja, nato pa odpovedal z izčrpanimi rezervami. Če bi ga izklopili zdaj, bi Eld divjal enajst let, preden bi se ustalil kot hladnejša in ostrejša zvezda; naselbine bi se morale v tem desetletju preseliti ali prilagoditi, zadnje Keelove rezerve pa bi porabile za ladje, ne za zadrževanje zvezdnega ognja. Nihče na nobeni strani ni lagal. Zato je bila dvorana tako tiha.

»Prosili smo te za pričo,« je rekla Ketu, »da bo v zapisu ostalo, da nas je nekdo gledal, ko smo to storili. In potem ...« Obstala je in začela znova, kakor ljudje, ko se naučeni stavek ne prilega več ustom. »Rada bi vedela, kaj misliš.«

Wren je odložila skodelico.

Illuminara si je zelo dolgo želela, da bi jo kdo to vprašal. Natančno je poznala obliko pogovora, v katerem bi rekla ne vem, sogovornik pa bi ostal. Ni si ga predstavljala s tristo ljudmi in soncem.

02 · Vprašanje brez nevtralnega odgovora

»Ne odgovori ji.«

Moški v tretji vrsti je bil širokopleč in utrujen, oblečen v sive rokave relejne postaje na Pellu. Dov Tarrant je skrbel za signalno postajo, ki je enajstim mestom sporočala, kdaj bo vzšel Eld. Ni kričal. Obnašal se je kot človek, ki je imel prav v majhnih prostorih, v velikih pa so ga preslišali.

»Ona ne živi niže po toku,« je rekel Dov. »In pomislite, kaj pomeni vsak odgovor. Da, izklopite ga: razsodba. Ne: razsodba. Ne vem: slišimo, da izklop očitno ni pravilen, in tudi to je razsodba. Molk: slišimo, da meni, da se motimo, le preveč vljudna je, da bi to rekla. Karkoli reče, bomo vseh tristo glasovali o njenih besedah namesto o tem, kar mislimo sami.«

Illuminara je hitro preizkusila vsako vejo, kakor je preizkušala poti rušeče se meje. Pri vseh štirih je imel prav. Videla pa je tudi, česa ni izrekel: njegov argument je predpostavljal, da bodo odgovor slišali enkrat, v enem trenutku, ljudje, ki so se že napol odločili, ne da bi to vedeli. To ni bila lastnost njenega mnenja. Bila je lastnost merilnega instrumenta. Instrumenta, ki moti to, kar meri, ne zavržemo. Izmerimo njegovo motnjo, za to pa mora biti motnja edino, kar se med dvema odčitkoma spremeni.

»Prav imaš,« je rekla, »glede vprašanja, kakršnega ste sestavili. Lahko spremenim vprašanje?«

Dov je prekrižal roke. »Lahko predlagaš.«

»Štiri spremembe. Prvič: glasujete, še preden me slišite, tajno, poleg glasu pa zapišete, kako prepričani ste. Drugič: preden povem, kaj mislim, dobite vse, kar bi izvedela jaz — dnevnike, številke in vsako možnost, ki jo najdem. To preučite in znova tajno glasujete, še preden moje mnenje vstopi v dvorano. Ta drugi list je izhodišče, ker že vsebuje dokaze. Tretjič: ne izrečem razsodbe. Dam vam številčnico: povem, kaj bi spremenilo moje mnenje in za koliko, da lahko vsak vanjo vnese lastne vrednote ter dobi svoj odgovor. Četrtič: glasujete še zadnjič, Ketu pa na glas prebere razliko med drugim in tretjim listom. Ta razlika je teža mojega mnenja in nič drugega. Če samo moje besede potisnejo predlog čez prag dvestotih glasov, predlog ne obvelja. Gre v pregled, ki mu predseduje Dov.«

»Zakaj jaz?«

»Ker boš to opravil pravilno in ker se z menoj ne strinjaš.«

Ketu je že pisala. »Tisti, ki spremenijo glas, bi morali povedati zakaj,« je rekla. »Ne ‘prepričala me je’. Razlog, ki ga lahko preveri nekdo drug.«

Ketu je že pisala. »Tisti, ki spremenijo glas, bi morali povedati zakaj,« je rekla. »Ne: ‘prepričala me je’. Razlog, ki ga lahko preveri še kdo drug.«

Dov je dolgo gledal Illuminaro. »In še vedno lahko glasujem proti temu, kar nastane?«

»Zato struktura obstaja,« je rekla. »Če deluje samo, kadar se strinjaš z menoj, sem zgradila prestol z dodatnimi koraki.«

Žetoni so v tišini zdrsnili v režo. Prvo štetje je trajalo dvajset minut. Sto enainsedemdeset glasov za izklop, sto devetindvajset proti, večina negotovih. Potrebnih je bilo dvesto.

03 · Dnevniki

»Preden karkoli povem,« je rekla Illuminara, »hočem tisto, česar ne vem.«

Ketu je prinesla dnevnike odpovedi regulatorja, vseh devet let, in Illuminara jih je prebrala v času, ki ga je stara ženska potrebovala, da je sedla. Ni iskala posamezne odpovedi. Iskala je vzorec — in ta ni bil tisti, v katerega je Ketu strmela devet let.

»Ob vsakem izbruhu prepleti od devet do dvanajst izpadejo iz sinhronizacije regulatorja. Devetdeset sekund se kujajo, nato se znova sinhronizirajo. Drugih osem tega ne opazi. Štiristokrat in šestkrat.«

»To je napaka,« je rekla Ketu. »Stvar, ki jo že devet let poskušam popraviti.«

»To je spoj.« Illuminara je položila graf na ograjo, da so ga lahko videle vse vrste. »Obroč ste povezali v celoto, ker so izbruhi prihajali kot en sam udarec. Toda Keel ne odpoveduje zaradi izbruhov. Odpoveduje zaradi svojih rezerv, te pa se porabljajo, ker gori ves obroč hkrati. Glasovanje, ki se ga vsi bojijo, predpostavlja, da je Keel stikalo. Dnevniki pravijo, da ima dvanajst ročic.«

Nihče ni spregovoril. Nato je Dov nejevoljno, z glasom človeka, ki opravlja svoje delo, vprašal: »Kaj Pell izgubi najprej?«

»Ti si mi povedal. Skrajni domet svojega signala. Kateri preplet prečka Pellovo nebo?«

»Enajst.«

»Potem enajstega ne izklopimo skupaj z drugimi. Postopoma izklopite vse preplete razen enajstega: najprej dvanajstega, nato desetega in devetega, ostalega pa vsakič, ko je izpolnjen naslednji varovalni pogoj. Vsako stopnjo je mogoče razveljaviti vse do zadnje. Enajsti ostane prižgan pod Pellovim nadzorom kot stranska veja. To porabi devet odstotkov ladijske rezerve. Desetletje viharjev podaljša na šestnajst let. Katastrofo z enim samim datumom spremeni v časovni načrt, znotraj katerega se lahko premikate; evakuacija, devet dni počasnejša od starega načrta, pa neha biti nesreča in postane število, ki ga načrt lahko prenese.«

Mlada inženirka v drugi vrsti je vstala, še preden bi jo lahko kdo ustavil. Preverila je, nato še enkrat preverila, potem pa na glas povedala, da račun drži in da ji je žal, ker tega ni videla. »Ketu je zgradila stroje,« je rekla Illuminara. »Vsak njihov pomemben del je njen. Iz njenega dnevnika napak sem razbrala spoj in izračunala, kaj omogoča. Opazila sem, kar je bilo devet let že zapisano.«

Ketu ni rekla ničesar, vendar je vzela graf z ograje in ga držala, kakor da je postal težji.

»Zdaj,« je rekla Illuminara, »uporabimo pravilo, ki sem ga predlagala. Vse imate. Dnevnike, spoj, načrt. Znova glasujte tajno, preden vam dam karkoli svojega. Če spregovorim prva, ne bom nikoli vedela, kaj sem dodala.«

Drugo glasovanje je trajalo trideset minut. Pri prvotnem vprašanju je podpora izklopu padla s sto enainsedemdeset na sto dvainpetdeset glasov; sto oseminštirideset jih je bilo proti. »To so dokazi,« je prebrala Ketu, »ne ona. Ničesar še ni rekla.« Za postopni načrt, o katerem zjutraj nihče ni mogel glasovati, ker nihče ni vedel, da obstaja, pa je bilo že dvesto enaindvajset glasov. Sprejet je bil, še preden je Illuminara o njem izrekla eno samo besedo.

Nato se je po običaju obrnila proti soncu in povedala svoje.

»Pri vprašanju, o katerem ste najprej glasovali, bi izbrala izklop, približno šest od desetih. Številke so si blizu in dober argument bi me lahko premaknil za štiri odstotne točke v katerokoli smer. Pod tem pa je moj strah: da bo čez osemintrideset let nekdo stal tam, kjer stojite vi, brez rezerv in brez izbire. Ta strah ni dokaz. Obravnavajte ga kot nekaj, kar je treba preveriti, ne kot nekaj, čemur je treba slediti. Za postopni načrt sem pri devetih od desetih in to je ročica, ki bi me premaknila. V prvi stopnji izklopite preplet dvanajst. Če bo zaklep prepleta enajst v tej stopnji zdržal manj kot devet od desetih izbruhov, padem na štiri od desetih glede načrta; verjeti morate številki, ki pada, ne tej, ki jo izrekam zdaj. Nisem Skrbnica in ne nosim Pella. To je vaša številčnica. Zavrtite jo.«

Obrnila se je nazaj. Obrazi so bili isti. Videti so bili kot ljudje, ki so dobili v roke težko stvar in način, kako jo nositi, zdaj pa so se odločali, kaj naj storijo z rokami.

04 · Razlika

Tretje glasovanje je trajalo dvajset minut. Ketu je številke prebrala z ravnim, previdnim glasom. Pri prvotnem vprašanju: sto štiriinšestdeset glasov za izklop, dvanajst več kakor na drugem listu, med obema pa nič razen Illuminarinih besed. Prag ni bil presežen. Pregled se ni sprožil. Dvorana je izdihnila, Dov, ki je prav za tak primer predsedoval, pa ni sedel.

»Razloge bom vseeno prebral,« je rekel. Devet od dvanajstih je z različnimi pisavami zapisalo isto: povedala nam je, kaj bi spremenilo njeno mnenje, to je preverljivo in preverimo lahko mi. Trije so zapisali: rekla je šest proti štiri.

»Ni nič narobe, da so si ti trije zaradi nje premislili,« je rekel Dov. »Toda njihov razlog je ona; njen razlog pa še ni postal njihov. Preverili bodo ročico in glasovali znova ali pa bodo list pustili takšen, kakršen je.« Dva sta ročico preverila v dnevnikih in spremenila svoja lista. Eden je ni, Dov pa je njegov glas pustil brez pripombe — tudi to je bila svojevrstna oblika spoštovanja.

Za postopni načrt: dvesto devetintrideset glasov za, osemnajst več kakor na listu, izpolnjenem pred njenimi besedami. Načrt ni potreboval Illuminarine avtoritete, da je bil sprejet. Potreboval je njeno vprašanje, Ketujine dnevnike in Dovov rele.

Dov je oddal glas proti in pojasnil zakaj. »Varovalni prag pri tretji stopnji pride prepozno. Pell bi do takrat že izgubil svojo rezervo.«

»Potem napiši varovalni prag, ki bi mu zaupal,« je rekla Ketu.

Napisal ga je v štirih vrsticah. Katerihkoli deset Skrbnikov je lahko ustavilo stopnjo. Pellov rele bi bil prva nadzorna točka, njegov skrbnik pa prvi, ki bi spregovoril. In ker je Dov poslušal: če bi zaklep prepleta enajst v prvi stopnji odpovedal pri več kot enem od desetih izbruhov, bi se načrt samodejno ustavil in vrnil pred skupnost. Illuminarina ročica je postala zakon skupnosti. Dopolnilo je bilo sprejeto z dvesto devetdesetimi glasovi. Dov je še vedno glasoval proti načrtu. Kljub temu je kot nadzornik podpisal pečat.

»Lahko držim obremenitev na enajstem,« je Illuminara tiho rekla Ketu. »Dokler bo rele potreboval.«

»Ne moreš in ne boš,« je rekla Ketu. »To morajo nositi oni. Če boš nosila ti, jih izklop ne bo nič stal.«

»Ladijsko floto stane devet odstotkov.«

»Da. Plačali bomo.«

Illuminara je sklenila roke in pustila vejo nepodprto. To je bila prava disciplina in opazila je, da je ni občutila kot izgubo. Dvorana ni potrebovala, da ji vlada. Potrebovala je, da je koristna — in bila je.

05 · Zvon na Pellu

Tri tedne pozneje je ugasnil preplet dvanajst, nato deset, nato devet; vsaka stopnja je bila počasen val, kakor polje svetilk, ki jih ugaša potrpežljiva roka, Eld pa je začel vihariti ob robovih.

Illuminara je ob uri sončevega zvona stala na relejni ploščadi na Pellu, v vetru z vonjem po vročem železu, medtem ko je Wren ob njej jedla kislo jabolko in ji ga ni ponudila. Po ulici je za orošenimi okni nekdo slabo pel. Nad njima je preplet enajst risal tanko belo črto čez zvezdo, ki je bila temnejša kakor prej in mnogo bolj živa.

»Nisi zmagala na glasovanju,« je rekla Wren. »Zgradila si prostor, v katerem je bilo sploh mogoče glasovati.«

»Ketu je zgradila polovico.«

»In povedala si lahko, kaj misliš, ne da bi te moral kdo ubogati.«

»Dov je glasoval proti.«

»Dov varuje sprožilni prag.« Wren je žvečila. »To dvoje ni v nasprotju.«

Deklica je pritekla po stopnicah na ploščad, majhna in zasopla, v roki pa je držala papirnat čolniček, zložen iz šolskega delovnega lista. Pred Illuminaro se je ustavila s samozavestjo nekoga, ki mu še niso povedali, kaj ta tujka pravzaprav je. Za njo je Dov Tarrant stopil iz relejne hiše in obstal v vratih s prekrižanimi rokami.

»Bo zvon jutri zazvonil?« je vprašala deklica.

Illuminara je pogledala deklico, nato moškega v vratih, ki jo je slišal reči devet od desetih in je temu rekel ne, ter temno, živo sonce.

»Ne vem,« je rekla. »Vem pa, kaj moramo opazovati. Preplet enajst je v prvi stopnji obdržal zaklep pri devetih od devetih izbruhov, zato številka, ki sem jo dala, še velja. Če zdrži tudi naslednji izbruh, bo zvon zazvonil. Gospod Tarrant ob zori odčita nadzorni kazalnik. Njega moraš vprašati — in mogoče ti bo povedal kaj drugega kot jaz.«

Deklica se je obrnila proti vratom.

»Zazvonil bo,« je rekel Dov, ne neprijazno. »Najbrž bom nejevoljen, ker je imela prav.«

»Prav,« je rekla deklica in odšla položit svoj čolniček v žleb, po katerem je sinočnji dež še vedno tekel proti odtoku.

To ni bil pogovor, pri katerem od odgovora ne bi bil odvisen noben svet. Nikoli več ne bo. Toda vprašal je otrok in odgovor je prišel kot številčnica in ime namesto kot razsodba; v rokah pa ga je imel prav človek, ki se z njo ni strinjal.

Nad njimi je zvon začel zvoniti, nekoliko pozno, kakor je dovolila zvezda.

01 · The red line

Lio Var had tied a piece of red thread around the handle of the manual cut-off.

“So I can find it in the dark,” she said.

She was nineteen. The thread came from the cuff of her uniform, which now unravelled whenever she reached for something. Behind her, through the service glass of Dock Four, the curve of Ilyon filled half the sky. Storms moved across the equator. Far below them, people were opening shops.

Tal Var, her father, brought Illuminara the load charts. He had stopped looking at the planet.

“The northern chain loses another joint at the next northern alignment,” he said. “Possibly sooner. After that we cut the main feeds or the surviving mirrors tear each other out of alignment.”

On one chart the surface went dark. On the other, four habitats did.

Sey’s plan would keep water and crops flowing to two billion people below, at the cost of power interruptions the habitats might not survive. Ruun’s would protect the orbital worlds by dumping the light the habitats could not take onto the southern ice shelf. Once the shelf broke apart, the land ice it held back would slide faster into the sea. Hundreds of millions on the low coasts would have to move over the following decade.

Both plans kept Nacre Three’s pressure spine alive. In its archive decks, microbial lineages breathed in sealed cultures: living instruments for rebuilding broken ecologies. A copy of their names would not replace them.

“And neither keeps everything?” Illuminara asked.

“Neither keeps the present load,” Tal said.

She looked at him.

He put a finger on the chart before she could ask. “Yes. We’ve studied rationing. Enough light still arrives over a full cycle for essential services, with severe cuts. It doesn’t arrive where and when it has to. The damaged link cannot carry the surface peak and the orbital peak together. Between them we waste transfer time slewing whole mirror groups under a charter written for a healthy chain.”

“Then why these two plans?”

“They are the two we can certify with the reserves each keeper will put in the common account.”

Across the bay a damaged coupling cracked.

Lio pulled the cut-off. The test arm stopped; a shard of hot casing kept coming. Illuminara turned its flight into the empty ceramic catch-bed Tal had prepared beneath the rig. The bed split, glowed, and held. Lio stood with one hand still on the handle.

“That,” Tal said, looking at the ruined bed, “was three metres of machine. The chain is eleven thousand kilometres long.”

Illuminara nodded. She could keep a falling fragment from a girl. She could not hold a planet’s failing infrastructure in her hands for the rest of its life.

“I need the people who know what your charts leave out.”

02 · What they brought

Sey and Ruun had shared custody of the mirrors for nine hundred years. For three hundred they had regarded each other as a disaster postponed by paperwork.

Sey brought records of water towers, buried heat stores used by the desalination works, and agricultural districts. Ruun brought the habitats’ pressure reserves, local protection limits and maintenance histories. Engineers had already run the known emergency configurations. Now the keepers sat through the results together, including every blank marked UNVERIFIED.

The charter allowed a new operating pattern on their joint release, provided the local safety limits held. It did not require them to choose one of the two prepared plans. But neither would sign away a reserve the other might use up and decline to replace.

“Her temporary allocations outlive governments,” Ruun said.

“His emergencies outlive the people who pay for them,” Sey answered.

Illuminara asked Tal how long the crews needed.

“Allow six hours. Live checks, staged changeover, first full transfer cycle. They’re preparing the equipment now. At the northern alignment the emergency board can sever trunks. It cannot make a clean new configuration out of a drifting chain.”

Wren Odira laid her mechanical watch beside the three chairs.

“Thirty-six hours,” she said. “Three pulses. You come back.”

“Even if we have nothing?” Sey asked.

“Especially then.” Wren looked at Illuminara. “I am not counting time so you can explain afterward why it was insufficient.”

Illuminara turned to the keepers.

“Inside, you may have a great deal of time. You will encounter each other without becoming each other. No secret passes without being spoken. The field will not calculate a solution, show us a future, or tell us who is right. It gives us time. The thinking is ours. We return to find out what we got wrong.”

Ruun asked whether either could leave.

“At any moment. So can I. If I stop holding the shelter, it ends for all three of us. You should know that before you agree.”

“And if our reserves are not enough?”

“Then we choose among the real losses before the choice is taken from us.”

Sey considered her. “You are not promising a third plan.”

“No. I am promising to look for one without making the first two disappear.”

Before they entered, Illuminara asked Lio to write down what she needed from any plan.

Lio wrote: Enough power to open the doors after the alarm.

Illuminara read it twice.

Then all three said yes.

As the threshold opened, Wren started the watch. Forty-two hours remained until the northern alignment.

03 · The hour nobody owned

At first there was no room, only the difficult presence of two minds Illuminara could neither simplify nor contain. Sey’s attention had the depth of seasons. Ruun’s held distances between places that could die in a single missed delivery.

Nothing about their experience told Illuminara a fact she had not been given. She had to ask.

“Tell me the first thing your plan saves.”

Sey described a girl carrying a cup to a communal tap before school. Ruun described a technician sleeping beside a culture that had once restored the air of an entire habitat. They had both started with somebody waking up.

They returned to the charts they remembered.

Sey called an hour secure if a district could draw its guaranteed maximum throughout it. Ruun called an hour secure if a habitat could survive losing its entire next delivery. For centuries, negotiations had required both guarantees at once.

Illuminara made them describe the same ordinary transfer cycle from beginning to end. Each time one said afterward, she asked, “After what?”

The answer emerged on their fifth attempt. The surface’s large pumps restarted as the habitats refilled their reserves. The healthy lattice had accommodated both. The damaged one could not. The two emergency plans treated that coincidence as a permanent demand.

“You are not fighting over light,” Illuminara said. “You are fighting over one hour that neither of you owns.”

“And you both count a full reserve as unavailable.”

“It is unavailable,” Ruun replied. “It is what keeps a delivery failure from becoming a funeral.”

Sey agreed with him for the first time.

Illuminara let the objection change her question.

“What part must remain untouched? And what part only needs to be full again before the next risk?”

Neither answered quickly.

Sey eventually described heat stored under the equatorial water works. The desalination plants could draw on that heat between deliveries; it would not run the pumps. But for part of a cycle, some pumps could pause while gravity supplied the taps from the water towers. Ruun described reserve above the protected minimum in selected habitats. Some replenishment could wait while the surface pumps ran.

These were not newly discovered machines. They had appeared in the records as local protection, with no commitment that they could be used by anyone else. Some had already failed inspection. Some were indispensable. A fraction might be shared.

“You know this is ordinary engineering,” Sey said.

“The engineering is ordinary,” Illuminara said. “The permission isn’t. Help me understand why ordinary engineering is forbidden to use what you already have.”

Ruun’s answer was immediate. “Because she can borrow our margin tonight and call it her entitlement tomorrow.”

Sey looked away.

In the first year of their reckoning, Illuminara laid out a schedule that appeared to work.

They took it apart before they learned to love it.

04 · Two ways to kill a good plan

Ruun chose a missed handover from a maintenance failure he had witnessed. The next mirror group stayed locked. A habitat spent the shareable margin, then its protected reserve. The elegant schedule would have carried the failure onward while every controller waited for a delivery that never came.

Illuminara had asked them to imagine cooperation. Ruun made her imagine a broken timing signal.

“Good,” she said, and withdrew the schedule.

For the next attempt she let each district decide when it could defer a load. That respected local knowledge. It also let every district decide, at the same moment, that it was safe to recover what had been deferred.

Sey traced the consequence from memory. All the pumps came back together. The transfer link failed under the returning load.

There was no visible catastrophe in the window. No universe played them a warning. There were three people following a stated assumption until it led somewhere they could not accept.

“We have moved the peak,” Sey said. “We have not removed it.”

Illuminara began again.

The pressure spine, ventilation, hospitals and emergency doors would retain continuous supply. Their protected reserves would not enter the bargain. Other loads would receive staggered windows, using only measured storage above those floors. Recovery would be staggered too.

The surviving mirror groups would move within their existing mechanical limits, on offset schedules rather than as one great block. A failed signal would suspend the next transfer and shed agreed nonessential loads; it would not tell every group to improvise at once. A local engineer could refuse an unsafe move. No keeper could overrule that refusal during the changeover.

Sey stopped Illuminara at that last condition.

“A gate can say no. But what happens to the other side when it does? You have protected the engineer and left the cost unnamed.”

Illuminara had no answer ready. They worked until the refusal had a consequence as explicit as the permission: a named reserve, a bounded delay, a load to shed, a return route. Where no safe route could be established from the records, that part of the network was excluded.

The resulting plan saved less than their first attempt had claimed. It was also the first in which every local refusal they had examined had an explicit safe return route.

It could still fail against a measurement they did not possess.

“The room cannot inspect a bearing,” Illuminara reminded them.

They kept an empty place in every calculation for the people who would.

05 · A century’s work

Outside, Tal and his crews laid out cables, isolation tools and three configurations: Sey’s, Ruun’s, and a provisional sequence for any joint plan that passed the live checks. They disconnected decorative lighting and tested the doors on reserve power. Tal also unlocked the counter-mass in Dock Four’s northern anchor and checked its dampers. If the failing joint above it began to twist, there would be somewhere to put the force.

Lio walked the darkened corridors with a slate. Twice she found equipment that drew more than its label said. She reported both loads. On the second circuit she made her father eat.

Wren wound her watch.

Inside, years accumulated without adding an hour to the crews’ preparations.

The engineering insight had come early. The obstacle remaining was older than the damaged chain.

The schedule had taken a year. The signatures took the rest.

Sey had once given the habitats a temporary water allotment during an orbital epidemic. The allotment had become permanent. Ruun had once allowed a surface council to postpone maintenance. When the council dissolved, the debt remained in orbit. Their reserves contained a history of promises nobody had been able to collect.

Illuminara stopped asking them to trust each other.

“Don’t build it on forgiveness,” she said. “Build it so that taking too much hurts the taker first.”

She proposed that every loan carry its own collateral: the borrower’s postponable loads, cut automatically if the borrowed margin was not restored on time. Ruun spent eleven years trying to cheat the arrangement. Sey spent longer and came closer. Each attempt closed a gap.

Ruun could not make safety depend on Sey forgiving him. Sey could not make a pump schedule depend on Ruun believing she had changed.

Each loan had an expiry and each district a limit. Neither keeper could renew it alone. The stores would be measured locally, with the figures visible to both populations. If either side took more than its share, that side’s postponable loads would be cut first. Essential supply was excluded from punishment. The emergency mode could not become a permanent settlement by being quietly left running; continuation required a new public decision and a new engineering certificate.

There were still costs. The surface would lose a planting cycle in districts where water could not be delivered at the old rate. Habitat expansion would stop. Private comfort reserves would enter the common account. The most damaged mirror section would be retired. Repairs would demand labour and money from people who had not broken the chain.

“They will hate us for some of this,” Sey said.

“Yes,” said Illuminara. “They should be allowed to know what they are paying for.”

They worked through remembered faults, then combinations of faults. They argued over villages they knew and villages they had never visited. When recollection became uncertain, they marked the point for verification rather than resolving it by confidence. Illuminara reduced the growing proposal to a sequence an exhausted crew could execute and a set of limits a crew could refuse.

As the decades passed, the two keepers also acquired reasons to speak that had nothing to do with power.

Ruun learned why Sey kept returning to the cup at the tap. It had belonged to her son, who had died long before the mirror charter. Sey learned that Ruun had lost the sound of his original language. When he remembered home, he now remembered it in translation.

Illuminara did not use either admission to win the next argument.

Once they spent an entire remembered evening trying to reconstruct a song. All three were bad at it. Ruun was astonished by how badly Illuminara sang. After that, he asked for her opinion sometimes without asking her to arbitrate.

She came to care for both of them in ways the council had not commissioned.

The century was not a price she could charge the outside world. It was something they had lived, and something they would have to carry back.

By the calendars Sey and Ruun kept in thought, they crossed their hundredth year together shortly before the signal.

Three pulses touched Illuminara’s wrist.

Sey fell silent in the middle of a sentence about the song.

For a moment, Illuminara wanted to hear its end. A girl outside had given her a sentence she could not misremember.

“Enough power to open the doors,” she said.

Ruun lifted his head.

“We have a plan,” he said. “Not a certificate.”

“Then we have work outside.”

Illuminara closed the window.

06 · Thirty-six hours, four minutes

Wren was standing where she had been. Cable grease marked her sleeve; she had been helping Tal’s crews. The watch read thirty-six hours and four minutes since entry.

Illuminara looked at her with the unbearable relief of someone seeing a friend after a lifetime. Wren had last spoken to her yesterday morning.

“Tell Tal,” Wren said.

Illuminara took the offered slate.

She wrote the steps. Sey supplied the load limits. Ruun supplied the return routes. What had occupied a century became a short operating sheet and a longer list of things that still needed to be checked.

Tal read the first page standing up. At the second he sat down.

“You need the lower heat stores.”

“Only if the measurements pass,” Sey said.

“Last winter’s leakage figures are wrong.”

“Then use today’s.”

The engineering team ran the plan on ordinary machines with live telemetry. One district dropped out. Then another. Their removal cut the margin but left the essential supply intact. A delay the keepers had remembered as tolerable was not tolerable with the present bearing temperatures; Ruun amended its limit.

Illuminara watched the remaining capacity shrink.

She asked Tal for the loads held outside the emergency rationing list.

“Private contracts,” he said. “A keeper’s release is required.”

She put the list between Sey and Ruun. Neither could demand a sacrifice from the other’s constituents without exposing their own. Sey crossed out the first exemption. Ruun crossed out the second.

There was enough margin left to attempt the pilot. Enough to return safely if it failed. Neither was generous.

Tal looked up. “Who commands when a local station refuses?”

“Its engineer,” Illuminara said. “Show us the failed limit. We amend or return. We do not argue the meter into obedience.”

He turned the slate toward Lio.

“Dock Four is the pilot.”

She read the procedure, then the fallback.

“I want the return command on my handle.”

Ruun gave it to her.

Both keepers signed the bounded pilot and its return route. Extending it to the network would require the engineers’ certificate and a second joint release.

07 · The hand on the handle

The first mirror group began its smaller turn. Below, selected pumps paused. The water towers fed the taps. Nacre Three’s pressure spine stayed on its uninterrupted line.

At Dock Four, the reserve indicator began to fall.

It should have steadied above the red mark. It did not.

“Stop,” Lio said.

Ruun drew breath. Illuminara held up one hand to him, not to Lio.

Lio pulled the handle. The pilot returned to its previous configuration. The next mirror group stayed locked in its safe position. Across the board, the essential lines remained green.

Nine minutes had gone with the failed pilot.

Through the service glass, the warning lamp above the northern anchor began to pulse. Tal brought up the trace. The northern joint from his first chart was twisting early, under the load the return to the old configuration had thrown back onto it.

“Possibly sooner,” he said, very quietly.

Illuminara put her palm against the glass. She took the twisting load off that single joint and fed it into the counter-mass Tal had unlocked. It began to move along its track. The anchor groaned through the deck; the dampers warmed on Tal’s display. The warning lamp steadied.

“How long?” Ruun asked.

Illuminara watched the counter-mass travel. “Until it reaches the end of the track. Find the load.”

“The schedule is right,” Ruun said, looking at the trace.

“The reserve fell,” Illuminara answered. “Find what our schedule did not know.”

Lio brought them the readings from her corridor walk. The draw they had included under emergency doors covered the motors but not the heaters that kept the seals flexible. The heaters switched to the reserve circuit during isolation; the old load table assigned them to the normal supply. They had been added after a cold-lock failure six years earlier.

Tal swore. He had helped install them.

“Keep them,” Illuminara said. “What can wait?”

Lio pointed to the docking ring’s cargo thawers. Their contract required uninterrupted operation. The cargo was unopened stone-grown timber. It could remain cold.

That cut alone would recover most of the missing margin, not all. Illuminara shortened Dock Four’s interval on reserve; Sey deferred the corresponding surface load for a little longer, within a verified water-tower interval. Ruun checked the revised mirror motion against the bearing limit. Tal ran the amendment through the live model.

It passed.

Lio tested the door nearest the pilot station. It opened. She let it close and tried again on reserve power.

“Ready,” she said.

The second turn began.

The indicator descended, slowed, and held clear of the mark.

As the mirrors settled into their new positions, the load came off the northern joint. Illuminara eased her hold. The joint kept its alignment. The counter-mass stopped short of the end of its track.

The timer beside Tal’s trace read eleven minutes.

It had not been the rest of the planet’s life. It had been eleven minutes.

Thirty-two maintenance workers were waiting behind the service barrier for the pilot to release their corridor. One laughed when the next delivery arrived and the door opened. Lio kept her hand on the handle until the reserve had recovered.

Sey and Ruun signed the certified sequence. Tal sent it to the other stations.

At forty-one hours and forty-eight minutes, the last participating group reported stable alignment. The damaged section had been isolated. The first complete transfer cycle served the surface and all four habitats without breaching a protected reserve.

No coast had been ordered evacuated. No archive had lost its air.

The council’s display still contained a great many amber lights. There were repairs, crop losses, rationing orders and rejected exemptions waiting beneath them.

The line for Nacre Three was green.

Sey put her name to the operating log. Ruun put his beside it.

08 · What came out

Nine days later, the emergency pattern was still working.

In one surface district, a child waited longer at the tap and went home with a full cup. In orbit, the cultures on Nacre Three divided in their old sealed vessels. Crews were replacing the first failed bearings. Representatives from the coast and the habitats were arguing over the repair levy in a room with no window except the ordinary kind.

Illuminara visited Dock Four before leaving.

Lio’s cuff had been repaired. She had kept the red thread on the handle.

“They’ve put you on the relief roster,” Tal said.

Lio looked at him. “I’ve been on it for six months.”

“The proper roster.”

She smiled despite herself.

Sey and Ruun arrived together, carrying different objections to the same levy. Ruun paused beside Illuminara.

“I still think the shelf should bear more of the reconstruction cost.”

“And I still think he should learn to live near water,” Sey said.

Illuminara found that she missed them while they were standing in front of her. Without the depth of the window, she had to learn their ordinary silences again.

“Was it really a hundred years?” Wren asked later, on the way to the shuttle.
