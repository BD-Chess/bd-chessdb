Ordinary Light

The first thing Nara saves is one yellow boot. Before power, before Illuminara, she discovers the kind of attention that leaves other people more themselves.

CHAPTER 1 — THE YELLOW BOOT

The first thing Nara saved was not a city.

It was one yellow rain boot.

It lay on its side beneath the departures board at Harbour Link Terminal, bright as a small warning in the dark. Around it, three hundred people were forgetting that they were three hundred separate people.

The storm had been leaning on Port Moresby since late afternoon. Rain struck the roof in hard tropical sheets. Wind pushed salt through every gap in the old terminal walls. The public-address system had just begun to repeat a ferry delay when the lights went out between two words.

For half a second there was only rain.

Then phones appeared, white and blue and badly aimed. Someone shouted, “Move!” A suitcase went down. A child screamed. The crowd obeyed the worst part of the word.

Nara had been standing near the route map with a paper bag of kaukau, tomatoes, instant coffee and six eggs going soft in the humidity. She had finished a twelve-hour shift untangling a refrigerated medicine shipment that customs had classified under the wrong code. Her neck hurt. She had not eaten since morning. Her mother had sent two messages and deleted a third before Nara could read it.

She saw the boot.

Small. Yellow. Rubber. One strap torn halfway through. Still holding the shape of a child’s foot.

That was the detail the crowd had lost.

Not the emergency arrows painted on the concrete pillars. Not the exit map. Not the guard reaching for the siren on his bullhorn.

The child.

Nara placed the grocery bag against the wall. Something fragile cracked inside it.

She moved sideways through the pressure, one palm raised where frightened people could see it. A man nearly struck her with his elbow.

“Watch yourself,” he snapped.

“I am,” she said, crouching for the boot. “You too.”

He looked down at the yellow rubber in her hand. His anger changed shape before he could defend it.

Nara climbed onto the low metal base of a closed ticket kiosk. She turned on her phone light and held it beneath the boot so it glowed.

She did not shout. Shouting already belonged to the panic.

“Whose child is missing this?” she asked.

The question entered the room differently from an order.

A man beside a stroller looked down as if he had only just remembered what he was holding. His hands were locked around the bar so tightly that his knuckles had gone grey. In the stroller, a girl of three or four stared upward with her mouth open and no sound coming out. One foot wore a red sock. The other wore its yellow boot.

“Mina,” the man said.

Nara looked at him, not over him.

“Can you hear her breathing?”

“What?”

“Your daughter. Can you hear her?”

He bent his head. The stroller shook with him.

“Yes.”

“Match her. Two counts in. Two out. Keep one hand where she can feel you.”

The man released the handle with one hand and placed it on Mina’s shoulder.

The change was almost nothing.

The stroller stopped shaking.

Nara turned toward a woman trapped beside a fallen suitcase.

“Will you be first to make space?”

“I can’t,” the woman said. “They’re pushing.”

“Half a step. Not for me. For the boot.”

The woman stared at the yellow shape in Nara’s hand. Then she moved one foot back.

A person behind her adjusted without being asked. Then another. A pocket of air appeared around the stroller as if the crowd had remembered that bodies were not floodwater.

Near the gates, the senior security guard lifted his bullhorn. His thumb found the siren switch. His face had the empty hardness of someone about to take control by making everyone more afraid.

Nara stepped down and walked toward him.

“Let him do his job,” someone hissed.

Nara stopped one arm’s length away. The boot remained visible in her left hand.

“Will you give me ten seconds before you use that?” she asked.

“Back away.”

“Ten seconds. If it fails, I step back and you do what you believe protects them.”

“You don’t know procedure.”

“No. You do.” She nodded toward the crowd. “Keep the aim. Change the method.”

He stared at her. Rain hammered the roof. The siren button waited beneath his thumb.

Not agreement. Not trust. Only a fraction of hesitation.

Enough was where everything began.

“Ten,” he said.

“Name one thing you can feel.”

His eyes narrowed. “The bullhorn.”

“One thing you can hear.”

“The child.”

“One thing you can see.”

“Your hand.”

“Then use yours,” Nara said. “Point. Don’t blast. Give them one direction and one sentence.”

The guard looked beyond her. The crowd was not calm. It was still frightened, still compressed, still one sharp sound away from teeth and elbows.

But now there was a pocket.

A father breathing with his daughter.

A woman who had made half a step and discovered that half a step could spread.

The guard lowered the bullhorn. He raised his free hand and pointed toward the service corridor behind a shuttered food stall.

“Phone lights on the floor!” he called, his voice cracking but clear. “Parents and injured first. Move in pairs. Nobody runs.”

The sentence became architecture.

Nara turned before relief could become applause. Applause would make her the center, and the center needed to be the exit.

She pointed to a teenager filming with his phone. “Can your light mark the first step?”

He lowered the camera. “Yes.”

“To the floor, then.”

She found a woman in a green blouse. “Can you count twenty people through and then trade places?”

The woman swallowed. “Twenty.”

“Not twenty forever. Twenty, then someone else.”

That mattered. People could agree to small burdens that did not pretend to be infinite.

A maintenance worker stood frozen beside a grey door, both hands wrapped around a ring of keys. His badge read TOMAS.

“That door?” Nara asked.

“Staff only.”

“Where does it lead?”

“Loading lane behind the terminal.”

“Can it open from here?”

“I’m not authorized.”

The crowd surged. Glass broke somewhere near the ticket windows.

Tomas flinched.

Nara did not take his keys. She did not tell him courage was easy.

“You’re not afraid of the door,” she said. “You’re afraid that if you choose wrong, the blame will finally have a name.”

His mouth opened.

“I’m not asking you to ignore that. I’m asking whether we can make the choice visible. The guard witnesses it. She records names.” Nara pointed to the woman counting. “I will say I asked. You will not stand alone in it. Will you open the door?”

Tomas looked at the guard.

The guard looked at Mina. At the boot. At the crowd.

“Open it,” he said.

Tomas found the key on his third attempt.

When the door swung outward, the smell of wet concrete, diesel and sea entered the terminal like mercy wearing work clothes.

The first twenty people moved through slowly. Then another twenty. Phone lights lay along the floor like a runway. Someone counted in English, someone else in Tok Pisin, and soon the two rhythms overlapped. The green-bloused woman traded places with the teenager. A nurse took the next count. A fisherman lifted the broken stroller over the threshold.

Mina reached for the boot.

Nara crouched and slid it over the red sock.

“You kept it,” Mina whispered.

“Everyone did,” Nara said.

It was important to say it exactly.

The evacuation was not perfect. Nothing human was. Two men shoved each other and then apologized badly. An elderly woman refused to leave until someone found her husband, who was waiting at another exit and furious that no one had told him where to be furious. A student cried over a backpack left near the gates. The guard barked once, caught himself, and tried again with fewer words.

But no one was crushed.

The siren never sounded.

The station emptied by degrees until the emergency strips finally came alive along the floor, red and late and trying.

Outside, people stood in the rain blinking at one another as if they had returned from a country whose language they almost understood.

The guard came out last with Tomas.

“What are you?” he asked Nara.

She almost said hungry.

Instead she held up the now-empty hand that had carried the boot.

“Someone who noticed.”

He frowned. He wanted a category—expert, fraud, hero, threat, liability. Something he could enter in a report.

Nara had no category for him.

Not yet.

The cost arrived before the ambulances.

It began in her right wrist: a fine tremor, as if a wire had been pulled too tight beneath the skin. Then the rain became too many separate impacts. Every drop struck with its own bright edge. The terminal sign hummed blue. A stranger’s grief brushed her ribs with such heat that she bent around it.

She tried to say, “I need a minute.”

The word minute was gone.

Not forgotten. Misfiled.

Nara found the wall instead. She leaned against the wet concrete and closed her eyes.

Three things.

The wall is cold.

The rain smells like metal.

My hand is shaking, but it is my hand.

The last sentence helped because it belonged to her.

After a while the guard placed a bottle of water on the ground between them. He did not come closer without asking.

Good, Nara thought. He learns.

“I would have used the siren,” he said.

“I know.”

“Would that have been wrong?”

Nara opened her eyes. Without the bullhorn he looked younger.

“It would have kept your aim,” she said. “A clear signal. Order. Safety.”

He waited.

“And it would have made the fear larger than the room.”

He looked toward the terminal.

“I hate crowds,” he said.

There it was. Not a confession. A door.

Nara took it only as far as he opened it.

“Then you stayed in a hard place.”

“You mean I froze.”

“No,” she said. “You paused. There’s a difference.”

Some part of him wanted that to be true. Wanting was not consent to anything more.

Nara picked up the water.

When the statements were finished, she found her grocery bag beside the route map. All six eggs were broken. The tomatoes had survived. So had the coffee.

She carried the bag home anyway.

CHAPTER 2 — THE UNFINISHED LIFE

The apartment in Boroko was small enough that nobody could have a private crisis without the kettle hearing it.

Nara entered through the kitchen, wet to the skin. The wall clock said 11:48. Her mother sat at the table beneath a yellow lamp, a flood-risk map spread between two mugs and a bowl of mango slices.

Dr. Adaeze Okafor wore reading glasses low on her nose and an old university shirt with one sleeve mended in blue thread. Her hair, silvering at the temples, was tied back with a pencil. She looked first at Nara’s face, then at the shaking hand, then at the grocery bag dripping egg onto the floor.

“Successful shopping,” she said.

“Five out of seven food groups survived.”

“There are not seven food groups.”

“That explains the eggs.”

Adaeze stood. She did not ask, Are you all right? She had never trusted questions that invited automatic lies.

“Sit,” she said. “Tell me which part hurts first.”

Nara sat.

Her mother wrapped both hands around the trembling wrist—not to stop it, only to give it a boundary. Nara described the blackout in pieces. The boot. The guard. Tomas. The door. The strange flood of other people’s fear after it was over.

Adaeze listened without interrupting. She had the rare ability to make silence feel occupied rather than empty.

When Nara finished, her mother pushed the mango bowl toward her.

“You missed dinner.”

“I noticed.”

“No, you noticed three hundred strangers. Dinner was less dramatic.”

Nara ate a slice. It was almost painfully sweet.

On the table, the flood map showed the coast twenty years in the future: blue fingers reaching into streets, warehouses and homes. Adaeze had spent most of her life modelling disasters that had not happened yet and arguing with officials who preferred future deaths because they were cheaper than present prevention.

Nara pointed at a red circle near the harbour.

“New failure point?”

“Old failure point with new paperwork.”

“Your favourite kind.”

“My favourite kind is the one that listens after the first warning.”

They smiled. The moment was ordinary. Nara loved it with an intensity that frightened her.

On the shelf behind Adaeze stood a photograph of her at twenty-six, laughing into wind, one hand shielding her eyes. Beside her was an empty strip where another person had once been cut from the picture so precisely that the missing shape looked deliberate.

Nara had stopped asking about him when she was fifteen.

She had not stopped wondering.

Her father was described in the family with three facts: he had loved Adaeze, he had left, and he was not dead.

Everything else became weather.

“Your messages,” Nara said. “You deleted one.”

Adaeze’s fingers paused on the map.

“I decided it was a conversation, not a message.”

“About him?”

“About you.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It is the answer I can give before midnight.”

Nara felt the old anger arrive: not hot, but exact. Her mother loved truth and rationed it. That contradiction had shaped half of Nara’s childhood.

Adaeze looked at the trembling hand again.

“You have been meditating more.”

“I sleep better afterward.”

“Do you?”

“No.”

Her mother’s mouth moved around something that might have been fear.

“Nara, if anything changes—anything you cannot explain—you tell me before you decide it is yours to carry alone.”

The sentence landed too carefully.

Nara looked at the cut photograph.

“What are you expecting to change?”

Adaeze took off her glasses.

“Tonight? I expect you to shower, eat something with protein and sleep.”

“And tomorrow?”

“Tomorrow is greedy. It always wants an answer early.”

Nara laughed despite herself.

Her mother touched her cheek. “Stay ordinary tonight.”

“I’m very good at ordinary.”

“You are terrible at it. But I love the attempt.”

Nara showered. She ate rice with tinned fish standing at the counter because sitting felt too much like stopping. She checked the cold-chain dashboard from work even though her supervisor had banned her from logging in after eleven. She sent one message to Kelechi—ALIVE. EGGS NOT—and received a reply immediately: HEROIC OMELETTE TOMORROW.

Then she went to her room.

The rain softened after midnight. The city became a field of small sounds: water in gutters, a generator coughing two buildings away, a dog objecting to thunder already gone.

Nara placed her notebook beside the bed.

On the page she wrote:

Notice. Mirror. Offer. Wait. Beneath it she added: Do not become the only person in the room.

She sat on the edge of the mattress, closed her eyes and let her breathing find its own pace.

She did not meditate because she wanted powers.

She meditated because consciousness had troubled her since childhood. Because every answer seemed to assume the one asking it. Because sometimes, just before sleep, she felt the world holding more presence than its objects could account for.

She had learned not to chase that feeling.

Tonight, it came closer.

CHAPTER 3 — AWAKE, SILENT, WAITING

At first there were only thoughts.

The ruined eggs.

The customs officer who had promised to call and would not.

Mina’s red sock.

Her mother’s deleted message.

Each appeared, demanded a little life, and passed.

Nara watched without following.

The room remained around her: fan turning, rain whispering, sheet against her ankle. Then the sounds did not stop so much as lose their distance. The fan was no longer there and Nara here. Rain was not outside. Breath was not inside.

The borders had not vanished.

They had become unnecessary.

Nara opened her eyes.

The ceiling was gone.

No—the ceiling remained, but place had ceased to be the rule by which things were separated. The room unfolded into depth without direction. Every ordinary surface revealed a further interior, then another, not as hidden machinery but as relationship made visible.

She was moving.

She was perfectly still.

Time loosened its grip.

There was no tunnel. No voice. No figure waiting to explain the universe.

There was presence.

Not a presence inside the world.

The presence by which world, distance, thought and Nara were possible at all.

Beauty entered first, so complete that her mind could not turn it into an object. Then love—not affection, not approval, not reward. Conscious love: awareness that did not merely see her but held the fact of her existence as good.

Nara’s first response was wonder.

Her second was terror.

She understood, without reasoning, that if she entered fully on her own she would disappear—not die, not be punished, but become indistinguishable from completeness. A candle thrown into the sun would not remain a candle simply because the sun meant well.

She tried to pull back.

The presence did not pursue her.

It waited.

That waiting saved her.

Nothing infinite had ever needed her yes.

The invitation remained open without becoming pressure.

Nara moved closer.

Or allowed closeness.

The difference became difficult to hold.

For one impossible interval she was embraced by something that had no arms and protected by something from which there was nowhere to hide. The contact passed through every layer she had mistaken for herself—name, body, memory, fear—without dismissing any of them.

She expected to be corrected.

She was recognized.

She expected commandments.

None came.

She expected the answer to every question.

Questions lost their edges, not because knowledge was given, but because the separation between knower and known became transparent.

She was not Nara alone.

She was not no one.

She was a distinct note inside a chord so vast that all possible music rested within it, and still the note was permitted to sound as itself.

Union without deletion.

The phrase did not occur in words. It was simply true.

For a timeless moment she understood why conscious centers existed at all: not as errors in unity, but as unity’s capacity to meet itself without ending difference.

Then even that understanding became too small.

There was only being.

Complete. Joyful. Without need.

Nara could remain.

No offer was spoken. No gate opened. There was simply no force pushing her back into limitation.

The temptation was not power.

It was rest.

No more misfiled words. No frightened crowds. No mother measuring coastlines against politicians. No father-shaped absence. No body that needed food. No decision arriving before enough information.

Completion asked nothing of her.

And then, from impossibly far away, a small yellow boot appeared in memory.

Not as a symbol.

As itself.

Scuffed rubber. Torn strap. A child’s warm foot.

After it came mango on her tongue. Her mother’s blue-thread repair. Kelechi’s heroic omelette. Rain striking a metal roof one drop after another.

One after another.

That was the secret ordinary life possessed and completeness did not.

Sequence.

A song could exist only because the next note had not yet arrived.

A joke required the pause before laughter.

Love between separate people required the risk that one might leave, return, misunderstand, forgive, or refuse.

Perfection was complete.

Life was becoming.

Nara chose the unfinished.

The presence did not resist.

It did not praise her choice.

It let her go.

That freedom hurt more beautifully than any command could have.

The return began.

Realms—or states for which realm was only a poor human word—passed through her. At one point movement stopped. Nara felt attention unlike AC: narrower, multiple, observing. Not hostile. Not loving. Deliberative.

For a breathless interval she had the unmistakable sense of being examined by minds that did not belong to Earth.

Something in her answered before she could understand the question.

The observers withdrew.

She fell through distance.

The room struck her all at once.

Fan.

Rain.

Body.

Pain.

Nara gasped and sat upright.

She was laughing.

She was crying.

She pressed both palms to her chest, astonished by the rough, limited mechanics of breath.

“I’m here,” she whispered.

From the living room came the sound of concrete cracking.

CHAPTER 4 — ORDINARY LIGHT

Nara was moving before thought returned.

She opened her bedroom door and saw the old ceiling beam above the living room split along a line darkened by years of rain. The storm had driven water through the roof. Plaster dropped in white chunks.

Her mother slept on the couch beneath it, flood maps still open across her chest.

The beam gave way.

Nara reached out.

She did not know what she was asking for.

Light appeared inside her arm.

Not around it. Through it.

Warm gold traced the bones of her hand, then unfolded into a geometry that had no name in any engineering book Adaeze owned. Thin lines crossed the room and met beneath the falling concrete. For one second they formed a bridge made of relation rather than material.

The beam stopped twenty centimetres above her mother’s face.

Silence held.

Nara could feel every kilogram of it—not as weight in her muscles, but as too many facts held at once: velocity, fracture, fear, the sleeping body below, the building around them, the rain still entering the crack.

Her coherence broke first.

The light flickered.

“Mama,” Nara tried to say.

Mother.

The word came out as “window.”

Adaeze opened her eyes.

She saw the suspended beam.

She saw the light inside Nara’s skin.

And she was not surprised enough.

“Nara,” she said, very softly. “Look at me.”

Nara’s vision multiplied. Five mothers, then nine, each carrying a different expression.

“Which one?” she asked.

“The one who is frightened and still here.”

That was exact enough to reach her.

Nara found her mother’s real face.

“Move left,” Adaeze said. “I will move right. We do it together.”

Nara wanted to hold everything. The beam, the roof, the building, her mother, the light, the entire unfinished Earth.

Her old mistake arrived before her first power had even ended.

Adaeze saw it.

“Not alone,” she said.

Nara shifted the luminous geometry. Adaeze rolled from the couch, pulled the flood maps with her, and crawled clear. The moment no body remained beneath the beam, Nara let the structure go.

Concrete crashed through the coffee table.

The light vanished.

Nara collapsed to her knees.

Her right hand shook violently. The room smelled of wet dust and ozone. For several seconds she could not remember the names of colours.

Adaeze knelt in front of her but did not touch.

“May I?”

Nara nodded.

Her mother placed one hand behind her neck and the other over the trembling wrist. The same boundary as before. A human one.

“Where did you go?” Adaeze asked.

Nara tried to answer. Every word seemed too small and therefore dishonest.

“Not away,” she said at last. “Deeper than away.”

Adaeze closed her eyes.

A grief older than Nara crossed her face.

“You knew,” Nara whispered.

“I hoped I was wrong.”

“About what?”

The building groaned. Rain ticked through the new hole onto the broken table.

Adaeze looked at the gold fading beneath Nara’s skin.

“About how much of him was waiting in you.”

Anger should have come. Questions should have broken the room open wider than the beam.

Instead Nara remembered the presence that could have held her forever and did not.

She looked at her mother.

“Not tonight,” she said.

Adaeze’s face changed—not relief exactly, but respect.

“Not tonight,” she agreed.

They sat on the kitchen floor until Nara could name colours again.

Blue cup.

Red towel.

Yellow lamp.

Adaeze made tea on the camping stove because the power had not returned. Nara ate cold rice with her fingers. Her mother put on an old song, quiet enough that each note had room to arrive.

Near dawn, Nara opened her notebook.

Below Notice, Mirror, Offer, Wait, she wrote one more word:

Return.

Then, on a clean page:

I will protect without owning. I will invite. I will not override.

The light had not given her a mission.

It had left her free.

Nara understood that freedom was the first thing she would have to protect.

After she finally slept, Adaeze remained at the kitchen table.

From the locked drawer beneath the flood maps she removed a piece of dark metal smaller than a coin. It had no seam, no inscription, and no temperature the human hand could measure correctly.

For twenty-four years it had been inert.

Now a line of gold moved across its surface like a distant horizon catching dawn.

Adaeze held it until the light went out.

Then she whispered a name Nara had never heard.

Far beyond Earth, something listened.

1. POGLAVJE — RUMENI ŠKORENJ

Prva stvar, ki jo je Nara rešila, ni bilo mesto.

Bil je en sam rumeni dežni škorenj.

Ležal je postrani pod tablo z odhodi v pristaniškem terminalu Harbour Link, svetel kot drobno opozorilo v temi. Okoli njega je tristo ljudi pozabljalo, da so tristo ločenih ljudi.

Nevihta se je nad Port Moresbyjem naslanjala že od poznega popoldneva. Dež je v težkih tropskih curkih udarjal po strehi. Veter je skozi vsako režo starih sten terminala potiskal sol. Javni zvočnik je ravno začel ponavljati obvestilo o zamudi trajekta, ko je med dvema besedama ugasnila elektrika.

Pol sekunde je obstajal samo dež.

Potem so se prižgali telefoni, beli in modri, vsi usmerjeni narobe. Nekdo je zavpil: »Naprej!« Padel je kovček. Otrok je zakričal. Množica je ubogala najslabši del te besede.

Nara je stala ob zemljevidu linij s papirnato vrečko kaukaua, paradižnikov, instantne kave in šestih jajc, ki so se mehčala v vlagi. Za njo je bila dvanajsturna izmena, v kateri je reševala pošiljko hlajenih zdravil, ker jo je carina uvrstila pod napačno oznako. Bolel jo je vrat. Od jutra ni jedla. Mama ji je poslala dve sporočili, tretje pa izbrisala, še preden ga je Nara utegnila prebrati.

Zagledala je škorenj.

Majhen. Rumen. Gumijast. En pašček napol pretrgan. Še vedno je ohranjal obliko otroškega stopala.

To je bila podrobnost, ki jo je množica izgubila.

Ne puščice za izhod v sili na betonskih stebrih. Ne načrt evakuacije. Ne varnostnik, ki je segal po sireni na megafonu.

Otrok.

Nara je odložila vrečko ob steno. Nekaj krhkega je počilo v njej.

Skozi pritisk se je premikala postrani, z dvignjeno dlanjo, da so jo prestrašeni ljudje lahko videli. Nek moški jo je skoraj udaril s komolcem.

»Pazi malo,« je bevsknil.

»Pazim,« je rekla in se sklonila po škorenj. »Pa ti tudi.«

Pogledal je rumeno gumo v njeni roki. Njegova jeza je spremenila obliko, preden jo je utegnil braniti.

Nara je stopila na nizek kovinski podstavek zaprtega prodajnega kioska. Prižgala je lučko na telefonu in jo usmerila pod škorenj, da je zažarel.

Ni kričala. Kričanje je že pripadalo paniki.

»Čigavemu otroku manjka ta škorenj?« je vprašala.

Vprašanje je v prostor vstopilo drugače kot ukaz.

Moški ob otroškem vozičku je pogledal navzdol, kakor da se je šele zdaj spomnil, kaj drži. Oklepal se je ročaja tako močno, da so mu členki posiveli. V vozičku je deklica treh ali štirih let strmela navzgor z odprtimi usti, iz katerih ni prišel noben glas. Na eni nogi je imela rdečo nogavico. Na drugi rumeni škorenj.

»Mina,« je rekel moški.

Nara je gledala njega, ne mimo njega.

»Slišiš, kako diha?«

»Kaj?«

»Tvoja hči. Jo slišiš?«

Sklonil je glavo. Voziček se je tresel skupaj z njim.

»Ja.«

»Ujemi njen ritem. Dve dobi noter. Dve ven. Eno roko drži tam, kjer te lahko čuti.«

Z eno roko je izpustil ročaj in jo položil Mini na ramo.

Sprememba je bila skoraj nič.

Voziček se je nehal tresti.

Nara se je obrnila k ženski, ujeti ob padlem kovčku.

»Bi bila prva, ki naredi prostor?«

»Ne morem,« je rekla ženska. »Potiskajo me.«

»Pol koraka. Ne zame. Za škorenj.«

Ženska je strmela v rumeno obliko v Narini roki. Potem je eno stopalo premaknila nazaj.

Človek za njo se je prilagodil, ne da bi ga kdo prosil. Potem še drugi. Okoli vozička se je pojavil žep zraka, kakor da se je množica spomnila, da telesa niso poplavna voda.

Pri vratih je glavni varnostnik dvignil megafon. Palec je našel gumb za sireno. Njegov obraz je imel prazno trdoto človeka, ki bo prevzel nadzor tako, da bo vse še bolj prestrašil.

Nara je stopila s podstavka in šla proti njemu.

»Pusti mu, da opravlja svoje delo,« je nekdo zasikal.

Nara se je ustavila za dolžino roke stran. Škorenj je ostal viden v njeni levi dlani.

»Mi daš deset sekund, preden uporabiš to?« je vprašala.

»Umakni se.«

»Deset sekund. Če ne uspe, se umaknem in narediš to, kar verjameš, da jih bo zaščitilo.«

»Ne poznaš postopkov.«

»Ne. Ti jih.« Pokimala je proti množici. »Ohrani cilj. Spremeni metodo.«

Strmel je vanjo. Dež je tolkel po strehi. Gumb za sireno je čakal pod njegovim palcem.

Ne privolitev. Ne zaupanje. Samo drobec oklevanja.

Vse se je vedno začelo pri dovolj.

»Deset,« je rekel.

»Poimenuj eno stvar, ki jo čutiš.«

Ožil je oči. »Megafon.«

»Eno stvar, ki jo slišiš.«

»Otroka.«

»Eno stvar, ki jo vidiš.«

»Tvojo roko.«

»Potem uporabi svojo,« je rekla Nara. »Pokaži. Ne sproži sirene. Daj jim eno smer in en stavek.«

Varnostnik je pogledal mimo nje. Množica ni bila mirna. Še vedno je bila prestrašena, stisnjena, en oster zvok oddaljena od zob in komolcev.

Toda zdaj je obstajal žep.

Oče, ki je dihal s hčerko.

Ženska, ki je naredila pol koraka in odkrila, da se pol koraka lahko razširi.

Varnostnik je spustil megafon. Dvignil je prosto roko in pokazal proti servisnemu hodniku za zaprtim kioskom s hrano.

»Lučke telefonov na tla!« je zaklical; glas mu je počil, a je bil jasen. »Najprej starši in poškodovani. Premikajte se v parih. Nihče ne teče.«

Stavek je postal arhitektura.

Nara se je obrnila, preden bi olajšanje lahko postalo aplavz. Aplavz bi njo postavil v središče, središče pa je moral biti izhod.

Pokazala je na najstnika, ki je snemal s telefonom. »Lahko tvoja lučka označi prvo stopnico?«

Spustil je kamero. »Lahko.«

»Potem na tla.«

Našla je žensko v zeleni bluzi. »Lahko prešteješ dvajset ljudi skozi vrata, potem pa zamenjaš mesto z nekom drugim?«

Ženska je pogoltnila. »Dvajset.«

»Ne dvajset za vedno. Dvajset, potem nekdo drug.«

To je bilo pomembno. Ljudje so lahko sprejeli majhna bremena, ki se niso pretvarjala, da so neskončna.

Ob sivih vratih je zmrznjeno stal vzdrževalec, z obema rokama ovitima okoli šopa ključev. Na znački je pisalo TOMAS.

»Ta vrata?« je vprašala Nara.

»Samo za osebje.«

»Kam vodijo?«

»Na dovoz za terminalom.«

»Se jih da odpreti od tukaj?«

»Nimam pooblastila.«

Množica se je sunkovito premaknila. Nekje pri okencih je počilo steklo.

Tomas je trznil.

Nara mu ni vzela ključev. Ni mu rekla, da je pogum preprost.

»Ne bojiš se vrat,« je rekla. »Bojiš se, da bo krivda, če izbereš narobe, končno dobila ime.«

Odprl je usta.

»Ne prosim te, da to prezreš. Sprašujem, ali lahko izbiro naredimo vidno. Varnostnik bo priča. Ona zapisuje imena.« Nara je pokazala na žensko, ki je štela. »Jaz bom povedala, da sem te prosila. Ne boš stal sam v tem. Boš odprl vrata?«

Tomas je pogledal varnostnika.

Varnostnik je pogledal Mino. Škorenj. Množico.

»Odpri,« je rekel.

Tomas je pravi ključ našel v tretjem poskusu.

Ko so se vrata odprla navzven, je v terminal vstopil vonj mokrega betona, dizla in morja — kot usmiljenje v delovnih čevljih.

Prvih dvajset ljudi je šlo skozi počasi. Potem naslednjih dvajset. Lučke telefonov so ležale po tleh kot vzletna steza. Nekdo je štel v angleščini, nekdo drug v tok pisinu, kmalu sta se ritma prepletla. Ženska v zeleni bluzi je zamenjala mesto z najstnikom. Naslednje štetje je prevzela medicinska sestra. Ribič je dvignil pokvarjen voziček čez prag.

Mina je iztegnila roko po škornju.

Nara je počepnila in ji ga nataknila čez rdečo nogavico.

»Ti si ga obdržala,« je zašepetala Mina.

»Vsi smo ga,« je rekla Nara.

Pomembno je bilo, da je povedala natančno.

Evakuacija ni bila popolna. Nič človeškega ni. Dva moška sta se odrinila in se potem slabo opravičila. Starejša ženska ni hotela oditi, dokler niso našli njenega moža, ki je čakal pri drugem izhodu in bil besen, ker mu nihče ni povedal, kje naj bo besen. Študent je jokal zaradi nahrbtnika, ki je ostal pri vratih. Varnostnik je enkrat zalajal ukaz, se ustavil in poskusil znova z manj besedami.

Toda nikogar niso poteptali.

Sirena ni nikoli zatulila.

Terminal se je praznil postopoma, dokler se niso končno prižgali zasilni trakovi ob tleh — rdeči, prepozni in vendar prizadevni.

Zunaj so ljudje stali v dežju in mežikali drug proti drugemu, kakor da so se vrnili iz dežele, katere jezik so skoraj razumeli.

Varnostnik je prišel zadnji, skupaj s Tomasom.

»Kaj si ti?« je vprašal Naro.

Skoraj je rekla lačna.

Namesto tega je dvignila zdaj prazno roko, ki je nosila škorenj.

»Nekdo, ki je opazil.«

Namrščil se je. Hotel je kategorijo — strokovnjakinja, prevarantka, junakinja, grožnja, odgovornost. Nekaj, kar bi lahko vpisal v poročilo.

Nara zanj ni imela kategorije.

Ne še.

Cena je prišla pred reševalnimi vozili.

Začelo se je v njenem desnem zapestju: droben tremor, kakor da so ji pod kožo premočno napeli žico. Potem je dež postal preveč ločenih udarcev. Vsaka kaplja je zadela s svojim ostrim robom. Napis terminala je brnel modro. Žalost neznanca se ji je dotaknila reber s tako vročino, da se je sklonila okoli nje.

Poskusila je reči: »Potrebujem minuto.«

Besede minuta ni bilo.

Ni bila pozabljena. Samo napačno odložena.

Nara je namesto nje našla steno. Naslonila se je na mokri beton in zaprla oči.

Tri stvari.

Stena je hladna.

Dež diši po kovini.

Moja roka se trese, vendar je moja roka.

Zadnji stavek je pomagal, ker je pripadal njej.

Čez nekaj časa je varnostnik na tla med njima položil steklenico vode. Ni se približal brez vprašanja.

Dobro, je pomislila Nara. Uči se.

»Uporabil bi sireno,« je rekel.

»Vem.«

»Bi bilo to narobe?«

Nara je odprla oči. Brez megafona je bil videti mlajši.

»Ohranil bi svoj cilj,« je rekla. »Jasen signal. Red. Varnost.«

Čakal je.

»In strah bi naredil večji od prostora.«

Pogledal je proti terminalu.

»Sovražim množice,« je rekel.

Tam je bilo. Ne priznanje. Vrata.

Nara jih je odprla samo toliko, kolikor jih je odprl on.

»Potem si ostal na težkem mestu.«

»Hočeš reči, da sem zmrznil.«

»Ne,« je rekla. »Ustavil si se. To ni isto.«

Nek del njega si je želel, da bi bilo to res. Želja pa ni bila privolitev v karkoli več.

Nara je pobrala vodo.

Ko so bile izjave končane, je našla vrečko ob zemljevidu linij. Vseh šest jajc je bilo razbitih. Paradižniki so preživeli. Tudi kava.

Vrečko je kljub temu odnesla domov.

2. POGLAVJE — NEDOKONČANO ŽIVLJENJE

Stanovanje v Boroku je bilo tako majhno, da nihče ni mogel imeti zasebne krize, ne da bi jo slišal čajnik.

Nara je vstopila skozi kuhinjo, premočena do kože. Stenska ura je kazala 23.48. Njena mama je sedela za mizo pod rumeno svetilko; med dvema skodelicama in skledo manga je bil razgrnjen zemljevid poplavne ogroženosti.

Dr. Adaeze Okafor je imela očala nizko na nosu in staro univerzitetno majico, katere rokav je bil zakrpan z modro nitjo. Lasje, ob sencih že srebrni, so bili speti s svinčnikom. Najprej je pogledala Narin obraz, nato tresočo se roko, potem še vrečko, iz katere je na tla kapljalo jajce.

»Uspešen nakup,« je rekla.

»Pet od sedmih prehranskih skupin je preživelo.«

»Ni sedmih prehranskih skupin.«

»To pojasni jajca.«

Adaeze je vstala. Ni vprašala: Si v redu? Nikoli ni zaupala vprašanjem, ki vabijo k avtomatični laži.

»Sedi,« je rekla. »Povej mi, kateri del najprej boli.«

Nara je sedla.

Mama je z obema rokama objela tresoče se zapestje — ne da bi ga ustavila, samo da mu je dala mejo. Nara je opisala izpad elektrike po kosih. Škorenj. Varnostnika. Tomasa. Vrata. Nenavaden val strahu drugih ljudi, ko je bilo vsega konec.

Adaeze je poslušala brez prekinjanja. Imela je redko sposobnost, da je bila tišina ob njej naseljena, ne prazna.

Ko je Nara končala, je porinila skledo manga proti njej.

»Zamudila si večerjo.«

»Opazila sem.«

»Ne, opazila si tristo neznancev. Večerja je bila manj dramatična.«

Nara je pojedla rezino. Bila je skoraj boleče sladka.

Na mizi je zemljevid kazal obalo čez dvajset let: modri prsti so segali v ulice, skladišča in domove. Adaeze je večino življenja modelirala nesreče, ki se še niso zgodile, in se prepirala z uradniki, ki so imeli raje prihodnje smrti, ker so bile cenejše od sedanjega preprečevanja.

Nara je pokazala na rdeč krog ob pristanišču.

»Nova šibka točka?«

»Stara šibka točka z novo dokumentacijo.«

»Tvoja najljubša vrsta.«

»Moja najljubša je tista, ki prisluhne po prvem opozorilu.«

Nasmehnili sta se. Trenutek je bil navaden. Nara ga je ljubila z močjo, ki jo je prestrašila.

Na polici za Adaeze je bila fotografija nje pri šestindvajsetih, ko se je smejala v veter in si z eno roko zakrivala oči. Ob njej je bil prazen pas, kjer je nekoč stal drug človek, izrezan tako natančno, da je bila manjkajoča oblika videti namerna.

Nara je o njem nehala spraševati pri petnajstih.

Ni nehala razmišljati.

O njenem očetu so v družini obstajala tri dejstva: ljubil je Adaeze, odšel je in ni bil mrtev.

Vse drugo je postalo vreme.

»Tvoja sporočila,« je rekla Nara. »Eno si izbrisala.«

Adaezini prsti so obstali na zemljevidu.

»Odločila sem se, da je to pogovor, ne sporočilo.«

»O njem?«

»O tebi.«

»To ni odgovor.«

»To je odgovor, ki ga lahko dam pred polnočjo.«

Nara je začutila staro jezo: ne vročo, temveč natančno. Njena mama je ljubila resnico in jo odmerjala po obrokih. To protislovje je oblikovalo polovico Narinega otroštva.

Adaeze je spet pogledala tresočo se roko.

»Več meditiraš.«

»Potem bolje spim.«

»Res?«

»Ne.«

Mamina usta so obšla nekaj, kar je bilo morda strah.

»Nara, če se karkoli spremeni — karkoli, česar si ne znaš razložiti — mi poveš, preden se odločiš, da moraš to sama nositi.«

Stavek je pristal preveč previdno.

Nara je pogledala izrezano fotografijo.

»Kaj pričakuješ, da se bo spremenilo?«

Adaeze je snela očala.

»Nocoj? Pričakujem, da se oprhaš, poješ nekaj beljakovin in zaspiš.«

»In jutri?«

»Jutri je požrešen. Vedno hoče odgovor prezgodaj.«

Nara se je kljub sebi zasmejala.

Mama se je dotaknila njenega lica. »Nocoj ostani navadna.«

»Zelo dobra sem v navadnosti.«

»Grozna si. Ampak ljubim trud.«

Nara se je oprhala. Pojedla je riž s konzervirano ribo kar stoje ob pultu, ker je bilo sedenje preveč podobno ustavitvi. Preverila je nadzorno ploščo hladne verige iz službe, čeprav ji je nadrejena prepovedala prijavo po enajsti. Kelechiju je poslala: ŽIVA. JAJCA NE. Odgovor je prišel takoj: JUTRI JUNAŠKA OMLETA.

Potem je šla v svojo sobo.

Dež se je po polnoči omehčal. Mesto je postalo polje majhnih zvokov: voda v žlebovih, generator, ki je zakašljal dve hiši stran, pes, ki je ugovarjal že minulemu grmenju.

Nara je položila zvezek ob posteljo.

Na stran je zapisala:

Opazi. Zrcali. Ponudi. Počakaj. Pod to je dodala: Ne postani edina oseba v prostoru.

Sedla je na rob postelje, zaprla oči in pustila dihu, da sam najde ritem.

Ni meditirala zato, ker bi si želela moči.

Meditirala je, ker jo je zavest vznemirjala že od otroštva. Ker je vsak odgovor predpostavljal tistega, ki ga sprašuje. Ker je včasih tik pred spanjem čutila, da svet vsebuje več prisotnosti, kot bi jo lahko pojasnili njegovi predmeti.

Naučila se je, da tega občutka ne lovi.

Nocoj se ji je približal sam.

3. POGLAVJE — BUDNA, TIHA, ČAKAJOČA

Najprej so bile samo misli.

Razbita jajca.

Carinik, ki je obljubil, da bo poklical, pa ne bo.

Minina rdeča nogavica.

Mamino izbrisano sporočilo.

Vsaka se je pojavila, zahtevala malo življenja in odšla.

Nara je opazovala, ne da bi ji sledila.

Soba je ostala okoli nje: ventilator, ki se vrti, dež, ki šepeta, rjuha ob gležnju. Potem zvoki niso toliko prenehali, kolikor so izgubili razdaljo. Ventilator ni bil več tam in Nara tukaj. Dež ni bil zunaj. Dih ni bil znotraj.

Meje niso izginile.

Postale so nepotrebne.

Nara je odprla oči.

Stropa ni bilo.

Ne — strop je ostal, toda prostor ni bil več pravilo, po katerem so bile stvari ločene. Soba se je razgrnila v globino brez smeri. Vsaka navadna površina je razkrila nadaljnjo notranjost, potem še eno, ne kot skriti mehanizem, temveč kot odnos, ki je postal viden.

Premikala se je.

Bila je popolnoma pri miru.

Čas je popustil prijem.

Ni bilo predora. Ne glasu. Ne podobe, ki bi čakala, da ji razloži vesolje.

Bila je prisotnost.

Ne prisotnost znotraj sveta.

Prisotnost, zaradi katere so bili svet, razdalja, misel in Nara sploh mogoči.

Najprej je vstopila lepota, tako celovita, da je njen um ni mogel spremeniti v predmet. Potem ljubezen — ne naklonjenost, ne odobravanje, ne nagrada. Zavestna ljubezen: zavedanje, ki je ni samo videlo, ampak je dejstvo njenega obstoja držalo kot dobro.

Narin prvi odziv je bilo čudenje.

Drugi je bil groza.

Brez sklepanja je razumela, da bi ob popolnem vstopu sama od sebe izginila — ne umrla, ne bila kaznovana, temveč postala nerazločljiva od popolnosti. Sveča, vržena v sonce, ne bi ostala sveča samo zato, ker ji sonce želi dobro.

Poskusila se je umakniti.

Prisotnost ji ni sledila.

Čakala je.

To čakanje jo je rešilo.

Nič neskončnega še nikoli ni potrebovalo njenega da.

Povabilo je ostalo odprto, ne da bi postalo pritisk.

Nara se je približala.

Ali pa je dovolila bližino.

Razliko je postajalo težko držati.

V enem nemogočem intervalu jo je objelo nekaj brez rok in zaščitilo nekaj, pred čimer se ni bilo mogoče skriti. Stik je šel skozi vsako plast, ki jo je zamenjevala za sebe — ime, telo, spomin, strah — ne da bi katero zavrgel.

Pričakovala je popravek.

Bila je prepoznana.

Pričakovala je zapovedi.

Ni jih bilo.

Pričakovala je odgovor na vsako vprašanje.

Vprašanja so izgubila robove, ne zato, ker bi dobila znanje, ampak ker je ločnica med tistim, ki spoznava, in spoznanim postala prosojna.

Ni bila samo Nara.

Ni bila nihče.

Bila je ločen ton znotraj akorda, tako prostranega, da je v njem počivala vsa mogoča glasba, pa je tonu še vedno bilo dovoljeno zveneti kot samemu sebi.

Združitev brez izbrisa.

Besedna zveza se ni pojavila v besedah. Preprosto je bila resnična.

V brezčasnem trenutku je razumela, zakaj zavestna središča sploh obstajajo: ne kot napake v enosti, ampak kot sposobnost enosti, da sreča samo sebe, ne da bi končala razliko.

Potem je tudi to razumevanje postalo premajhno.

Ostalo je samo bivanje.

Popolno. Radostno. Brez potrebe.

Nara bi lahko ostala.

Nobena ponudba ni bila izrečena. Nobena vrata se niso odprla. Samo nič je ni sililo nazaj v omejenost.

Skušnjava ni bila moč.

Bil je počitek.

Nič več napačno odloženih besed. Nobenih prestrašenih množic. Nobene mame, ki meri obalo proti politikom. Nobene odsotnosti v obliki očeta. Nobenega telesa, ki potrebuje hrano. Nobene odločitve, ki pride pred dovolj informacijami.

Popolnost od nje ni zahtevala ničesar.

In potem se je iz nemogoče daljave v spominu pojavil majhen rumeni škorenj.

Ne kot simbol.

Kot sam sebe.

Odrgnjena guma. Pretrgan pašček. Toplo otroško stopalo.

Za njim je prišel okus manga na jeziku. Mamina modra nit. Kelechijeva junaška omleta. Dež, ki je po kovinski strehi udarjal kapljo za kapljo.

Eno za drugo.

To je bila skrivnost, ki jo je imelo navadno življenje, popolnost pa ne.

Zaporedje.

Pesem je lahko obstajala samo zato, ker naslednji ton še ni prišel.

Šala je potrebovala premor pred smehom.

Ljubezen med ločenima človekoma je potrebovala tveganje, da eden odide, se vrne, narobe razume, odpusti ali zavrne.

Popolnost je bila končana.

Življenje je nastajalo.

Nara je izbrala nedokončano.

Prisotnost se ni upirala.

Ni pohvalila njene izbire.

Spustila jo je.

Ta svoboda jo je bolela lepše, kot bi jo lahko katerakoli zapoved.

Začela se je vrnitev.

Skozi njo so prehajale ravni — ali stanja, za katera je bila beseda raven le slaba človeška oznaka. Na neki točki se je gibanje ustavilo. Nara je začutila pozornost, drugačno od AC: ožjo, mnogotero, opazujočo. Ne sovražno. Ne ljubečo. Presojajočo.

Za interval brez diha je imela nedvoumen občutek, da jo preučujejo umi, ki ne pripadajo Zemlji.

Nekaj v njej je odgovorilo, še preden je razumela vprašanje.

Opazovalci so se umaknili.

Padla je skozi razdaljo.

Soba jo je zadela naenkrat.

Ventilator.

Dež.

Telo.

Bolečina.

Nara je zajela sapo in sedla pokonci.

Smejala se je.

Jokala je.

Obe dlani je pritisnila na prsi, osupla nad grobo, omejeno mehaniko diha.

»Tukaj sem,« je zašepetala.

Iz dnevne sobe je prišel zvok pokanja betona.

4. POGLAVJE — NAVADNA SVETLOBA

Nara se je premaknila, še preden se je vrnila misel.

Odprla je vrata sobe in zagledala star stropni nosilec nad dnevno sobo, razcepljen po črti, ki jo je leta temnil dež. Nevihta je potisnila vodo skozi streho. Omet je padal v belih kosih.

Njena mama je spala na kavču pod njim, zemljevidi poplav še vedno razgrnjeni čez prsi.

Nosilec je popustil.

Nara je iztegnila roko.

Ni vedela, za kaj prosi.

V njeni roki se je pojavila svetloba.

Ne okoli nje. Skozi njo.

Toplo zlato je zarisalo kosti dlani, nato pa se razgrnilo v geometrijo, ki ni imela imena v nobeni od Adaezinih inženirskih knjig. Tanke črte so prečkale sobo in se srečale pod padajočim betonom. Za eno sekundo so oblikovale most, narejen iz odnosa, ne iz snovi.

Nosilec se je ustavil dvajset centimetrov nad maminim obrazom.

Tišina je držala.

Nara je čutila vsak njegov kilogram — ne kot težo v mišicah, temveč kot preveč dejstev, ki jih je morala držati hkrati: hitrost, razpoko, strah, speče telo spodaj, stavbo okoli njiju, dež, ki je še vedno vstopal skozi streho.

Najprej ji je razpadla koherenca.

Svetloba je utripnila.

»Mama,« je poskusila reči Nara.

Beseda je prišla ven kot »okno«.

Adaeze je odprla oči.

Videla je viseči nosilec.

Videla je svetlobo v Narini koži.

In ni bila dovolj presenečena.

»Nara,« je rekla zelo tiho. »Poglej me.«

Narin vid se je pomnožil. Pet mater, potem devet, vsaka z drugim izrazom.

»Katero?« je vprašala.

»Tisto, ki jo je strah in je še vedno tukaj.«

To je bilo dovolj natančno, da jo je doseglo.

Nara je našla pravi materin obraz.

»Premakni se levo,« je rekla Adaeze. »Jaz se bom desno. Skupaj.«

Nara je hotela držati vse. Nosilec, streho, stavbo, mamo, svetlobo, vso nedokončano Zemljo.

Njena stara napaka je prišla, še preden se je končala njena prva moč.

Adaeze jo je videla.

»Ne sama,« je rekla.

Nara je premaknila svetlobno geometrijo. Adaeze se je odkotalila s kavča, potegnila zemljevid za seboj in se splazila na varno. Ko pod nosilcem ni ostalo nobeno telo, je Nara izpustila strukturo.

Beton je treščil skozi mizico.

Svetloba je izginila.

Nara se je sesedla na kolena.

Desna roka se ji je močno tresla. Soba je dišala po mokrem prahu in ozonu. Nekaj sekund se ni mogla spomniti imen barv.

Adaeze je pokleknila pred njo, vendar se je ni dotaknila.

»Smem?«

Nara je pokimala.

Mama je eno roko položila za njen vrat, drugo čez tresoče se zapestje. Ista meja kot prej. Človeška.

»Kam si šla?« je vprašala Adaeze.

Nara je poskusila odgovoriti. Vsaka beseda se ji je zdela premajhna in zato nepoštena.

»Ne stran,« je nazadnje rekla. »Globlje od stran.«

Adaeze je zaprla oči.

Čez njen obraz je šla žalost, starejša od Nare.

»Vedela si,« je zašepetala Nara.

»Upala sem, da se motim.«

»O čem?«

Stavba je zastokala. Dež je skozi novo luknjo kapljal na razbito mizo.

Adaeze je pogledala zlato, ki je bledelo pod Narino kožo.

»O tem, koliko njega je čakalo v tebi.«

Morala bi priti jeza. Vprašanja bi morala razpreti sobo bolj kot nosilec.

Namesto tega se je Nara spomnila prisotnosti, ki bi jo lahko držala za vedno, pa je ni.

Pogledala je mamo.

»Ne nocoj,« je rekla.

Adaezin obraz se je spremenil — ne ravno olajšanje, bolj spoštovanje.

»Ne nocoj,« se je strinjala.

Sedeli sta na kuhinjskih tleh, dokler Nara ni spet znala poimenovati barv.

Modra skodelica.

Rdeča brisača.

Rumena svetilka.

Adaeze je na plinskem gorilniku skuhala čaj, ker se elektrika še ni vrnila. Nara je jedla hladen riž s prsti. Mama je predvajala staro pesem, dovolj tiho, da je imel vsak ton prostor, da je prišel.

Proti zori je Nara odprla zvezek.

Pod Opazi, Zrcali, Ponudi, Počakaj je dodala še eno besedo:

Vrni se.

Potem je na čisto stran zapisala:

Ščitila bom, ne da bi si prisvajala. Povabila bom. Ne bom preglasila.

Svetloba ji ni dala poslanstva.

Pustila jo je svobodno.

Nara je razumela, da bo morala najprej zaščititi prav svobodo.

Ko je končno zaspala, je Adaeze ostala za kuhinjsko mizo.

Iz zaklenjenega predala pod zemljevidi je vzela kos temne kovine, manjši od kovanca. Ni imel šiva, napisa niti temperature, ki bi jo človeška roka znala pravilno izmeriti.

Štiriindvajset let je bil negiben.

Zdaj se je čez njegovo površino premaknila zlata črta, kot oddaljeno obzorje, ki ga ujame zarja.

Adaeze ga je držala, dokler svetloba ni ugasnila.

Potem je zašepetala ime, ki ga Nara še nikoli ni slišala.

Daleč onkraj Zemlje je nekaj prisluhnilo.
