01 · Five Days

High Wendel, the school gate · Monday, ten past eight, four days before the board votes

The cable car crossed the sky above the gate every eleven minutes, a red cabin against thin cloud, and nobody in High Wendel looked up at it any more. In February an avalanche had shut the pass for eleven days. The car that opened in April had been built so that nothing would ever shut the town in again.

A woman in a freight-shed jacket stood at the gate with a six-year-old by the hand, and she did not let go when the bell rang.

SabrinaI'm Sabrina Vedd. I pack the bread that goes up on that car, six days a week, so don't think I came to ask you to switch it off.

SabrinaIn March this one said li forty ways before breakfast. Now she looks at me and does her face. That's all. She does her face.

IlluminaraTell me one of the forty.

SabrinaYes. It goes up at the end, like she's calling the dog.

The girl looked at the ground, at the gate, and at Illuminara, and said nothing at all. Her name was Nessa. She had her mother's chin.

A thin man with a folder under his arm had been waiting for the pause, and took it.

Dr OrsolyaPavel Orsolya, the school's physician. I would like to be brief and polite, and I can manage one of those. Forty children under nine, every one of them silent in class within the same six weeks. The nurse screened their hearing and all forty passed. The speech therapist who comes up from Dessau on the first Monday of the month has seen nine of them and found nothing in the ear, the mouth or the tongue. In February the mountain came down and the pass was shut for eleven days. Children keep their fear until it is safe to put it down. April was the first safe month.

SabrinaThey talk at home. A bit. In the back rooms, with the door shut.

Dr OrsolyaWhich is exactly what fear does with a safe room. And here is the harm I am trying to prevent. The reading assessments are in six weeks. A child who will not read aloud is held back a year, and a held-back child of seven learns that the school is not for her. The clinic at Dessau can watch them every day. I cannot, and the therapist manages once a month.

IlluminaraDoes every family have to agree to Dessau?

Dr OrsolyaIn my proposal, yes, and each child asked as well, where a child can be asked. On Friday the board votes on a draft that says placement and parental notice. I did not write those words, and I did not get them struck.

SabrinaThey'd take her on a notice.

Dr OrsolyaThey might. So on Friday I will oppose the wording and recommend the observation. Both. It is an unpopular position and I hold it.

IlluminaraWhat would show you that you were wrong?

He considered the question as though it were a patient.

Dr OrsolyaA forty-first fact that fear cannot explain. I have looked and I have not found one. If you find it, bring it to me before Friday and I will read it. I will not enjoy it.

IlluminaraI would like to sit in their room for one day and ask them for nothing.

Dr OrsolyaAsk them for nothing. That much I will gladly sign.

02 · A Bad Student

The little room, High Wendel school · Monday, half past ten

Illuminara had learned all four thousand words of Wenn overnight. The tones were another matter. A tone is not something you know; it is something you do with your voice, and the one syllable li meant river if it stayed level, sorrow if it fell and yes if it rose. Her voice could do anything, which was the trouble. It had no habits to blame.

Before she sat down she did something she did not usually do. She turned her hearing down to the range of an ordinary teacher, so that she would not be the only person in the room who could hear what the children could not. It felt like walking with a hand over one eye.

The room was warm from the stove. Twelve children sat on low benches in a silence so well-behaved it was almost a courtesy. Nessa Vedd sat in the second row.

The teacher, a quiet man named Daan, asked whether anyone would like the window opened. Nessa raised her chin and said one syllable, with a small bright lift at the end of it.

Illuminara heard river. Level and flat, a word for a thing that was not in the room.

The teacher heard river too. He tilted his head and told her, kindly, that there was no river in here, and some of the others smiled with him. It was a very kind smile. Nessa's face closed like a door on a hinge, and she said nothing more that morning.

She said yes, Illuminara thought. I watched her chin go up. I heard something else.

Four more times before the noon bell a child tried a word and was answered with something that did not fit it, and each time the child looked at the floor a little longer than the time before.

At noon they ran down to the garden below the school, and the silence broke the way a river does when the ice goes out.

The garden was loud. It was full of li, rising, falling and level, thrown from child to child like a ball, with whole sentences chasing it. Nessa stood on the wall at the bottom, bossing a boy about a bucket.

Illuminara sat on a stone beside her and, being a polite guest, tried a word. She said li, meaning yes, and let her voice go where she thought a yes should go.

Nessa put the bucket down and laughed so hard she had to hold on to the wall.

NessaThat's sorrow. You said you were sad. Yes goes up. Up, like a hill.

IlluminaraShow me which hill.

NessaA little hill. Like Gran's, behind the church. Not a mountain. You're doing a mountain.

Illuminara did a little hill. Nessa judged it with her head on one side, said it would do, and explained at length why the bucket was hers.

They talk down here. They stop at the door. She carried that sentence up the garden path like a full cup.

At the school door she let her hearing open all the way, and the floor spoke. A single flat note was rising through the stone, no louder than a kettle at the back of a house and as steady as a held breath. It sat exactly as high as a six-year-old's voice. Under every word said in that building it drew a level line, and a level line is what a river sounds like.

03 · Show Me the Sound

The upper station, machine hall · Monday, half past two

Ines Kallas had designed the cable car at a kitchen table in a valley that would not fund it, and she looked at Illuminara the way she looked at a frayed strand. The great bullwheel turned behind her, enormous and almost soundless.

InesSo you're the one who says my machine is eating children.

IlluminaraI think your machine sings, and I think the children's voices live exactly where it does.

InesMy machine is quiet. Fifty-one decibels at the school wall. That is a library.

IlluminaraA library is not a note. Your gearbox has a mesh tone at about three hundred and ten hertz at cruise, and a second one at six-twenty. A man's voice sits beneath it. A six-year-old's sits right on top of it. In Wenn the glide of the voice, up or down, is the whole difference between yes and sorrow, and a glide drawn over a steady note of its own pitch is a pencil line on a pencil-grey wall. They say yes in that room and are heard saying river. Nowhere else.

InesAt fifty-one decibels.

IlluminaraLoudness isn't what hides it. Sameness is.

Ines said nothing for as long as it took a cabin to leave the platform.

InesYou want me to stop the only road this town has on the strength of a theory about sound. I was on the pass in February with a shovel. I know what it costs when the road is shut. Show me the sound.

IlluminaraI'm not asking you to stop it. I'm asking you to let me show you, and to write down first what would prove me wrong.

InesWritten where?

IlluminaraOn a card, signed by the three of us, before anyone hears a word.

InesFine. And I run my own sweep on my own instrument. I don't take anyone's ears for a frequency, yours least of all.

She carried a spectrum trace to the bench and held it under the lamp. Two spikes stood in it, narrow and clean, at three-oh-nine and six-eighteen.

InesA peak is not a child.

IlluminaraNo. That's the other half. At the noon rest the car is held for the rope check, and for twenty minutes there is no note. I'd like to play the same words in both halves, and see who hears what.

InesTwenty minutes is all you get. Not twenty-one.

04 · The Noon Rest

The school hall · Tuesday, two minutes before noon

The card had been signed the night before by three people and sealed in an envelope on the piano. Illuminara read it aloud, so that nobody could say they had not heard its terms.

Eight listeners, in two panels of four, in two rooms. The twenty-four recordings, twelve children's and twelve adults' saying the same words, are split into Set A and Set B, six children and six adults in each. Panel One hears Set A with the drive running, before noon, and Set B with it stopped. Panel Two hears Set A with it stopped, and Set B with it running, after the rest. So every voice is judged by four listeners with the drive running and by four others with it stopped, and nobody hears a voice twice. A voice is misheard in a condition when three of its four listeners write a word other than the one its speaker meant, as the parent who asked has written on the back. If, with the drive running, more than three of the twelve children's voices are misheard, and with the drive stopped no more than one, and the adults are heard correctly both ways, we call it masking. If not, Illuminara says so and drops it. Dr Orsolya adds: this does not rule out fear. Ines adds: nothing here touches the car.

Fourteen families had been asked for a voice note from the spring, before the car, and each child had been asked first. Two had said no and had not been asked twice. The twelve who agreed sat in the envelope beside twelve adults saying the same words. Nessa had told her mother, in the back room, that her yes-to-the-dog could be played once, and she sat at the back of the hall with her arms folded. Nobody had asked her to say a word.

Six parents, Ines and Dr Orsolya sat with slips of paper: Panel One in the hall, Panel Two in the little room across the passage, a speaker in each. Illuminara did not vote. She drew the order of each set from a hat, and every listener wrote river, sorrow or yes.

Panel One's first block ran with the car running. At noon the cabin stopped at the platform with a long sigh, and the note went out of the floor like a held breath let go. Illuminara felt it go in her own breastbone. If the slips come back flat, she thought, I have put forty children's week on a hunch. For the twenty minutes of the rest both rooms listened at once, Panel One to Set B and Panel Two to Set A.

At twenty past twelve the car moved off and the note came back into the floor, and Panel Two began its running block, Set B. She counted the slips twice, with Ines and Dr Orsolya at her shoulders, and read the results in the order she had promised.

IlluminaraChildren, drive running: seven of twelve voices misheard. Children, drive stopped: none. Adults, running: none. Adults, stopped: none.

Nobody spoke. A cabin crossed the window and went on up the mountain.

InesOrder.

IlluminaraPanel One heard the car running first and then stopped, Panel Two the other way round. Each set was heard both ways, by different people in opposite order, and each panel's own slips show the same split: the running block misheard, the stopped block clean. Here are their sheets.

She laid them on the piano beside the envelope. Ines read them the way she would have read a weld.

Dr OrsolyaSeven. And none. Then I have spent four months listening to forty children through the wrong wall.

IlluminaraOr through a wall that nobody knew was there.

Dr OrsolyaI would like that to be a comfort. It is not yet. This does not tell me whether they are also afraid.

IlluminaraNo. We can't know until the wall is gone. Then you'll hear what is left, and you'll be the one to answer it.

Dr OrsolyaThen take it down. I will find out what is left.

Ines had filled in her own sheet like everyone else, and was now staring at her own trace.

InesThree-oh-nine. I called that note smooth for two years. I tuned that gearbox with grown-up ears.

She folded the paper in four.

InesI'll fix it. Don't thank me.

05 · Four Percent

The upper station, machine hall · Wednesday night, ten past ten

Ines had asked the town for the night. Eleven hours without its one road was the price, and she had named that first. She worked with the car held from the last evening run until nine in the morning and a crew of five, and she explained as she went, because she had decided that the town would not be mended by anything it could not follow.

InesThree things. An isolation mount under the gearbox, so the rock stops carrying the note up the ridge. A tuned absorber on the casing, for the note itself. And four percent off cruise speed, because the school floor is a drum, it rings at three-ten, and I'd like the note to arrive where the drum isn't.

IlluminaraWhat does four percent cost?

InesA ride is thirty-eight minutes. Four percent is a minute and a half. You get that and not a second more.

InesAnd here are my limits, so nobody wonders. Tension, brake and rope I don't touch, for you, for the board or for God. Mounts and speed are mine. And here is what puts the old mount back: the coupling outside the maker's alignment limit, the gearbox shaking harder than it did last year, or the car taking one metre longer to stop. Any one of the three and the rigid mount goes back tonight, and you can all be cross with me.

IlluminaraI wouldn't want you to do anything else.

At six the sweeps ran, hers and the valley inspector's. He had driven up in the night with a thermos and a distrust of enthusiasm, and he had come to check that the repair had not touched what it must not touch. The two traces lay one on the other like a signature and its copy. The note at the school wall was down by twenty-six decibels, and nobody could find it in the floor.

Then came the part nobody would put in a story. The coupling sat inside its limit. The gearbox shook less than it had the year before. At seven the car ran up empty and came down empty. At eight Ines ran the emergency stop from full cruise with a cabin of sandbags, and the inspector, who had expected to find fault, wrote the stopping distance down and looked at it as though it owed him something: inside the certificate, with half a metre to spare. At nine the first loaded cabin went up, forty crates of bread, Sabrina Vedd's. She stood at the lower station with her arms folded and watched it go as if it had been rude to her bread. Ines signed the commissioning sheet under the inspector's name, and not before him.

On Friday the board met in the hall. Sabrina Vedd spoke for four minutes, which was one more than she had been given. Dr Orsolya stood after her with a letter, and read it in the same dry, careful voice in which he had asked for a forty-first fact.

Dr OrsolyaI diagnosed forty children with what I could not hear. I withdraw my recommendation of the transfer, and the sentence in the draft that calls it a placement. The offer of observation at Dessau stands for any family that asks for it, in any month, and no child goes on a notice of mine. If in three weeks a family still wants it, the request is theirs to make, and I will sign it.

The board voted to shelve the transfer and to keep the Dessau offer for any family that asked. It was not unanimous and nobody pretended it was. Two members thought the doctor had given way too fast, said so aloud, and were thanked for it.

06 · A Little Hill

The little room, High Wendel school · three weeks later, the first snow

The snow came down without wind, and the cabin crossed it, red and slow, humming in a register that had dropped into the walls of the world.

The teacher asked again whether anyone would like the window opened. A girl in the second row raised her chin.

Nessa said li. It rose, small and bright, a little hill, and Illuminara, who sat at the back with her hearing turned all the way up and her hands in her lap, discovered that she had stopped breathing.

Daan opened the window. The cold came in, and the sound of the car, and a boy at the back said li with a fall in it that was unmistakably a complaint about the cold. The room laughed. Nobody laughed at a river.

Thirty-eight of the forty were speaking by then, at school and at home, loudly and sloppily and without pause. Two were not. For Wilma and Teo the silence had a second root, as Dr Orsolya had said it might, and the wall had been hiding it. Now that the channel was clean he could hear it, and he went to the little room on Tuesdays and sat with them and asked them for nothing. Dessau stood in the minutes as an offer. Wilma's mother had asked to see the place, and had come home and chosen Tuesdays instead; Teo's parents had not asked. Nobody called it failure.

At the gate Sabrina Vedd was waiting in her freight-shed jacket. Nessa, who had been talking for forty minutes without drawing breath, stopped in the middle of a sentence about a bucket.

NessaLi.

It was a little hill. Sabrina said li back, badly, in the wrong register, and her daughter corrected her all the way down the road.

Illuminara watched from the gate. The cabin crossed above them, humming low, in a register no six-year-old needed, and a hill, a small one, went on up and down the road in the snow.
