01 · The Form for Rain

Harrow Landing school, recording room · the first day, 09:10

The recording room smelled of warm dust and new foam, and Sefa Dray played the old woman's voice before Illuminara had her coat off. It was a good voice, dry and patient, eleven syllables to a breath, and it said something about a kettle. Illuminara had spent most of her working life standing between two people who shared no word, and she knew the sound of a sentence that was not being said to anybody. This was one. Every verb in it ended on the same soft closing vowel, as if the old woman were reading out the weather.

Sefa DrayOrrel Tane, five years ago. Forty hours, and it is the last clean set we have. Since then they sit with us and talk, but not into the microphone. I have ten speakers, an average age of eighty-one, and a grant that ends in six weeks.

IlluminaraWhy does every verb end the same way?

Sefa DrayDefault ending, the linguist said. Aru has three. That is the plain one.

A hand reached past Illuminara's shoulder and pressed the pad on the machine. The kettle went silent in the middle of its boiling. A short, heavy woman in a green cardigan stood there with a jar held against her chest like an infant.

Ilu MarwenThat is the ending we use for rain, and for the dead. Orrel Tane is not dead. She sat in that chair for forty hours and spoke to a box as if the box were a funeral.

Sefa DrayShe agreed to it, Ilu.

Ilu MarwenShe agreed to a grant. Ask her again, in Aru, and see what verb she answers with.

Sefa Dray opened her mouth, shut it, and looked at the floor, which Illuminara counted in her favour.

IlluminaraYou are Ilu Marwen. You have refused the board four times.

Ilu MarwenFive, counting this one. And you are the interpreter, the one who is to make the machine work, which means you will want to learn us so that we can be kept.

IlluminaraI was asked to make the recording work. I would like to know what it is for before I decide whether it can.

Ilu MarwenYou want to learn it so it can be kept. We want it learned so that someone can be asked for something in it. Those are not the same task, and the board has only ever paid for the first.

Sefa DrayBecause the first is the only one that outlasts you. When the last of you goes, a kettle in a file is what is left.

Ilu turned to her without any heat at all.

Ilu MarwenYes. That is a good fear. I would have it too, in your chair.

Three endings, Illuminara thought. One for rain and the dead. What are the other two for?

IlluminaraTell me what the other two are.

Ilu MarwenI will, if you ask properly.

IlluminaraHow do I ask properly?

Ilu unscrewed the jar, took a spoon from her cardigan pocket as if she had been carrying it for years against exactly this afternoon, and held out a heap of dark jam that smelled of smoke and berries.

02 · A Spoon of Jam

Ilu Marwen's kitchen, Harrow Landing · the same afternoon, 15:30

The kitchen had one window, and the river ran past it, brown and low. By the door stood a blue enamel jar, empty to the bottom, and a bucket with a rope handle. Illuminara ate the jam and it was the best thing she had tasted on Teshun, and she said so, in the common speech, and Ilu shook her head as though a plate had been dropped.

Ilu MarwenIn Aru you cannot take a thing without the verb saying who owes. You may not say it was good and leave it there. Say it again, and mean the weight.

IlluminaraThen teach me the verb.

Ilu MarwenTevma. I ask, and I owe the asking. It ends in the first of the two endings, the one that points at the speaker. Say it.

Illuminara said it. Her tongue had no trouble. Ilu listened with her head to one side, the way a woman listens to a pan she suspects of being cracked.

Ilu MarwenIt is a word. It has no weight. You said it as if nobody was owed.

IlluminaraI can learn every word the board recorded by tonight, Ilu. I can say it all perfectly. I do not think that is what you mean.

Ilu MarwenNo. You could say the whole forty hours and not once be speaking. Perfect Aru with nobody in it is the rain ending in a good coat.

Illuminara put the spoon down. She had been given things all her life, power and insight and the long attention of a great many people, and she could not remember the last time she had been handed something and been unable to give it back in the same minute.

IlluminaraWhat do I owe you?

Ilu MarwenThe jar by the door is empty. The well is at the bottom of the lane, four hundred paces. Fill the jar by hand.

By hand. Illuminara looked at the bucket, and then, for the length of one breath, at the well she could not see, and Ilu saw her do it.

Ilu MarwenI have watched you look at pipes. Do not make the water come. I would know the difference by the taste.

So she walked the lane with a bucket. The rope bit her palm on the second trip, and by the fourth she had forgotten to be clever; the word went with her, tevma, tevma, in time with the water slopping on her boots, and somewhere around the sixth bucket it stopped being a word and became the weight in her arm. On the eleventh trip she emptied the bucket into the blue jar and the jar was full, and her shoulders ached in a plain animal way she found she was glad of.

Ilu MarwenNow ask me.

IlluminaraTevma. I ask what the other two endings are for, and I owe the asking.

Ilu closed her eyes for a moment, and then answered in a voice that had changed shape, lower and rounder, a voice that had stepped across a threshold.

Ilu MarwenTevsa. You asked, and I owe you the answer. The second ending points at the other person. Ma, I owe you. Sa, you are owed. And the third is the one the board has. O. Nobody owes anybody. Rain, and the dead.

The river went on past the window. Illuminara found she was smiling like a person who has been let into a house.

IlluminaraThen the grammar is a ledger.

Ilu MarwenIt is a ledger that can talk. That is the only reason it has lasted ten thousand years on a river that floods.

03 · Forty Cents a Word

Harrow Landing school, side corridor · the third day, 18:40

Teo Marwen was nineteen and waited at the end of the corridor with a satchel and the look of a person with an appointment he did not want. He was Ilu's great-nephew, and he had been the board's best reader for two years.

Teo MarwenYou are the one Aunt Ilu fed. Everyone in the lane knows. She has not fed anyone since my grandmother died.

IlluminaraI carried eleven buckets for a spoon of jam.

Teo MarwenThen you already do it better than I do. I get forty cents a word. I read the lists into the machine, six hours a day. I am very good. The linguist says I have the cleanest vowels on the river.

IlluminaraAnd when you speak Aru to your aunt?

He did not answer for long enough that the corridor lights buzzed and clicked off on their timer. Neither of them moved to wake them.

Teo MarwenShe answers me with the rain ending. Every time. I thought I was bad at it. I thought there was a part of the language that you had to be born into and I had missed it, and that I was the one who had taken the money for something I could not even do.

Illuminara could see a shape then, the way a floor plan comes clear when someone opens the last door. It did not take the languages of forty courts to see it. It took a nineteen-year-old with clean vowels and a pay slip.

IlluminaraTeo. You are not bad at it. You are excellent. Here is what I think, and I can be wrong about it: the board pays you for every word, so every word you say is already settled. Nobody owes anybody, and the verb has nowhere to put the weight. Your aunt may not be rejecting your Aru. She may be answering the only grammar you have given her to answer.

Teo MarwenThe money cancels it?

IlluminaraIf I am right, every debt, before it is made. I would not bet your aunt's kitchen on it yet.

He sat down on the floor with the satchel in his lap. It was a long time before he spoke, and when he did, it was in the common speech, very low.

Teo MarwenBashe Corrin will not speak to me at all since I started. She is ninety-one. She taught my mother. She turned her chair to the wall when I came in last spring.

IlluminaraShe may not think you are bad at it. She may think you have been bought.

Teo MarwenWhat do I owe her, then?

IlluminaraThat is for her to say, and for you to ask. I only know that it cannot be money, and that it must be something she may refuse.

She did not tell him that the thing in his satchel, the forty hours of his own flawless reading, had frightened her more than the empty river had. If she was right, the unowed Aru was complete. A thousand patient hours could keep it exactly as it was, a language laid out like a body for the funeral.

04 · The Arithmetic

Board office, Harrow Landing · the ninth day, 10:00

Sefa Dray had made tea in two mugs and put the sugar between them like a treaty. Her desk calendar had five weeks left on it, and the last week was marked in red by someone whose handwriting looked like her own.

Sefa DrayI will say it plainly, so that you cannot say I hid it. Hale Orne has a heart that the clinic gives four months. Bashe Corrin is ninety-one. In five weeks one of them may be a name on a stone, and if the board has not recorded what she carries, nothing of it exists. I am not afraid of kitchens. I am afraid of a grammar lesson going down a hole with her.

IlluminaraYou are right to be afraid of it.

Sefa DrayThen why am I being asked to bet the last ten voices on a custom about jam?

It was the strongest thing anyone had said to her on Teshun, and Illuminara let it stand a moment before she answered.

IlluminaraYou are not being asked to bet them. You are being asked not to spend them. The recording as it stands is fluent and dead, and a hundred hours more only makes a longer corpse. What I can offer is smaller and less safe: young people who owe old people something real, and a language that has somewhere to live.

Sefa DrayAnd who pays them?

IlluminaraNobody pays them. Pay the learner and the debt is cancelled. But some things must not depend on a young person's debt at all. Nursing. Winter fuel. The clinic run. Those the board pays for, to the elders directly, whether any apprentice stays or goes, so that no child is ever the only thing between an old woman and the cold. What the apprentices owe is what only a person can owe: time, errands, being there, the carrying.

Sefa looked at the calendar. She was not stupid, and she was not unkind. She was doing the arithmetic she was paid to do.

Sefa DrayNow let me be unkind, since you are being kind to me. Anselm Voy wrote the grammar, twenty years on this river. Voy never heard debt in those endings. Voy heard the room. Rain and the dead are said out of doors to the whole lane; ma and sa at a table with the door shut. Who is listening, how formal the place is. Teo's trouble is that a microphone is a room with no one in it. Pay him or do not, it changes nothing, and you have built my winter on jam.

IlluminaraIt is a good account. It fits everything I have heard as well as mine does.

Sefa DrayThen you do not know.

IlluminaraI do not know. And you have just told me how to find out. Voy and I expect different things. If it is the room, then a formal, public, witnessed exchange will bring out ma and sa even when nothing is owed. If it is the owing, a plain private favour will, and the public, paid one will not. The paid one must be finished before a word is said: the thing handed over, the money in the hand, nothing left to return and nothing left to do. The favour must leave exactly one duty, taken freely. The same two speakers, the same new sentence, one that is not in the forty hours. Ilu and Hale Orne listen from the next room and say which ending they hear. Not me. I have told Ilu what I think; Hale has not heard it, and she speaks first.

Sefa Dray found an envelope in her drawer, wrote on a slip of paper, and sealed it with the flat of her thumb. Illuminara wrote hers on the back of a bus ticket and handed it over to be kept.

Sefa DrayIf it falls Voy's way, there is no programme. I record, and you go home.

IlluminaraYes.

Sefa DrayAnd if it falls yours, I still need something that can fail in public. I will not fund a feeling.

IlluminaraThen name the test.

Sefa DrayBy first frost, in five weeks. At least six elder and apprentice pairs, speaking Aru in the owed endings, witnessed by someone who is not you. If it is fewer, the money returns to recording and I do not apologise for it.

IlluminaraThat is fair. They may also refuse me entirely. I have not been accepted yet.

Sefa DrayI know. I asked Ilu this morning whether you were any good, and she said you had eleven buckets of water in you and had not spilled a drop on her floor.

05 · Behind the Blanket

Hale Orne's front room, Harrow Landing · the tenth day, 17:00

Hale Orne lay on three pillows with her feet against a hot-water bottle and the face of a woman who had made her peace with dying and not with being bored. A grey blanket hung across the kitchen doorway on a length of clothesline. Ilu sat beside the bed with her knitting in her lap, not knitting. Illuminara had the floor by the door. On the sideboard lay a sealed envelope and a bus ticket, and from behind the blanket came the sounds of two people trying not to scrape their chairs.

Hale OrneFour months, the clinic says. I will give you this afternoon. Do not make me hear anything twice.

Ilu MarwenYou will hear what you hear.

Sefa had tossed a coin in the yard to decide the order, and had told no one how it fell.

A bell rang in the kitchen. Someone read out terms in the flat voice of a person reading a receipt: a coat, sold outright to Teo Marwen, forty cents, paid and handed over; nothing kept back and nothing to bring back. A pen scratched. At the door a third person coughed, the school caretaker, who had been brought to stand witness and had been told to say nothing. Then Teo's voice came through the blanket, the clean vowels of the best reader on the river, saying a sentence that was in none of the forty hours.

You put the coat in my hands. Every verb in it closed on the same soft vowel.

Hale OrneRain.

Ilu MarwenRain. A man at a market.

The caretaker's boots went out and the door shut. The light under the blanket changed to a lamp's. Something was said too low to hear, and a long quiet followed, and then Teo again, with the same sentence in the same clean voice, and it was not the same sentence. The verb in its middle leaned toward the other person and stayed there. After it, a half-breath late, like a foot finding a stair that is not there, Sefa's voice answered.

Hale OrneMa. And she gave him the sa, though she was slow with it. He is frightened he will break it.

Ilu MarwenHe is frightened for the right reason.

The blanket was lifted aside. Teo came through with Sefa behind him, and Sefa took the envelope from the sideboard and opened it with a thumbnail, as if it might bite.

Sefa DrayI wrote: both of them ma and sa. The room does it. The bell, the receipt, the witness. I wrote Voy.

IlluminaraMine says the first would be rain and the second ma. I did not see the order. I only wrote what I would be wrong about.

Hale OrneYou wrote the failing guess down. Nobody ever does that.

Ilu MarwenVoy was not stupid, Sefa. Listen. A debt wants somebody to hear it closed. That is why we have the lane. Voy heard the closing, and the closing wants a witness, and he took it for the owing.

Sefa DrayThen the room matters.

Ilu MarwenFor the closing. Not for the owing.

IlluminaraTeo. What happened in the kitchen, the second time?

Teo MarwenShe held the coat out and said keep it till you are warm, and she looked at the window while I decided.

IlluminaraWhich meant you had to bring it back.

Teo MarwenTomorrow, washed. I said I would, and I meant it.

Hale OrneShe looked at the window. She left you the door.

Teo MarwenI could have said no.

Hale OrneThere. That is what I would have told your Voy, if he had asked an old woman instead of a book. The first coat was bought and finished, and nobody owed anybody for it. The second was lent, and you took on one duty by your own say-so. Had she stood over you and said take it, you would owe her nothing at all. A thing forced is nobody's. We have the rain ending for that too. You cannot owe what you could not refuse.

Ilu MarwenThat is the part I had not said aloud.

Illuminara found that she had stood up without meaning to. The design she had carried into Sefa's office had one more beam under it than she had known, and it was this one.

IlluminaraThen nobody can be assigned. Not by the board, not by me, not by an elder's need. Every morning is asked again, and either of them may say no.

Hale OrneThen you will lose some.

IlluminaraYes.

Hale OrneGood. That is how you will know the rest are real. Now go away, all of you. A girl is coming to read to me and I want to look as though I have been resting.

06 · Two Refusals

Harrow Landing school hall, a canteen table pulled to the middle · the twelfth day, 19:00

There were nine chairs at the table and two of them were empty, because Hale Orne was in bed and Orrel Tane had sent word that she had said all she meant to say to the board. Bashe Corrin sat at the head in a shawl with her hands folded on the table and her chair turned a quarter away from Teo, who stood by the wall with his satchel, and she had not looked at Illuminara once. Ilu sat beside her, and Sefa Dray sat at the foot with a folder she had not opened.

Illuminara laid out the design as plainly as she could, with what Hale and Ilu had heard on the tenth day: the endings came only where each party could have said no, so no one would be assigned and every morning would be asked again. Then she waited, because what came next was not hers to hurry.

Bashe CorrinMy brother owed a man in the old camp. Eleven years, for a roof. The man never said the closing word and my brother never got to say it for him. He died owing. He was owed for thirty years by a man who would not close it. A debt is also a chain, girl. You would teach children to owe us, and when we are dead who opens the door?

The room did not move. It was the real thing and everyone in it knew it. Illuminara had no clever answer and did not look for one.

IlluminaraYou are right. A debt with no end is a chain. Everything I have said is wrong if the one who owes cannot leave.

Ilu MarwenThe closing word is the creditor's. Aru has no word for the debtor to close his own.

IlluminaraThen the creditor says it first. Before the first errand, the elder says the closing word aloud, in front of a witness from the lane, and the witness keeps it. How long, what for, and the word that closes it, all spoken at the start. The learner may call the witness on any morning and ask for the closing, and it is said, and the debt is closed, whether the elder is fond of them or angry or asleep. The elder may call it too. The learner may leave in the season they choose. The elder may send them off.

Ilu MarwenA closing said before there is anything to close. That is not in the forty hours.

Bashe CorrinThen it is not a chain. A chain has no key in the debtor's pocket.

Ilu MarwenIt may be bad Aru. We shall see what the lane does with it.

Bashe CorrinWritten words are the board's. Aloud.

IlluminaraAloud. Never written.

Bashe Corrin considered this with her mouth very small. At last she unfolded one hand and laid it flat.

Bashe CorrinThat is a better rule than the one my brother had. It is not for me. I will not owe a child and I will not be owed by one. Put my name in no kitchen.

IlluminaraI will not.

Bashe CorrinIf anyone wishes to learn from me, they may sit at Ilu's table and listen. They will not ask me for anything.

Ilu did not argue. She had waited sixty years for this woman to say a thing she could not argue with. Instead she turned toward the foot of the table.

Ilu MarwenAnd your machine, Sefa Dray. You still want it.

Sefa DrayYes. With whatever any of you will give.

Ilu MarwenThen you ask. And whoever asks, owes.

Sefa DrayThe board cannot owe a person, Ilu. It can only be accountable to the funder.

IlluminaraThen it can write the debt into its own charter and read it aloud at this table. A recording given by any speaker makes the board the debtor. The archive is never sold, never used to teach anyone who has not first owed an elder, and stands only as long as the apprenticeships stay free.

Sefa looked at the folder. She picked up her pen and put it down, and did a thing Illuminara had not asked of her. She turned her chair to face the old woman.

Sefa DrayTevma. I ask for the voices, and I owe the asking.

Ilu MarwenBadly. Again.

Sefa DrayTevma.

Ilu MarwenBetter. Tevsa. You are owed the answer, and the answer is yes, from five of us. Not from Bashe. Not from Orrel. And Hale will answer for herself.

At the door, afterward, a thin hand closed on Illuminara's sleeve. Bashe Corrin had not asked anyone to help her with her stick.

Bashe CorrinYou carried Ilu's water. I counted eleven buckets from my window. Nobody does that for a tourist.

IlluminaraI was not being a tourist.

Bashe CorrinI know. I will tell you one thing, and you will not owe me for it. It is the sound a river makes under ice. It has no word in the common speech. We call it the glass-talking.

IlluminaraTevma, Bashe Corrin. I owe you that.

Bashe CorrinI did not ask you to.

IlluminaraNo. It is mine to say.

Bashe CorrinThen hear this too, and you will not owe me for it either. That boy stood against the wall for an hour tonight with his bag on his shoulder, and nobody stands an hour who is only paid. When I am in the ground, if he asks for the glass-talking, and asks as if it weighed, you may give it to him. I will not hear him say it. I am deciding it now so that nobody has to guess at my burial.

IlluminaraI will give it to him if he asks like that, and not otherwise.

Bashe CorrinNot otherwise.

07 · The Fifth Afternoon

Hale Orne's front room · the fifth week, a Thursday, 15:00

Merri had been sixteen for a month when she began reading to Hale Orne in the owed endings, mornings, before school. In the third week she told Hale that mornings would not do, and Hale said afternoons or nothing, and Merri said afternoons. In the fourth, with her examinations coming, she asked the neighbour from number nine to come and say the closing word. Hale, who was fond of her and had every reason to hold on, could have said no. She said: I would rather you stayed. Merri said: I know, I am asking. The neighbour said the word that Hale had spoken into her keeping on the first day, and the debt was closed, and the lane heard it, and the nurse came on Thursday as before, because the board paid the nurse and Merri had never been the only thing between Hale and the cold.

On the fifth Thursday Illuminara came to Hale's porch with a loaf from Ilu and did not go in, because she could hear the front door open and a chair drawn up, and the screen was thin.

Hale OrneThe term is closed, girl. I heard the word said. You do not owe me a Thursday.

MerriI know. Nobody does.

There was a pause, and a chair, and then Merri said something in Aru that made Illuminara put the loaf down on the step. It used the closing vowel of the rain ending, the one that said nobody owes, but it leaned on it the way the first ending leans, toward the speaker, so that the sentence meant at once that nothing was owed and that she had come, and that she wanted the old woman to know she knew the difference.

Hale Orne laughed until she coughed.

Hale OrneSeventy years, and nobody has said that to me. That is neither one thing nor the other. Ilu will call it vile grammar and then say it at supper.

MerriIs it allowed?

Hale OrneI have no idea. I have not been asked for a ruling. Read to me. Not the lists. The one with the dog.

Illuminara listened to the paper turn. Hale Orne died on the Sunday of that week, with the girl in the chair and the book open on the quilt, and the closing word was spoken at the bedside by the old woman herself in her last clear hour. It was not for the term, which had ended. It was the one she kept for what she had been given.

08 · First Frost

Ilu Marwen's kitchen · the sixth week, first frost, 07:20

The window had frosted at its corners and the river was black behind it. In the lane outside there were people carrying things. Illuminara counted them as she came up the path, which she had not planned to do, and stopped at seven: a boy with a basket of split kindling, a girl with an old man's shopping, two teenagers with a handcart of stove-wood, and three more she did not know. None of them was carrying a recorder.

Teo was in the kitchen already, kneeling at the stove with the ash-pan. He had been there every dawn for four weeks. He and Ilu had spoken the terms aloud on the first morning in front of the neighbour from number nine, who had stood holding her coat and cried quite openly: fourteen weeks, the stove and the water and the lane errands, and the closing word held in her keeping. In the third week he had told Ilu he could not do the lane errands and the stove both with his lessons, and Ilu had said the stove, then, and the neighbour had come again to hear the new term said and cried again, as far as anyone could tell about everything. He had a bandage on one thumb, where the poker had caught him.

Teo MarwenTevma, Aunt. I ask you for the morning words, and I owe you the asking, and I owe you for the day I let a machine hear me and not you.

Ilu stopped with the kettle in the air.

Ilu MarwenSay that last again.

Teo MarwenI owe you for the day I let a machine hear me and not you.

She put the kettle down. It was a sentence nobody in the room had ever heard, because in ten thousand years nobody on the river had needed to apologise to a machine. Teo had taken the owed ending, which was made for jam and water, and bent it, with fear and a little arrogance, to hold something the language had never carried. It did not break.

Ilu MarwenThat is not in the forty hours.

Teo MarwenI know. I made it.

Ilu MarwenTevsa. You are owed the answer. It is a good sentence. It is very bad grammar, and I will tell you why, and it will take all morning.

Illuminara stood in the doorway with the cold at her back and did not go in. It was not hers. She had said the first word in this house and it was not necessary that she be in the room for the thousandth.

A car had stopped at the gate. Sefa Dray got out of it in yesterday's clothes with the folder under her arm, and came up the path with the walk of someone who has been awake all night and decided against sitting down.

Sefa DrayI came in person, because it is not a thing to say to a machine. The panel met last night. They would approve a second year if the archive were opened to visiting scholars from January. I said no sale, no extraction, and nobody taught from it who has not first owed an elder. They said, then no equipment line. I said, then no equipment line.

IlluminaraAnd the winter.

Sefa DrayIs in the board's charter, read aloud by me in the school hall with half the lane standing round it, and the panel cannot reach it. Nursing, fuel and the clinic run are paid to the elders directly, whether any apprentice stays or goes. I read the clause to the panel as well. Aloud. They did not like it. It is in the minutes.

IlluminaraWhat did it cost?

Sefa DrayA year of the good microphones.

She did not sound sorry. She told the rest standing in the frost, while the kettle began again behind her: seven pairs by first frost, witnessed by the neighbour from number nine, who was not Illuminara.
