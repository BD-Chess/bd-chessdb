What Must Be Allowed to Fall

A hillside begins moving above a neighbourhood. Nara can hold part of it for a few minutes—but temporary protection starts to look like a promise that everyone may stay. To save the people, she must admit what her light cannot preserve, let their homes fall and allow others to decide what leaving will mean.

Act I

The Hill Moves

A local physical failure becomes a public promise faster than Nara can name its limits.

01

The Glass Walks

Kavera Ridge Community Clinic · 06:12 · nine days after CIVIC-9

The first sign that the hill was moving was that the water in Kelechi’s cup walked uphill.

He had set it on the clinic counter beside three cartons of insulin, a roll of cold-chain labels and the breakfast he had been trying to make Nara eat since Boroko. The cup trembled once. Then it slid six centimetres toward the wall.

Kelechi caught it before it fell.

“Your powers are getting petty,” he said.

Nara did not answer.

The clinic floor had a hairline crack beneath the counter. A bead of water climbed out of it. Not leaked. Climbed—pushed by pressure below.

Outside, rain pressed the hillside settlement into blue-grey layers: corrugated roofs, garden tarps, water tanks, footpaths cut into clay. Kavera Ridge had grown by addition rather than design. One room, then another. One concrete step where a dirt step had failed. One family making space for the next.

The crack moved.

A sound passed under the building like a heavy drawer opening.

Nara put both hands flat on the counter.

“Get Sefa.”

Kelechi had learned the difference between her quiet voice and her ordinary one. He was already moving when the front retaining wall bowed inward.

The waiting room tilted.

A metal chair slid across the tiles. An oxygen cylinder rolled off its stand, regulator first, toward an old man’s ankle. Nara caught the collar with one foot and lowered it before the valve struck concrete.

That was the first thing she saved.

Not the clinic. Not the hill.

One valve that could have become a missile.

Nurse Sefa came through the treatment-room door with a child on her hip and disbelief already turning into procedure.

“What moved?”

“Everything,” Kelechi said.

The rear stair gave a metallic cry.

Three patients were on it: a pregnant woman, a boy carrying a box of dressings and Miri Soro, the clinic committee chair, who had come to complain about a delivery and had stayed to help unload it.

The upper landing pulled away from the wall.

Gold opened beneath Nara’s skin.

She did not reach for the whole building. The temptation arrived anyway—a bright diagram of roof, walls, foundations, people, every load asking to become hers.

She chose the landing.

Thin lines crossed the stair frame, found two concrete columns and rejected the cracked retaining wall. Metal stopped dropping. The pregnant woman froze halfway down.

“Do not look at me,” Nara said. “Look at the next step.”

Miri put one hand beneath the woman’s elbow.

“You heard her. One step.”

The boy went first. Then the woman. Miri came last, carrying the dressings against her chest.

The instant her foot touched the path, Nara released the landing.

It fell half a metre and folded against the wall.

The gold vanished.

Sefa stared at the broken stair, then at Nara.

“How long could you have held it?”

“Eighteen seconds.”

“You counted?”

“My body did.”

A second crack opened through the pharmacy floor.

Nara pointed to the laundry door at the uphill side.

“Which path does not cross the retaining wall?”

Sefa answered immediately. “Through the wash court, behind the church.”

“Then we move people, insulin, antibiotics and oxygen. In that order unless a patient changes it.”

Kelechi lifted the cold box.

“Breakfast?”

“Fifth priority.”

“Abuse of authority.”

“No authority. Only triage.”

They left the clinic through the wash court as the front windows cracked in a single horizontal line.

At the path, Miri turned back toward the building.

Nara caught her arm only long enough to stop the first step. Then she let go.

“What is still inside?”

“Registers. Land copies. My father’s radio.”

“People?”

“No.”

“Then not yet.”

Miri looked at the gold fading from Nara’s wrist.

“You held it.”

“One stair,” Nara said. “For eighteen seconds.”

Above them, a water tank leaned and did not return upright.

02

No One Leaves a House Twice

Kavera Ridge lower junction · 06:31

By the time Municipal Resilience arrived, the hill had drawn a dark line through the neighbourhood.

It crossed a garden, split a concrete drain, passed beneath three houses and opened again in the road wide enough to take a hand. Rainwater disappeared into it without reaching the bottom.

Lani Vagi stepped from the first vehicle carrying a radio and the expression of someone who had been given responsibility faster than information. Kila came behind her in a rain shell, one hand holding yellow barrier tape and the other held away from the holster he no longer wore when Nara was present.

Adaeze arrived in a survey truck with two engineers and a case of instruments. She looked once at Nara, once at the clinic and then at the slope.

“How much light?” she asked.

“One landing. Eighteen seconds.”

“Any colour loss?”

“No.”

“Any delay between seeing and hearing?”

“No.”

“Good. Keep it that way.”

Lani raised her radio.

“Mandatory evacuation from the crown crack to the lower junction. Buses will take residents to Kone Transit Centre. Police assistance is requested for refusals.”

Nobody moved toward the buses.

Instead, doors opened uphill. People came out carrying document folders, cooking pots, school bags, framed photographs, sacks of rice and the flat, watchful faces of those who had heard the word temporary before.

Miri stepped onto the road between Lani and the first bus.

“Kone is outside the ward.”

“It is above the flood line and has sanitation.”

“It also has a fence.”

“It is a shelter.”

“That is what they called it after the Mavara fire. Six families never came back.”

Lani looked at the widening crack.

“A dead family keeps no address.”

Miri did not flinch.

“And a living family without an address becomes easy to move again.”

At the bottom of the road, three yellow excavators waited on transport trailers. Their company name had been painted over with white rectangles that were not quite opaque.

Miri pointed.

“You brought the machines before the blankets.”

“They are city contractors for debris clearance.”

“They are Harbour Crown machines. Harbour Crown has wanted this ridge cleared for four years.”

Lani turned to one of her staff. “Are they?”

The staff member did not answer quickly enough.

A deep pop came from uphill. One of the rain tanks rolled ten centimetres downhill and stopped against a house.

Adaeze drove a steel rod into the road beside the crack. The rod leaned before she released it.

“This is not a retaining-wall failure,” she said. “The crown is rotating. We need the old borehole data.”

One of the engineers opened a tablet.

“The inclinometer at K-4 is still reporting.”

“How fast?”

“Eight millimetres a minute.”

“Trend?”

“Accelerating.”

Miri heard it.

“What does that mean in time?”

Adaeze did not soften the answer.

“It means I cannot tell you whether the large movement begins in six minutes or forty. I can tell you that the hill has already chosen to move.”

The residents looked at Nara.

Not at Lani. Not at Adaeze’s instruments.

At the woman whose light had held the clinic stair.

Nara felt the shape of their question before anyone spoke it. That did not make the answer hers.

Miri asked it.

“Can you hold it?”

Nara looked at the crack. At the houses resting on wet ground. At the buses. At the excavators. At Kelechi carrying insulin past people who were trying to decide whether a cooler mattered more than a title deed.

Nine days earlier she had trusted a coherent answer from a municipal system and moved water toward the wrong gate. A responder’s ankle had broken. Medicines had been destroyed. The record still carried her name.

Connection was not knowledge.

She turned to Adaeze.

“What can we map in three minutes?”

“The clinic route, the crown crack and the north stair. Not the deep slide.”

“What can we verify?”

“That the north stair is presently the safest path down. That the clinic terrace will fail before the rock spur. That any hold you create will store load somewhere else.”

Nara looked at Miri.

“I cannot hold the hill. I may be able to keep the north route open while people leave.”

“For how long?”

“Twenty minutes if the measured movement stays within the current range.”

“And the houses?”

Nara should have answered only that question.

Instead she looked at the families in the rain and tried to make the limit bearable.

“I can hold the movement long enough for everyone to take what matters and get out.”

Phones lifted.

Miri searched her face.

“Twenty minutes?”

“Twenty. Not safety. Time.”

Miri nodded once.

“Then give us time.”

The first bargain was made.

03

Twenty Minutes Is Not Forever

Kavera Ridge crown road · 06:38

They put the countdown where everyone could see it.

Kila tied a digital road timer to the church fence. Lani assigned one radio channel to household counts and another to slope movement. Sefa opened the church hall and turned pews into sorting tables: medicine, documents, food, mobility assistance.

Miri refused police inside the houses.

“Residents choose what leaves.”

Lani accepted.

“Residents also choose whether the search teams enter after twelve minutes.”

Miri accepted that too.

Nara stood astride the crown crack with Adaeze three metres away. The old borehole map lay inside a transparent sleeve between them. Four marked points offered actual support: a rock spur near the church, two deep concrete piers beneath the clinic terrace and a drainage culvert that Adaeze trusted only enough to curse by name.

“No line into the lower road cut,” Adaeze said. “It has no reinforcement on the old plan.”

“No line I cannot verify.”

“No line you cannot release.”

Nara looked at her mother.

“That is a different rule.”

“It is the rule you need today.”

The timer began at T−20:00.

Nara lowered one hand toward the crack.

Gold did not cover the hill. It appeared in brief, narrow geometries—one line to the church spur, one to the clinic piers, one web beneath the north stair. Nothing touched the houses themselves.

The ground moved against her.

Not as a single mass. As wet layers disagreeing about gravity. Clay slid over weathered rock. Water pressed into old fill. Foundations pulled at pipes, pipes pulled at walls, walls pulled at roofs. The complexity was enormous and indifferent.

She did not become the diagram.

She held three verified routes.

The crack slowed.

A sound passed through the residents—not a cheer yet, but the breath before one.

“Move,” Miri called. “The time is for leaving.”

Household by household, Kavera Ridge began to empty.

A woman carried a pressure cooker with both hands and nothing else. A teenage boy wore three school backpacks. Two men moved an elderly neighbour in a plastic garden chair because her wheelchair could not clear the mud. Kelechi took insulin names from Sefa and taped doses to families rather than boxes.

“Medicine follows the person,” he said. “Not the shelf.”

At T−15:42, the clinic front wall cracked outward and stopped against Nara’s first line.

At T−13:09, the north stair sank two centimetres. Gold tightened beneath it.

At T−11:31, Miri returned from her blue-doored house carrying a small battery radio and a ring of keys.

“No registers?” Nara asked.

“Copies are in the clinic.”

“The clinic is failing.”

“Then I am going back.”

“No.”

Miri’s eyes sharpened.

Nara corrected herself.

“The route is open. The building is unverified. I will not tell you the choice is safe.”

“That is different from no.”

“Yes.”

Miri looked uphill, then at the people moving down.

“Two minutes.”

“One. Kila goes with you.”

Miri nodded. A bounded yes.

They went.

At T−09:58, a child at the church fence pointed toward the golden lines and shouted, “She stopped it.”

Somebody repeated the sentence.

By T−09:41, it had reached the lower junction.

By T−09:20, a live video caption read:

LIGHT WOMAN HOLDS KAVERA RIDGE — HOMES MAY BE SAVED

Nara could not see the caption.

She could hear the cheering.

04

Gold Across the Ridge

Kavera Ridge · 06:49

Hope changed the direction of traffic.

People who had reached the church turned uphill again. Not all of them. Enough.

A man went back for a freezer he had bought after six years of saving. Two sisters returned for their mother’s sewing machine. Someone started carrying sheets of roofing iron toward a pickup because a roof could become another house if enough of it survived.

Kila stood in the path with both palms open.

“The route is out.”

“She is holding it.”

“She is holding the route.”

“Same hill.”

“No.”

The word was correct and too small.

Lani heard the report over radio and looked toward Nara.

“Nara has stabilized the crown,” she said on the public channel. “Use the window to evacuate.”

Stabilized.

The word entered every phone more cleanly than the sentence around it.

Nara heard it.

She should have corrected it immediately.

The clinic wall shifted. The north stair pulled at her field. Three load paths demanded different answers at once.

She told herself she would correct it after the next breath.

Then after the next measurement.

Then the hill used the time she had borrowed.

At T−07:12, Miri and Kila came out of the clinic carrying two plastic crates of land and medical records.

At T−06:48, the lower road dropped seven centimetres.

At T−06:31, the freezer’s owner crossed the crown path with an extension cord wrapped around his arm.

At T−06:17, Kelechi saw the house above him tilt.

He shoved the man and the freezer clear.

A sheet of corrugated metal tore loose from the veranda and came down edge-first.

Kelechi raised his arm.

The metal opened his rain jacket from wrist to elbow.

For one second the cut was white.

Then it filled.

Nara reached for him.

The gold line beneath the north stair thinned.

Adaeze shouted her name.

Nara stopped the metal, not the blood. Kila dragged Kelechi downhill. Sefa pressed gauze into his arm while he remained furious about the insulin cooler he had dropped.

“I am fine,” he said.

“You are leaking on the labels,” Sefa said. “Be quiet.”

The timer showed T−05:54.

Miri looked at the people still uphill. Then at Nara.

“You made hope look like engineering.”

“I said twenty minutes.”

“You said you could hold it.”

“I said long enough to leave.”

“Most people heard the first promise. The rest sounded like the part experts add so grief has no one to sue.”

The line struck because it was unfair.

It also struck because it was true enough.

Nara looked at Kelechi’s blood on the rainwater.

“I should have named the limit first.”

“Yes.”

No absolution. No time for one.

Adaeze’s engineer called from the borehole monitor.

“K-4 just doubled. Sixteen millimetres a minute.”

The countdown was no longer the clock.

Act II

The Promise Grows

The deep slide reveals a political one. Protection must survive evidence-based distrust.

05

Hope Looks Like Engineering

Kavera Ridge church terrace · 06:52

Nara removed the line from the clinic wall.

The wall fell.

It did not explode. It folded into the waiting room with the slow, crushing sound of a cupboard full of plates. The roof sagged after it. People cried out from the path even though everyone had been counted outside.

Nara kept the north stair and church spur.

“Why did you let it go?” someone shouted.

“To keep the path.”

“You could hold both.”

“Not safely.”

“You were holding both.”

“That was the danger.”

The words did not repair the image of gold leaving a building.

Kelechi sat beneath the church awning while Sefa wrapped his forearm. His face had gone pale beneath brown skin.

Nara crouched one metre away.

“May I look?”

“You have been looking since before it happened.”

“That is not consent.”

He exhaled through his teeth and moved the arm toward her.

The cut was deep but clean. No tendon visible. Sefa’s hands were steady.

“You do not need my light,” Nara said.

“I need you to stop making sentences about my arm and carry the blue insulin box.”

She did.

The small obedience mattered.

At the sorting table, Miri opened the recovered registers. The clinic’s patient book was dry. The land copies were not. Water had blurred several signatures into blue clouds.

Miri pressed the pages flat.

“This is why people went back.”

“Not only for objects.”

“No. For proof they existed somewhere before someone safer decided the place was empty.”

Nara looked downhill at the excavators.

Their engines were now running.

Lani followed her gaze.

“I did not authorize movement.”

One trailer began to reverse toward the ridge access road.

Miri closed the wet register.

“And now you know why a bus can look like a weapon.”

06

The Deep Line

Survey point K-4 · 06:55

Adaeze took Nara to the borehole because argument had become less dangerous than ignorance.

The instrument stood inside a dented steel box uphill from the church. Its graph showed depth on one axis and displacement on the other. Near the surface, the line bent gently. Eleven metres down, it broke sideways.

“The slip plane,” Adaeze said. “The hill is moving under everything you can see.”

Nara followed the line with one finger above the screen, not touching.

“My field slowed the crown.”

“And transferred movement downslope.”

“To the road cut.”

“Yes.”

“You told me not to anchor there.”

“You did not. The houses did. When you held their upper supports, the load searched for the next weakness.”

Nara looked toward the lower road. A long bulge had appeared beneath the asphalt like a submerged animal.

“I was not stopping it.”

“No.”

“I was storing it.”

“Yes.”

The answer hurt more because Adaeze did not decorate it.

Nara closed her eyes for one breath.

“Then I was wrong. What can we verify now?”

Adaeze enlarged the old terrain map. Before the houses, before the road, a narrow quarry gully had run along the north edge. Most of it remained open beyond Miri’s house.

“When the toe releases, the upper row will rotate across the north stair. If that happens before the last households clear, the route closes.”

“Can we hold the stair?”

“For a minute, perhaps. Not beneath six houses.”

“Alternative?”

Adaeze hesitated.

That was worse than a fast answer.

“We empty the north row and release it into the old gully before the main movement. You hold the stair side, not the houses. Gravity takes them away from the route.”

Nara looked at the map.

The first rectangle was blue.

Miri’s house.

“How many?”

“Six certain. Another nine likely when the main mass moves. More may be condemned afterward.”

“Can we save the structures another way?”

“Not with the time or equipment here.”

“Can I?”

Adaeze met her eyes.

“You can decide to become the temporary foundation of a neighbourhood. You cannot make that temporary choice permanent. Your body will fail, and the hill will collect the debt from whoever is still here.”

Below them, the excavator trailer turned into the access road.

Nara looked at the blue rectangle again.

“What must be allowed to fall?”

Adaeze did not answer for Miri.

07

The Clearance Order

Kavera Ridge lower junction · 06:58

Mere Igo arrived on foot because the road was blocked.

She carried no umbrella. The independent rights observer from the CIVIC-9 incident held her phone inside a clear evidence pouch and a printed order above the rain.

“Stop the machines,” she said.

Lani stepped in front of the first trailer and raised one hand. Kila pulled the portable barrier across the road. The driver braked hard enough to rock the excavator on its chains.

Mere gave the order to Lani.

It had been issued at 05:57, fifteen minutes before the clinic floor cracked.

EMERGENCY CLEARANCE — KAVERA RIDGE DEVELOPMENT PRECINCT

The document authorized demolition of unsafe structures, removal of debris and suspension of occupancy pending redevelopment review. Harbour Crown was named as the preferred emergency contractor.

Lani read it twice.

“I was not copied.”

Miri laughed without humour.

“We were.”

She showed them a message sent to several residents at 06:20: evacuation transport available; uncollected property subject to clearance.

The refusal had not begun with denial of the hill.

It had begun with evidence.

Nara felt shame try to turn itself into urgency: tell them to leave now, explain later, use the light, be trusted. The old temptation was not to hurt anyone. It was to decide that because the danger was real, their reasons could wait.

She let the temptation pass without obeying it.

Miri looked at her.

“Say it.”

“You were right not to confuse evacuation with consent to disappear.”

“And the hill?”

“Is still moving.”

“So both things are true.”

“Yes.”

Lani tore the contractor access slip from the order and handed it to Mere.

“I can suspend emergency operations under my authority. I cannot settle land tenure on a road in the rain.”

“You do not need to settle it,” Mere said. “You need to preserve it.”

She opened the disaster statute on her phone. One clause allowed temporary evacuation without abandonment, transfer or extinguishment of occupancy claims. It required a named shelter, a public notice and a record of affected households.

“How long?” Lani asked.

“To issue? Three minutes. To survive a court challenge? Ask me after we are alive.”

The hill made another deep sound.

The church timer sounded at 00:00. Nara released the continuous web beneath the ridge, keeping only the north stair in brief, verified pulses. The first bargain had ended. Anything after it would need a new one.

Nara looked at the buses, the fenced transit centre route card in each windscreen and the church school two streets below the slide boundary.

“Change the destination,” she said. “The school stays inside the ward and within sight of the ridge.”

“It has toilets for eighty,” Lani said. “There are more than three hundred people.”

“The transit centre can send toilets, water and bedding here. People do not have to be transported away to receive them.”

Miri pointed at the machines.

“And those leave first.”

Lani keyed her radio.

“All Harbour Crown equipment reverse to the vehicle yard. Engines off. No plant enters Kavera Ridge without resident witnesses and a fresh safety authorization.”

The contractor supervisor protested over the channel.

Lani cut him off.

“That was an order, not a negotiation.”

Mere began recording.

The first excavator reversed.

The physical direction mattered more than every reassurance said before it.

08

A Safer Place to Leave From

Kavera Ridge · 07:02

Lani read the notice on the public channel.

“Evacuation from Kavera Ridge is for immediate life safety. It does not surrender occupancy, ownership or land claims. No demolition, transfer or redevelopment action is authorized for seventy-two hours, and no extension may occur without resident representation and public notice.”

Mere posted the signed document before Lani finished the last sentence.

At the bottom of the hill, the excavators rolled away.

At the church school, doors opened. Teachers dragged desks to the walls. Water drums came up from the transit centre instead of residents being taken down to it. Household names were written on removable cards, not on intake forms that changed addresses.

Miri watched all of it.

Then she took the public radio from Kila.

“This is Miri Soro. North row first. One bag per person. Medicines and documents before furniture. Nobody returns after their household is counted. We are leaving the ridge. We are not giving it away.”

Movement resumed.

Not trust.

A safer way to act without trust.

Nara went to Miri.

“The first six houses must be released into the old gully before the main slide crosses the stair.”

Miri’s face did not change.

“Mine first.”

“Yes.”

“Will it be destroyed?”

“Yes.”

“Do not say likely.”

“It will be destroyed.”

Miri looked at the blue door. Rain had darkened the paint around the handle. A child’s height marks ran up the frame in black pen.

“How long?”

“Nine minutes if the movement stays where it is. Less if it changes.”

“And if I say no?”

Nara answered the actual question.

“If the route is still occupied and the house begins to fall across it, I will move it to protect people. I do not need consent to stop that harm. But we have time for you to choose how it happens and what leaves first.”

Miri closed her hand around the key ring.

“Then I choose.”

She turned to Kila.

“One minute inside. You stand at the door. If I do not come out, you pull me.”

Kila nodded.

Miri looked at Nara again.

“Let mine go first.”

The second bargain was smaller than the first.

It was also true.

Act III

Release

The bridge is no longer holding everything. It is choosing what must be released—and who gets to decide.

09

Let Mine Go First

Miri’s house · north row · 07:05

Miri entered her house without Nara.

That mattered.

Nara waited outside the threshold while Kila stood where he could reach Miri without pretending he owned the minute. Inside, drawers opened. A cupboard door struck a wall. The house shifted and every loose spoon answered.

Miri came out carrying the battery radio, a cloth bag and the narrow wooden strip marked with her children’s heights.

Kila took the bag. She kept the strip and keys.

“My husband built the back room wrong,” she said to Adaeze. “He knew. He said the hill would forgive him.”

“Hills do not forgive,” Adaeze said. “They also do not accuse.”

“Convenient.”

“No. Just heavy.”

Adaeze marked the release path with white work lights. The old quarry gully lay empty beyond the north wall. Kila cleared everyone behind the church spur. Lani confirmed the stair count: forty-three people still descending from the upper row.

Nara placed one hand toward the blue house.

Gold appeared at three points only: the stair-side corner, the roof beam that could shear into the path and the rear foundation nearest the gully.

Miri watched from the safe line.

“Do you want me to make it gentle?” Nara asked.

Miri looked at the height strip in her hand.

“No. Safely.”

Nara released the rear foundation.

The house moved five centimetres and stopped.

The ground beneath it opened.

She held the stair-side corner until the roof rotated away from the path. Then she let the corner go.

The blue door remained upright for one impossible second after the rest of the house began to slide.

Then it turned, caught the rain and vanished into the gully with timber, iron and the room where Miri’s children had become taller.

No one cheered.

The stair remained open.

Miri did not thank Nara.

She lifted the radio.

“House one released. Route clear. Keep moving.”

The next owner stepped forward.

“My kitchen roof will swing toward the stair,” he said. “Take the water tank first.”

Adaeze checked the map.

“He is right.”

The work became grief with sequence.

10

The Hill Chooses Gravity

Kavera Ridge north stair · 07:08

House two went roof-first.

House three broke at the middle and slid in two pieces.

The water tank from house four rolled toward the stair. Nara caught its path with a curved gold plane, held it away from the last households and let it continue into the gully. It burst below them in a white rush.

The slope accelerated.

“K-4 at thirty-eight millimetres,” the engineer called. “No—fifty-one.”

The hill stopped offering numbers that sounded small.

The north stair was full of people moving in disciplined fear. Miri stood at the landing and counted households. Kila took the upper end. Lani held the lower channel open. Adaeze stayed beside Nara with the borehole display and refused to let the light become the only instrument.

“Upper row clear except Soro lane,” Miri called.

“Two responders,” Kila answered. “No residents.”

Nara felt the main mass begin to rotate.

It arrived in her body as a false horizon. The church leaned left though it had not moved. Rain seemed to fall sideways. Gold lines multiplied without permission, trying to attach to every house still standing.

She closed most of them.

“The path,” Adaeze said.

“The path.”

The first wave entered the gully. Mud, foundations and broken water pipes moved together. Nara held one edge away from the stair and released the other. The gold did not stop gravity. It gave gravity a safer direction for three seconds at a time.

A responder at the top slipped. Kila caught his vest.

“Clear!”

Miri counted again.

“Three hundred twelve.”

Lani checked the clinic list.

“Three hundred thirteen expected.”

The missing name was Pita Kora, keeper of the community office.

Miri’s face hardened.

“He went for the original land box.”

The office stood above house six.

Nara saw a figure in the doorway carrying a metal case.

The easy judgment came first: foolish, material, late.

Then the verified context followed. The wet copies at the church had lost signatures. The box might be the difference between evacuation and legal erasure.

Nara could not tell Pita the papers did not matter.

She also could not let him remain.

She opened a narrow shield above the path, not around his will.

“Pita!” Miri shouted. “Mere uploaded the registry scan from the land office. The originals are evidence, not our only existence. Leave the box.”

Pita looked downhill.

“Verified?”

Mere answered over Lani’s radio. “Timestamped, witnessed and copied to three independent stores. I can prove the file existed before the clearance order.”

The office shifted.

Pita set the metal box inside the doorway.

He ran.

His choice, built from evidence he trusted.

Kila pulled him across the crown line as the office dropped.

“Three hundred thirteen,” Miri said.

Everyone was out.

Nara kept holding.

A row of houses above the clinic remained connected to her field. Empty. Unrecoverable. Still asking.

“Nara,” Adaeze said.

“I can take another line.”

“The path is empty.”

“The lower clinic—”

“Empty.”

“The water main—”

“Closed.”

“There are animals—”

“Released.”

Each answer removed a reason to stay.

The true reason remained.

If she let go, the neighbourhood would fall while she watched.

“Nara.”

Her mother did not say you are dying. She said the more difficult thing.

“There is no one left for your body to be a foundation for.”

The gold flickered.

Nara looked at Miri.

Miri held the height strip against her chest. Her face was wet with rain and something rain did not explain.

“Release,” Miri said.

A clear, terrible permission for the thing that belonged to her.

Nara opened her hands.

The hill chose gravity.

Houses folded, slid and separated. Roofs became sheets. Rooms opened briefly to the rain—beds, calendars, cooking shelves, the private architecture of ordinary life—then entered the gully and the lower debris field.

Nara held only the church spur, the empty stair and the line that kept the moving mass away from the school road.

One line.

Then none.

The sound arrived like weather breaking a building-sized bone.

The ridge fell.

11

Which Way Is Down

Kavera Ridge lower junction · 07:13

When the movement stopped, Nara was standing on a vertical road.

She knew intellectually that the road was almost level. Her body disagreed. The sky lay to her right. The church wall was beneath her. Every step attempted to fall in a different direction.

She went to one knee.

Adaeze reached toward her and stopped before contact.

“May I?”

Nara tried to nod and nearly struck the ground.

“Yes.”

Adaeze took her shoulders. The pressure gave her one local definition of upright.

“Count?” Nara asked.

“Three hundred thirteen,” Miri said.

“Again.”

“Three hundred thirteen. All residents. All responders.”

Nara trusted the count because she could not trust the horizon.

She tried to stand.

The road rolled under her. She vomited rainwater and the bread Kelechi had made her eat half of.

“Elegant,” Kelechi said from beneath the church awning.

His forearm was bandaged from wrist to elbow. He had no business smiling and did it anyway.

“You should be at the clinic,” Nara said.

“The clinic fell.”

“The other clinic.”

“You first.”

“No.”

Kelechi raised his uninjured hand.

“That word is familiar. People are allowed to use it with you too.”

Kila and Sefa brought a rescue board. Nara looked toward the ridge.

A dark shape moved in the debris.

“Someone—”

“A tarpaulin,” Adaeze said. “I checked.”

“I need to see.”

“No,” Miri said.

Nara looked at her.

Miri’s voice did not soften.

“You asked us to leave when the ground could no longer hold us. Now leave.”

The symmetry was not kind.

It was necessary.

Nara lay back on the board.

They carried her down the road she had not saved, toward the school that had become a shelter without becoming an exile.

For the first time since the hill moved, she let herself be one of the people evacuated.

12

The Key Still Opens Something

Kavera Ward School · that evening

Thirty-one homes were destroyed. Fourteen more were marked unsafe. The clinic was gone above the foundation line. Kelechi received twelve stitches and a lecture he claimed was longer than the cut.

No one died.

The sentence did not cancel the others.

The school gym filled with mattresses, water containers, cooking pots, medicine trays and the low conversation of people discovering which parts of a life had fit inside one bag. The city brought portable toilets uphill. Nobody was transferred to Kone Transit Centre.

Mere pinned the evacuation notice beside the gym entrance. Beneath it she posted the clearance order, uncancelled and visible, with Lani’s suspension across the front.

Repair left a record.

Nara spent most of the afternoon on a mat because every time she closed her eyes, gravity changed direction. By evening she could sit if one shoulder touched the wall.

She used that position to rebuild the clinic medicine list with Sefa.

Ordinary work. Batch numbers. Doses. Names.

Kelechi lowered himself beside her, bandaged arm in a sling.

“I saved your breakfast,” he said.

He handed her a crushed parcel of bread, wet on one side and streaked with his blood on the other.

“That is not food.”

“It was before your day touched it.”

She ate the dry corner.

Lani came with the preliminary incident report. One line read:

Public misunderstanding of temporary stabilization contributed to delayed evacuation and re-entry.

Nara read it twice.

“Change it.”

“To what?”

“I said I could hold the movement long enough for people to take what mattered. I did not name what I was holding before I named the hope. I heard ‘stabilized’ on the radio and did not correct it. My wording and my delay contributed to re-entry.”

“That will be used against you.”

“It should be used to stop us doing it again.”

Lani studied her.

Then she crossed out misunderstanding.

Miri approached carrying the narrow height strip and the key to the blue door.

She read the revised line over Lani’s shoulder.

“Keep that version.”

“We will,” Lani said.

Miri sat on the floor across from Nara.

“I am still angry.”

“Yes.”

“You helped us leave. You also helped us wait.”

“Yes.”

“I do not know which part I will remember first.”

“You do not have to choose tonight.”

Miri turned the key in her fingers.

“It opens nothing now.”

Nara looked at the worn teeth, the paint caught in the ring, the small object refusing to understand the scale of what had changed.

“It still belongs to a door that was real,” she said.

Miri closed her hand around it.

“That is not the same as opening something.”

“No.”

A pause.

“Maybe later,” Miri said.

It was not forgiveness.

It was not nothing.

After Miri left, Nara opened her notebook. Her handwriting tilted uphill.

Beneath Connection is not knowledge and Repair leaves a record, she wrote:

A limit unspoken becomes a promise.

Then she closed the book before the sentence could become a shield against feeling what it meant.

At dusk, Adaeze returned alone to the edge of the slide.

The rain had washed the exposed earth clean enough to show layers no map recorded. Red clay. Broken fill. Weathered stone.

And beneath them, a smooth black arc wider than a road.

It had no seam. No rust. No tool marks.

One incomplete gold line moved across its surface and went dark.

The small sphere in Adaeze’s pocket warmed in answer.

She looked toward the school, where Nara was finally asleep among hundreds of people whose homes had fallen and whose names had not.

Adaeze did not wake her.

Some truths were urgent.

This one had been waiting beneath the hill for longer than the hill had a name.

“A limit unspoken becomes a promise.”
Behind the story · what Nara learned

First non-harm act: Nara catches and lowers an oxygen cylinder before its valve strikes the floor, then holds only the clinic stair long enough for three people to leave. She protects a specific life path rather than claiming the whole building.

Failure: Her time-limited hold becomes public evidence that the neighbourhood may be preserved. She hears imprecise language and does not correct it quickly. Re-entry delays evacuation and Kelechi is injured.

Counterforce: Residents are not merely irrational or attached to objects. A pre-issued clearance order gives them evidence that evacuation may be used as dispossession.

Second bridge: Shelter and services move into the ward; contractor machines withdraw; occupancy claims are explicitly preserved; the community leads its own evacuation. This does not require trust, only a safer structure for leaving.

Decisive choice: Miri chooses to release her own house first. Later, she tells Nara to release the empty ridge and, finally, to let herself be evacuated.

Growth: Nara learns that a boundary named too late behaves like a broken promise. Protection is not preservation, and letting go is not abandonment when every person is already safe.

Cost: After holding a moving slope, Nara’s vestibular sense fails. She cannot tell which way is down and must accept being carried away while others continue the work.

Continuity hook: The slide exposes a seamless buried structure that answers the artifact connected to Nara’s father.
