01 · The Pier

Little Skerry, harbour pier · thirty hours before the second storm, 18:05

The fleet had been in for three days and had not gone back out. Two hundred boats lay nose to tail along the breakwater with their nets wet and their masts still, and the only light on the pier burned over the fish-scale hut where Marit Okonjo stood with her back to the water, as if the sea were someone she had not yet decided to speak to.

Illuminara walked the length of the pier without hurrying. She had rehearsed nothing, on purpose. She stopped two paces short and began before Marit could turn.

IlluminaraMy track for the first storm was eleven kilometres east of where the storm went. That put five point one metres of swell on the Gannet Run at the hour Hesper Dawn crossed it, not four point three. I called the Run safe. I called it safe because the chart gave nine metres of water on the Run, and because I never asked anyone who lives here whether the chart was true.

Marit turned. She was fifty, small, with a face the weather had been editing for decades.

Marit OkonjoYou've had three days to practise that.

IlluminaraThree days to check it. It comes out the same each time.

Marit OkonjoInes Varga has a daughter at the university in the city. Dov Hallam is nineteen and bought a guitar he can't play. Lise Harrow can't swim well. My husband holds the end of the net because he says it's the place where you can see everybody's face. Say their names. I want to hear if you've got them right.

IlluminaraInes Varga, skipper. Dov Hallam. Lise Harrow. Tobiah Okonjo. I don't know which of them is alive. I have watched the drift charts every hour since Tuesday and I don't know.

She had imagined that saying it would feel like setting something down. It felt like lifting something. She noticed that she was holding her hands very still, the way one does near a thing that might break.

Marit OkonjoNow you tell me you've fixed the model. Second storm is thirty hours off. Sixty-one boats from the outer villages are still on Gannet Roads, sixteen hours from home, and the Run is the only way in that beats the weather. You'll want my fleet on your second guess.

IlluminaraI won't tell you it's fixed. I don't know what else it can't see. I came to find out what it can't see, and to ask who is allowed to refuse it.

Marit OkonjoEveryone. Me first.

IlluminaraThen I'd like to be refused properly, by people who've looked at the numbers. Let me take them to the Pilots' Hall and let the old pilots tell me where I'm wrong.

Marit OkonjoMy husband is out there and you want me to sit in a hall.

IlluminaraI want you to find him. I can't say where he is. I can say where the water would take him, and only if the pilots tell me what this water does that my model doesn't know. Without them I'd be guessing where to look, and I've guessed enough.

Marit looked at her for a long time. Behind them a halyard rang against a mast, once, and was still.

Marit OkonjoOne hour. After that I decide who talks to my fleet, and it isn't you.

02 · The Hall

Pilots' Hall, Little Skerry · twenty-eight hours before the second storm, 19:40

The hall smelled of tar and old paper. Fourteen chairs stood in a ring, and on the knee of the oldest pilot lay a ledger bound in sailcloth, its pages ruled by hand into columns that no chart-maker had ever seen. Wenna Tolliver was seventy-one and did not get up. A girl of sixteen sat on the stairs with a hydrophone in her lap, winding its cable round her hand, pretending not to listen.

Illuminara unrolled her bathymetry on the table and weighted the corners with Wenna's own tea mugs, because nobody had told her not to.

Wenna TolliverFive-year-old chart. The Choir isn't a rock, girl. It's a bank, and a bank walks.

IlluminaraWalks how fast?

Wenna TolliverSlowly, until a gale. Then in a night. The note tells you. Water goes over the crest and sings, higher when it's shoaler, the way a whistle rises when you pinch it. We call it the Choir because it was once a hymn, and it's been out of tune with the chart since the September blows.

IlluminaraWhat note is the chart's nine metres?

Wenna TolliverA low E. I've not heard a low E on the Run in a month.

Pip MarrenIt's an A, Wenna. I took it Sunday. Nobody asked me.

Wenna TolliverThat's why you're on the stairs, Pip. Be quiet and be useful.

Illuminara did the sum where all three of them could see it. A swell breaks when it is more than three-quarters as tall as the water under it. In six metres of water, four and a half metres of swell breaks. Hesper Dawn had met five point one.

IlluminaraThen the chart was wrong and my track was wrong, and either one right would have kept her out of it. I took both because they agreed with each other.

Wenna TolliverThat's the worst reason to take anything.

IlluminaraYes.

Wenna turned a page of the sailcloth ledger without looking at it. Her thumb knew where the page was.

Wenna TolliverYou'll want the marks. Eighty years of them, a note and a depth and a date, written down in this hall and nowhere else. Give them to a table and the table gives them to a firm in the city, and the firm sells a sea to every trawler from the south. Then we're all pilots and nobody is.

IlluminaraThen they don't leave the hall. I won't copy a page. You read me the note, I give you the swell, and you decide. Nothing goes in my model that you didn't say aloud.

Wenna TolliverSlower. Good. Slow is what keeps people alive. Ines Varga trusted a lane because it was on a screen, and a lane on a screen has nobody inside it to say no.

IlluminaraI'd like that written down too.

Wenna looked at her for the first time as one looks at a stranger who might turn out to be a colleague.

Wenna TolliverTonight you come out on the Run with Pip and me. I'm not describing a note to someone who has never heard one. If you're going to be wrong again, you'll be wrong in a boat.

03 · The Choir

Arrow Strait, the Gannet Run · twenty-four hours before the second storm, 23:50

The skiff was small and smelled of diesel and cold coffee. Pip held the tiller and Wenna sat in the bow with the hydrophone's cable over her knee, and Illuminara sat amidships with a lamp that she was told not to shine on the water. The Run was only a line on the chart; in the dark it was a long loose silence between two white seams of surf.

Pip lowered the microphone. The earphones went round once and Illuminara took them.

It began as a hum, the sort that lives in a refrigerator, and then as they crossed the mark buoy it climbed, steadily, like a voice going up to ask a question. She heard it bend and thicken.

Pip MarrenThat's the A. Last month it was an F-sharp.

Wenna TolliverSix metres, six and a half. And it's coming up. Hear that? It's still walking.

IlluminaraMy model has nine here.

Wenna TolliverYour model has a chart.

Illuminara did not answer. The note held in her ears, and she found it was much harder to doubt than a number. She had never been afraid of a number, she realised. She was afraid of this.

Wenna TolliverTake us north, Pip. Three hundred.

They crept across the Run with the engine at a mutter. The hum dropped as they went, a tone, then another, then a long low note at the edge of hearing, and the earphones filled with something almost like breath.

I could have asked. Any day this year, I could have asked.

Pip MarrenLow G.

Wenna TolliverEleven, twelve metres. The crest used to sit here, before it walked south onto the Run. It left a trough behind it.

IlluminaraA lane.

Wenna TolliverA lane that's a lane tonight. I'm not telling you it'll be one tomorrow. Tomorrow you ask me again.

IlluminaraEvery time. At the outer mark, with the note, before any boat crosses.

Wenna TolliverHmph.

The skiff drifted. Far to the east the sky had a stain on it where the second storm was assembling, and nobody mentioned it.

IlluminaraWho did the Choir take?

Wenna TolliverMy brother Jory, forty-one years back, on a day so flat it looked ironed. I told him the note was a D, and it was an E-flat. I said so in the hall the next morning, to everybody, before one of them could ask. That's the only reason they let me keep the book.

She said nothing else about it. Pip, at the tiller, turned her face away and studied the dark with great seriousness.

Illuminara took the earphones off. The sea went on without her. For a moment she let herself want something very small and very selfish, which was to have been out here three days ago, in this boat, hearing this.

04 · The Terms

Pilots' Hall · twenty hours before the second storm, 04:10

Marit had not slept, and had not pretended to. The sailcloth ledger lay open on the table under the hall's one good lamp, and beside it three sheets of paper, each headed in Wenna's hand. Illuminara had brought two mugs of tea. Marit refused the first and drank the second without comment.

Marit OkonjoTell me what you found.

IlluminaraThe Run is dead. The bank is under it, six metres, shoaling. The storm's swell will break there from early evening. About three hundred metres north there is a trough, eleven to twelve metres, left behind when the crest walked. Wenna calls it the Slot. If the pilots' note holds, the fleet can use the Slot and cross ahead of the weather.

Marit OkonjoIf the note holds.

IlluminaraYes. That's the load. It's why I'm not the one to say it.

Marit OkonjoGood. Here's how it goes. Three predictions, written in the book before the sun's up. The old table says nine metres, no break. Wenna says the note's an A, a break sometime after eight. Yours says the pre-swell reaches the Run at about six-forty and the Slot stays quiet with a low G. Pip takes the skiff out at first light and writes down what she hears. If yours doesn't beat both of the others on something we can see, nobody uses it.

IlluminaraThen put a number in it that I can be caught on. Write the width of the break. If it can't be wrong in a way you can see, it isn't a forecast, it's a prophecy, and I've already given you one of those.

Marit OkonjoHow wide?

IlluminaraTwo hundred metres of white across the Run. Write it last, under the others, in my name.

Marit wrote it. Her pen did not hesitate, and Illuminara understood that she had just made herself easier to refuse, and that this was what she had come to do.

Marit OkonjoAnd if yours is right?

IlluminaraThen it's still not mine to use.

Marit OkonjoNo. Wenna calls the marks on the radio. I call the fleet, in my own voice, because they'll answer mine. You stay on the pier.

IlluminaraA sixteen-year-old in a skiff, under a swell.

Marit OkonjoShe's the best skiff hand in the strait and I'm on the radio. Abort rule: if Pip hears anything higher than a B on the Slot, the water's shallower than eight metres and the fleet stays out under the lee of Gannet with sea anchors. I never call the Slot. That costs sixty-one boats a night at anchor in a bad wind. I know what it costs. I'm telling you it's mine to pay.

Illuminara did not answer at once. She had a great many ways to reach that skiff other than waiting on a pier, and the hall had written no rule against any of them. She also knew that a lane the fleet used because she had gone and made it safe would be hers, and that the whole of the night's work had been to make one that was not.

IlluminaraAgreed. All of it.

Marit OkonjoThat took you a while.

IlluminaraIt took as long as it should.

Marit turned a page of the ledger she did not need to read.

Marit OkonjoTobiah.

IlluminaraTwo places. With the pilots' eddies in the drift it tightens to Gull Tooth ledge, where Wenna says the flood sets anything that floats, or the Tail Race. Nobody lives through the Tail Race. Today's spring tide covers the ledge a little after two in the afternoon. A boat has to be there by half past eleven, by the inner passage. That one isn't the Run. It's sheltered.

Marit OkonjoDon't tell me he's alive.

IlluminaraI don't know that he is. I'll be on the pier until he's either on it or someone has told you he won't be.

Marit OkonjoI didn't ask you to stay.

IlluminaraI know. I'm telling you where I'll be.

Marit said nothing. The lamp hissed. Out in the dark somebody was already hauling a skiff down the slip. Pip, Illuminara realised, who had been awake as long as any of them and had not been asked to sleep.

05 · The Test

Little Skerry, harbour pier · seventeen hours before the second storm, 06:30

The radio sat on an upturned crate between Marit and Illuminara, and neither looked at it. Wenna's voice came out of it dry and level, calling the marks as if reading a menu. Out on the Run a small light moved against a grey sea. The pre-swell was already visible as a slow shouldering of the horizon, like something large turning over in its sleep.

Pip MarrenRun mark. I've got the A. It's a clean A. Bank's where Wenna said.

Wenna TolliverSwell?

Pip MarrenLong. Long and low and it's lifting me.

Marit OkonjoSix thirty-eight.

Marit wasn't speaking to anyone. She had the three sheets of paper in her fist.

Pip MarrenIt's coming up under me. I'm going to the Slot. Wenna, I'm going to the Slot now.

Wenna TolliverGo.

Illuminara heard the engine rise. The skiff was a mile out under a breaking sky with one girl in it, and Illuminara could have been beside it before the next sentence ended. She stood on the pier with her hands at her sides, because she had said she would, and because a lane she had to carry a girl through was not a lane. It was the hardest sum of the night, and the answer was to do nothing.

Pip MarrenThere it goes. Oh. Oh, it's breaking. It's breaking white across the whole Run. It's more than two hundred, it's, I don't know, three hundred and fifty, I can see the whole edge.

Marit OkonjoSix forty-one.

Marit did not look at Illuminara. She did not need to. A hundred and fifty metres of white where her name stood on the sheet for two hundred.

Pip MarrenI'm at the Slot mouth. It's lifting. It's a B. It's not quiet, Wenna, it's a B.

Marit OkonjoA B is not higher than a B.

Wenna TolliverIt's the mouth. The Slot starts further north than your girl thinks. Pip, forty metres. Along the white post and then forty. Listen for the G.

IlluminaraMine had the mouth too far south.

Wenna TolliverYours had it where the chart put the old crest. The crest walked sideways too. I can hear that. You couldn't.

Pip MarrenNorth forty. North, north. There. G. That's the G, Wenna, it's quiet, it's lifting but there's no break.

Nobody spoke on the pier. Marit read the three sheets again, and then, slowly, once, without hurry, she put them down on the crate.

Marit OkonjoTable said no break. Wrong. Wenna said the bank right and the hour wrong by an hour and a half, and the mouth right. Yours had the hour to the minute, and the Slot, and the width a hundred and fifty out, and the mouth forty out. Wenna's ear moved your mouth and your hour moved her break. Neither one puts a boat through alone.

IlluminaraMy bank came from Wenna.

Marit OkonjoAnd the hour came from you, off a track you got wrong and a swell period you got right. I'll use the second.

She picked up the fleet microphone, and for a moment her hand did not seem to belong to her.

Marit OkonjoGannet Roads, this is Marit Okonjo, fleet-captain, Little Skerry. Come in by the Slot, three hundred north of the Run and not by the Run, and forty past the white post. Wenna Tolliver calls the note at the outer mark. You cross on a G or lower. You hear a B, you hold, you drop the sea anchors and I'll answer for it. This is my call. Repeat it back to me.

The voices came back one by one, crackling, sixty-one of them, each repeating the same four sentences in a different accent. Illuminara counted them and found she was crying and had not noticed. She wiped her face with the back of her hand as if it were spray.

Marit OkonjoYour name is in the book.

IlluminaraWhere?

Marit OkonjoOn the left. In the column Wenna keeps for what we got wrong. She wrote it herself before you'd sat down.

06 · Gull Tooth

Gull Tooth ledge, outer skerries · thirteen hours before the second storm, 11:10

They went by the inner passage, which was sheltered and slow, in a boat that Marit helmed herself. She had said Illuminara would come, and that she would hold the lamp and touch no rope. Pip, wet through and radiant, sat in the stern with the hydrophone in her lap. Wenna's voice, from the hall, hummed in the speaker like an insect in a jar.

Gull Tooth was a black shelf the size of a kitchen, awash on every third wave, with a reef in front of it where the swell stood up and fell. There were two figures on it, lashed to a stanchion bent from some wreck, and a third shape, long and still, wrapped in a yellow oilskin.

Pip MarrenMarit. There's two of them standing.

Marit did not answer. She had a hand on the throttle and the knuckles had gone the colour of bone.

IlluminaraThe surge on the reef is running ten minutes high and then easing. The next slack is eleven minutes long and begins in ninety seconds. If the gap is where the pilots' notes put it, you have time to cross once.

Wenna TolliverThe gap's two boat-lengths south of the white post, on an E. You hear an E, you go. You hear an F, you stop and I'll curse you.

Marit OkonjoPip.

Pip MarrenE. I've got an E.

Marit OkonjoThen we go.

It took four minutes, the boat dipping and rising, the hydrophone singing its single note, Marit's wet cheek turned toward the ledge. The wave stood up behind them, hesitated, and passed. Then they were under the stanchion and the lamp lit two faces, grey and swollen, one of them laughing in the helpless way of someone who had not expected to.

Tobiah OkonjoYou took your time.

Marit OkonjoI was in a hall.

The three of them were lifted over the gunwale, Lise Harrow first, then Tobiah, and then the long yellow shape. Dov Hallam was dead, and had been since the first night, Tobiah said, which was why he had lashed him there. Marit was the only one who touched him, gently, the way she had once touched her own sleeping children. Ines Varga was not on the ledge. Tobiah said so once, quietly, at the tiller, and Marit nodded and did not make him say it again.

On the way back he looked across the boat at the stranger holding the lamp.

Tobiah OkonjoMarit says you said our names on the pier. Before anyone asked.

IlluminaraI didn't know which of you were alive.

Tobiah OkonjoThat's what she told me you said.

Illuminara held the lamp until her arm ached. She did not set it down, because nobody had told her she could.

07 · The Count

Little Skerry, breakwater and Pilots' Hall · two and a half hours before the second storm, 21:30

The rain had come early. Fifty-two hulls were in, and the breakwater lamp showed them one at a time as they slid past the pier, each skipper standing at the wheel and looking at no one. Wenna counted with a stub of pencil and her tongue between her teeth. Marit stood at the end of the breakwater with the fleet microphone in her fist. Illuminara stood two paces behind her, where she had been told, with the lamp hanging from her hand. Out at the mouth of the Slot, Pip's skiff rode the swell with its hydrophone down, and the speaker on the breakwater carried what Pip heard: one long, even G, like a held breath.

Pip MarrenFifty-two in. G. Next is Rook.

Aud HallamMarit, Rook. I've the mark in sight. Coming through.

Marit's thumb was already on the key when the note moved.

Pip MarrenB. I've got a B.

Wenna TolliverA B's allowed.

Pip MarrenIt's a B and it's walking. It's, Marit, it's a C.

The rule had been written in the book at four in the morning and had been nothing since but ink.

Marit OkonjoHold. All boats, hold. Rook, hold. Sea anchors down and keep your heads.

Aud HallamMarit. I have been sixteen hours on this water to get to my son. He's on a bench in your hall and I'm sixteen hours late. Don't make me wait on a note.

Marit OkonjoYou wait.

Aud HallamWhose number is that?

Marit OkonjoMine.

Marit lowered the microphone and, without turning her head, spoke to the person behind her.

Marit OkonjoIs it the Slot, or the surge?

IlluminaraThe surge. The pulse on the reef runs ten minutes and the last two ran ten. The note should fall in about six.

Marit OkonjoShould.

IlluminaraShould. I'd hold until Pip says G regardless.

Marit OkonjoI wasn't asking you when. I was asking what.

IlluminaraI know.

It took seven minutes. Nobody spoke on the breakwater, and the wind got into the radio and sang a note of its own.

Pip MarrenG. G, Marit. It's gone down. G.

Marit OkonjoRook first. Come through.

They came through, nine hulls in the rain, and the last of the lamps showed Rook's skipper at the wheel with her chin up. As she passed the pier her voice came out of the speaker, level, and not for the sea.

Aud HallamIs she there? The woman who did the sums.

Marit OkonjoShe's behind me.

Aud HallamThe first sum cost me my boy. The second brought me in to him. I'll carry both, and you'll not ask me to put either one down.

IlluminaraNo.

The sixty-first hull slid in past the pier at ten thirty-five. Wenna put a tick in the sailcloth ledger and sat down on the nearest bollard as if her legs had been taken from her.

Inside, the lamp was low. Tobiah lay on a bench under a blanket with a mug of broth. Lise had been taken home. Dov lay under a grey blanket at the end of the hall, and his mother sat beside him with her hand on the blanket and her back to the room.

Marit OkonjoI'm not forgiving you.

IlluminaraNo.

Marit OkonjoInes Varga's daughter lands on Thursday. You'll tell her what you told me, in this hall, with the book open, and she'll say whatever she likes. If it's nothing, it's nothing. If it's worse than I said, you'll hear it.

IlluminaraYes.

Wenna TolliverAnd when the sea drops, Pip and I take the skiff down the Tail Race eddy. A mark an hour on the ebb, fifteen of them. You give us the swell for each mark and we say which mark. If the daughter wants to ride along, she rides.

IlluminaraI'll bring the swell. You choose the mark.

Marit OkonjoAnd the next time there's a storm, you ask the hall before you tell the harbour. Not after. Before. In the book, as a rule.

IlluminaraI'll write it myself. In my own hand, on the left.

Marit almost smiled, and it went out like a match in a draught.

Tobiah OkonjoLise says the woman with the lamp never put it down, Marit.

Marit OkonjoNobody told her she could.

Tobiah OkonjoThen somebody should.

Nobody did. Illuminara found she had stopped waiting for it.

Wenna had come in from the cold and stood dripping by the stove. She laid the sailcloth ledger open on the table, three columns now where there had been two: what the table said, what the water said, what the hall did about it. Beneath the first storm the left column held a line in her old square hand. Illuminara read it, and did not look away.

Wenna TolliverCome here and hear this.

She switched on the speaker at the end of the table. Out beyond the breakwater the hydrophone still hung where Pip had left it, and through the rain it carried a long, low, steady note, a G, the sound of a lane open in the dark, with sixty-one hulls resting above it, and a hundred and twenty miles of weather coming in behind them to find nothing it could break.

What the critic still says doesn't work

Illuminara admits the fatal limits of her first forecast, learns from Wenna's tide-hearing, makes her replacement prediction refusable and fallible, and helps Marit bring sixty-one boats home without taking the harbour's knowledge or authority from its people.

Tonight's shape

Seven scenes across thirty hours: an error said aloud before anyone asks, a hall of old pilots who will not give up their marks, a night on the water learning to hear a bank, terms set by the woman Illuminara wronged, a dawn test in which the hall and the model each catch the other's miss, a rescue timed by two kinds of knowledge, and a crossing at the breakwater where the abort rule is used, a harmed voice is heard without absolution, and a count ends that is not forgiveness.

01 · Pomol

Little Skerry, pristaniški pomol · trideset ur pred drugo nevihto, 18.05

Flota je bila v pristanišču tri dni in ni več odplula. Dvesto čolnov je ležalo s premci drug za drugim vzdolž valobrana, z mokrimi mrežami in negibnimi jambori, edina luč na pomolu pa je gorela nad kočo z ribjimi luskami, kjer je Marit Okonjo stala s hrbtom proti vodi, kakor da je morje nekdo, s komer se še ni odločila spregovoriti.

Illuminara je brez hitenja prehodila pomol. Namenoma ni vadila ničesar. Ustavila se je dva koraka proč in začela, še preden se je Marit lahko obrnila.

IlluminaraMoja pot za prvo nevihto je bila enajst kilometrov vzhodneje od poti, po kateri je nevihta dejansko šla. Zato je bilo na Gannet Runu ob uri, ko ga je prečkala Hesper Dawn, pet celih ena metra valovanja, ne štiri cele tri. Rekla sem, da je Run varen. Rekla sem, da je varen, ker je zemljevid kazal devet metrov vode na Runu in ker nikogar, ki živi tukaj, nisem vprašala, ali zemljevid drži.

Marit se je obrnila. Imela je petdeset let, bila je drobna, njen obraz pa je vreme urejalo že desetletja.

Marit OkonjoTri dni si imela, da si to vadila.

IlluminaraTri dni, da sem preverjala. Vsakič se izide enako.

Marit OkonjoInes Varga ima hčer na univerzi v mestu. Dov Hallam je star devetnajst let in si je kupil kitaro, ki je ne zna igrati. Lise Harrow ne zna dobro plavati. Moj mož drži konec mreže, ker pravi, da je to mesto, od koder vidiš obraze vseh. Izgovori njihova imena. Slišati hočem, ali jih imaš prav.
