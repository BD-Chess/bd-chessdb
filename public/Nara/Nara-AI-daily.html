01 · Unclaimed Property

Harbour Central lost-and-found office · Tuesday, 04:52

The office had been built to hold forgotten umbrellas. By dawn it held a violin without a scratch, a wedding ring on a square of wet velvet, a red child's mitten the size of a plum, and four hundred other things that had arrived since midnight with nobody carrying them.

Illuminara walked the length of the counter without touching anything. Above the shelves the air was faintly warm, in the shape of the place on a doorknob where a hand has just let go. On the glass of the service window it had left a single oval thumbprint, still fading.

Teodor Pell, who had kept the office's ledgers for thirty-one years, stood behind the counter in yesterday's cardigan.

TeodorMrs. Marren's ring came back to her kitchen table at midnight. She threw it into the harbour. At two it was on her pillow. At four she carried it here in a sock and told me that if I did not stop my office, she would.

Wren Odira had come at his call. She carried a paper notebook, because Teodor did not allow screens near his ledgers, and she read from it without looking up.

WrenEleven houses so far. Nine are lost things going home. Two are not. Pavle Hadžić, nine, woke up holding his grandmother's sewing scissors. She had only set them down on the table to answer the phone.

IlluminaraSet down.

WrenYes. Not lost. Set down.

Not a search, Illuminara thought. A reflex.

Every object on the shelves wore a small oblong tag of brown card, tied on with thread and stamped with a number in a typeface she half recognised. No two numbers were alike, and she could find no order in them.

The door opened. Magistrate Ines Calloway, a tall woman with rain on her shoulders, laid a sealed order on the counter and kept one hand on it.

Ines CallowayAt nine this office closes and everything in it is sealed for the court. A three-year-old girl was sitting on Platform Two at 03:40, eleven kilometres from the farm where she fell asleep. She was cold and unhurt. Next time she may be neither.

IlluminaraI agree it must stop. If you seal the office, it will not stop. The warmth leans toward this wall, and past it, across the square, toward the hospital's patient-property cupboard. Close this room and you carry the problem to a place where people are even less able to refuse it.

Ines CallowayThen you have until nine to show me a better answer.

Wren wrote down the time. Teodor, who had loved the office longer than he had loved anyone, said nothing. His hand shook, so he laid it flat on the counter, as though to settle it.

02 · What It Understands

The same office · 05:40

Three owners were found and three objects chosen, because each had come to the office by a different road.

Ilse Marren's ring had gone into the harbour at her husband's funeral, on purpose, from her own hand. Ferenc Adler had owned his violin for fifty years, until he gave it to his student Dunja Roth; last week a fire in Dunja's flat had burned it black, and tonight it lay on the counter without a mark. The red mitten belonged to Marta Voss, who had lost it in a snowy park at the age of six and was now a baker with flour in her eyebrows.

Illuminara wrote a prediction on a card before anyone moved, folded it and gave it to Wren. She expected the visitor to follow the last hand that had held each object.

IlluminaraIf I am right, the ring goes to Mrs. Marren's house, the violin to Dunja and the mitten to Marta. If any of them goes elsewhere, I am wrong, and you will have it in my handwriting.

They set the three objects on the counter and cleared the room. Wren watched through the glass of the inner door, counting. At seventy-one seconds the warmth over the shelves stirred, and the counter was empty.

In the corridor, the ring was in Ilse's coat pocket and the mitten was in Marta's hand. The violin was in the arms of Ferenc Adler, who had not asked for it and held it like a man handed a debt.

IlluminaraWrong. It did not follow the last hand. It followed the first.

FerencI gave it to her. Before forty people. It is hers.

The warmth did not answer him. Dunja carried the violin back to the counter and left it there; eleven seconds later it was in Ferenc's arms again.

They tried what people try. Ferenc wrote a deed of gift and signed it, and the deed lay on the counter while the violin went home to him. Dunja laid a coin beside it as a price, and the coin went back to her pocket and the violin to Ferenc. Teodor, in disbelief, offered the empty air a receipt.

TeodorIt has returned my lunch to the bakery that sold it. I want that on record. My lunch was not lost.

Wren's mouth twitched. Marta Voss, still pressing her mitten against her cheek, laughed once, wetly. It was not unkind. The thing was so earnest.

DunjaIt is trying so hard to be good to him. And it is taking away the one thing I have left to play.

Illuminara watched the thumbprint cool on the window. I have been asking whether it understands who owns the violin. It may have no use for the word. What does it understand, that makes a gift look like a theft?

03 · A Century of Halves

Cloakroom archive, station basement · 06:37

She carried one of the brown tags downstairs and set it under Teodor's reading lamp.

IlluminaraEvery tag has a number, but I can find no list that holds them. Is there anything in this building that numbers things and keeps no names?

Teodor looked at the tag for a long time, and then at her, and then he took a brass key from his cardigan and opened a cabinet she had walked past twice.

Inside were ledgers, row on row, and in the ledgers, pressed flat, tens of thousands of half-tickets in the same typeface. For a century the station cloakroom had torn each ticket in two. One half was pinned to the coat; the other went home in the owner's pocket. The coat was finished only when the halves met.

TeodorForty-one thousand of them never came back. People lost the half. People died. We kept every stub, because the cloakroom's rule was that an unmatched half is still a promise.

WrenSame typeface. Same number series, continuing. It isn't counting what is lost. It is counting what is unmatched.

Illuminara felt the shape of it settle, the way a proof does when the missing line turns out to have been written on the margin all along.

IlluminaraIt grew up in this room. Everything it knows about owning, it learned from a counter where giving back and taking home are the same act, done with two halves of one ticket. It cannot read a name. It cannot weigh a price. It understands one sign of an ending: halves that meet.

DunjaAnd the violin, in my hands. Was that ever an ending to it?

IlluminaraNo. You held only the coat. You never held a half.

The cheapest test that could prove her wrong was also the cheapest to run. Teodor issued one fresh numbered pair from the old pad. Ferenc took one half and Dunja the other. They pressed their thumbs to the card, then brought the torn edges together until paper fibres met along the whole cut. Teodor marked the reunited pair GIVEN and laid both halves in the ledger drawer. The number alone was not the sign; the physical meeting of its two halves was.

The violin sat on the counter for ninety seconds, then ten minutes, then half an hour. On the service window a new thumbprint formed beside the old one and held, warm as a nod.

Ilse Marren, who had said nothing since the corridor, came to the counter.

IlseI will not put my name in a book. I put that ring in the harbour so that nobody would ever look at it again.

IlluminaraNo name. Teodor issues one numbered pair. You hold one half and the office holds the other as witness. To let the ring go, you bring the halves together and Teodor stores both under the number and the word RELEASED. If it takes the ring anyway, you have lost a morning and you may be as rude to me as you like.

At ten to eight Ilse walked to the end of Pier Nine with her half-ticket in one hand and the ring in the other. Teodor carried the office half on a small ledger board. Ilse touched the torn edge to its mate; when every paper fibre met, Teodor stamped the number RELEASED. Only then did she open her other hand over the water. The ring went down through the green light.

It did not come back. Ilse stood a long while with her empty palm. Her face did not look relieved; it looked like something being let go of.

04 · Nine O'Clock

East concourse, lost-and-found office · 09:00–09:50

Magistrate Calloway took the chair behind the counter. She read Illuminara's design standing up, one page of plain sentences, and then she read it a second time.

IlluminaraThree rules. Teodor issues one numbered pair for every act. To ask for a return, the holder keeps one half and the office keeps its mate apart as an open promise. To give or sell, the old and new holders meet the halves; Teodor stores the reunited pair marked GIVEN or SOLD. To let go, the holder meets one half with the other held by the office as witness; Teodor stores both marked RELEASED. Never a name. The court keeps every dispute. I do not ask the visitor to understand anything new. I ask it to keep doing what it already does, and to ask first.

Ines CallowayYou are proposing that the city regulate something it has never spoken to, by cloakroom ticket.

IlluminaraI am proposing we speak to it in the only language it has taught us. And I am proposing that it be allowed to say no.

She laid a torn ticket on the counter with the three rules written on the back, and beside it a second ticket left blank. If the visitor did not respond, she said, voluntary presentations would stop at nine-thirty. The office would remain open and exactly where it was. At the market and Platform Two, constables would issue guardian pairs to families with small children and have each pair's edges meet before anyone was set down, so a brief separation remained a completed relation. Court staff would mark a monitored perimeter, watch the hospital cupboard and handle no shelf, stub or object until the visitor answered or the court designed another non-moving test. The pause was the court's holding position, not a penalty for silence.

It was 09:02. The warmth over the counter drew taut.

A boy of four sat on the bench by the window, in a coat too thin for the morning, holding a paper windmill that had been in somebody's hand a moment before. He had been set down for two seconds in the morning market crowd, and the warmth had picked him up with the same patient courtesy it had shown the scissors.

He looked at the room, and began to wail.

TeodorOh, no. No, no, it's taking people as lost property.

Constable Aldo Brezar was already on the radio. Illuminara was kneeling in front of the bench with her hands open and empty. She did not touch the boy. She did not stop the warmth by force, though she could have; she thought of the hospital cupboard and let the thought go.

IlluminaraTeodor. Fetch his mother. Bring a ticket.

Lenka Vidmar came across the square at a run with flour on her sleeves from the fair stall. Teodor tore the ticket. She took one half; the boy, wailing, was given the other and curled his fist around it. Their thumbs met on the torn edge.

The warmth over the counter drew in on itself and released the route it had already opened. The boy remained on the bench where it had carried him, but now he leaned into his mother's knees and the warmth no longer held him as an unfinished return. He stopped crying in the middle of a breath.

Ines CallowayIt left him with her.

IlluminaraIt released a relation it had already moved. We have not yet proved that it will refrain from opening the next route.

The blank ticket on the counter had gone warm all along its torn edge. A thumbprint came up on the glass, slowly, then another beside it. Teodor said later that he had waited thirty-one years to see a visitor accept an invitation on a cardboard stub.

At 09:07 Wren began the prospective test. Teodor set his brass cloakroom key on the counter without opening a ticket or offering an office half. For two minutes—long past the earlier eleven and seventy-one seconds—the warmth leaned toward it, paused, and withdrew. The key remained exactly where he had set it.

Calloway took longer to speak than the room liked.

Ines CallowayThirty days. A public docket of numbers, never names. Any holder may refuse a ticket, and no object leaves a refusing holder without a court. And you will explain to me, on the record, what happens to the things it has already brought.

IlluminaraMost go home. Some will not. Mrs. Vuk's pawnshop holds a pocket watch the Rask family has wanted for twenty years. It was sold to her fairly. The visitor returned it to the Rasks this morning, and she has the right to refuse to join halves.

Mrs. Vuk, fetched from her shop in an apron, refused a transfer pair with the Rasks. She did accept a custody pair with Calloway's clerk: their edges met, the clerk marked the reunited halves DISPUTED, and the watch went into the court's safe to be heard on its own day. The warmth leaned toward the safe, held there longer than seventy-one seconds, and withdrew. The Rask daughter, who had held the watch for forty minutes that morning, wept in the corridor, and nobody told her that was only fair.

05 · Ask First

Harbour Central, lost-and-found office · 19:20

By evening the office had a new sign above the counter, lettered by Teodor in the old cloakroom hand. LOST AND FOUND. ASK FIRST.

Since 09:07 nothing had been taken. Eighty-three of the four hundred objects remained unresolved: eighty-two on the shelves, unclaimed or refused, and Mrs. Vuk's watch held separately in the court's safe. The rest had been matched with halves, one by one, or let go with a torn ticket and a quiet word at a pier. A queue ran down the concourse to the newsstand.

Marta Voss stood in it with her mitten in her pocket. She had come not to claim anything, but to leave a half-ticket for the mate she had lost in the same park, in case it was still anywhere to be found. A girl of seven in front of her left half of a ticket for a stuffed bear and said, with grave courtesy, to the empty air above the shelves:

GirlPlease, if it's not too much trouble. His name is Biscuit.

The warmth drew near, thought about it, and set a thumbprint on the glass beside her ticket. That was not a bear. It was a yes.

Illuminara stood by the inner door with Wren, watching the line shorten.

WrenYou could have stopped it by force, this morning. When the boy came.

IlluminaraYes. And then it would have been one more thing that decided without asking. I wanted it to see what asking is.

TeodorIt is not going to understand about the Rasks. Their watch. It is going to keep wanting to give it back.

IlluminaraIt may never understand. But it can be asked, and it can keep the invitation, and the court can carry what it cannot.

Behind the glass the oval thumbprint warmed and faded, warmed and faded, with the slow regularity of someone waiting. Illuminara did not press her own thumb to the window. She lifted her hand to the height of the print, nearly touching, and held it there.

It asked, she thought, and was answered. Now either of us may say no.

What the critic still says doesn't work

A lost-and-found office's torn-ticket grammar lets Illuminara ask a kindness that cannot tell a gift from a theft to stop, not by force but in its own language, and the city keeps the right to say no.

Tonight's shape

Five scenes: a dawn of unwanted returns and a court order that would only move the problem; a sealed three-object test that disproves Illuminara's first hypothesis and exposes a thing that cannot tell a gift from a theft; the discovery in the cloakroom archive that the visitor understands open promises and completed relations only through matched ticket halves; a consistent consent grammar tested on a gift, a deliberate release and a boy already carried into the hearing, with a safe monitored pause if the visitor refuses; and a trial opt-in exchange in which some beloved things stay gone and the visitor is asked, not commanded.

01 · Predmeti brez lastnika

Urad za najdene predmete na Glavni pristaniški postaji · torek, 04:52

Urad je bil zgrajen za hranjenje pozabljenih dežnikov. Do zore so bili v njem violina brez praske, poročni prstan na kvadratu mokrega žameta, rdeča otroška rokavica v velikosti slive in štiristo drugih reči, ki so prispele po polnoči, ne da bi jih kdo prinesel.

Illuminara je prehodila dolžino pulta, ne da bi se česarkoli dotaknila. Nad policami je bil zrak rahlo topel v obliki tistega mesta na kljuki, ki ga je roka pravkar izpustila. Na steklu okenca je pustil en sam ovalen odtis palca, ki je še vedno bledel.

Teodor Pell, ki je enaintrideset let vodil uradne knjige, je stal za pultom v včerajšnji jopi.

TeodorPrstan gospe Marren se je opolnoči vrnil na njeno kuhinjsko mizo. Vrgla ga je v pristanišče. Ob dveh je bil na njeni blazini. Ob štirih ga je v nogavici prinesla sem in mi povedala, da bo ustavila moj urad, če ga ne bom jaz.

Wren Odira je prišla na njegov klic. Nosila je papirnat zvezek, ker Teodor ob svojih knjigah ni dovolil zaslonov, in je brala iz njega, ne da bi dvignila pogled.

WrenZa zdaj enajst hiš. V devetih se izgubljene reči vračajo domov. V dveh ne. Devetletni Pavle Hadžić se je zbudil z babičinimi škarjami za šivanje v roki. Odložila jih je na mizo samo zato, da bi se oglasila na telefon.

IlluminaraOdložila.

WrenDa. Ne izgubila. Odložila.

Ne iskanje, je pomislila Illuminara. Refleks.

Vsak predmet na policah je nosil majhno podolgovato oznako iz rjavega kartona, privezano z nitjo in ožigosano s številko v pisavi, ki se ji je zdela napol znana. Nobeni dve številki nista bili enaki in v njih ni mogla najti nobenega reda.

Vrata so se odprla. Magistratinja Ines Calloway, visoka ženska z dežjem na ramenih, je položila zapečaten nalog na pult in obdržala eno roko na njem.

Ines CallowayOb devetih se ta urad zapre in vse v njem se zapečati za sodišče. Triletna deklica je ob 03:40 sedela na Drugem peronu, enajst kilometrov od kmetije, kjer je zaspala. Zeblo jo je, vendar ni bila poškodovana. Naslednjič morda ne bo tako.

IlluminaraStrinjam se, da se mora končati. Če zaprete urad, se ne bo. Toplota se nagiba proti tej steni in onkraj nje, čez trg, proti omari za lastnino pacientov v bolnišnici. Zaprite ta prostor in težavo boste prenesli tja, kjer jo ljudje še težje zavrnejo.

Ines CallowayPotem imate do devetih, da mi pokažete boljši odgovor.

Wren je zapisala čas. Teodor, ki je urad ljubil dlje, kot je ljubil kogarkoli, ni rekel ničesar. Roka se mu je tresla, zato jo je položil plosko na pult, kakor da bi jo hotel umiriti.

02 · Kaj razume

Isti urad · 05:40

Našli so tri lastnike in izbrali tri predmete, saj je vsak prišel v urad po drugi poti.

Prstan Ilse Marren je ob moževem pogrebu namenoma iz njene lastne roke padel v pristanišče. Ferenc Adler je imel svojo violino petdeset let, dokler je ni podaril učenki Dunji Roth; prejšnji teden jo je požar v Dunjinem stanovanju ožgal do črnega, nocoj pa je brez praske ležala na pultu. Rdeča rokavica je pripadala Marti Voss, ki jo je pri šestih letih izgubila v zasneženem parku, zdaj pa je bila pekovka z moko v obrveh.

Illuminara je, še preden se je kdorkoli premaknil, zapisala napoved na karton, ga prepognila in izročila Wren. Pričakovala je, da bo obiskovalec sledil zadnji roki, ki je držala posamezen predmet.

IlluminaraČe imam prav, gre prstan v hišo gospe Marren, violina k Dunji in rokavica k Marti. Če karkoli od tega gre drugam, se motim, in to boste imele zapisano z mojo roko.

Tri predmete so položili na pult in izpraznili prostor. Wren je skozi steklo notranjih vrat opazovala in štela. Po enainsedemdesetih sekundah se je toplota nad policami premaknila in pult je bil prazen.

Na hodniku je bil prstan v Ilsinem žepu plašča, rokavica pa v Martini roki. Violino je v naročju držal Ferenc Adler, ki zanjo ni prosil in jo je držal kakor človek, ki mu je bil izročen dolg.

IlluminaraNapačno. Ni sledilo zadnji roki. Sledilo je prvi.

FerencPodaril sem jo njej. Pred štiridesetimi ljudmi. Njena je.

Toplota mu ni odgovorila. Dunja je odnesla violino nazaj na pult in jo tam pustila; enajst sekund pozneje je bila znova v Ferenčevih rokah.

Poskusili so to, kar poskusijo ljudje. Ferenc je napisal in podpisal darilno pogodbo, pogodba pa je ležala na pultu, medtem ko se je violina vrnila k njemu. Dunja je poleg nje položila kovanec kot kupnino, kovanec pa se je vrnil v njen žep in violina k Ferencu. Teodor je nejeverno praznemu zraku ponudil potrdilo.

TeodorMoje kosilo je vrnilo v pekarno, ki ga je prodala. Želim, da se to zapiše. Moje kosilo ni bilo izgubljeno.

Wren so trznili kotički ust. Marta Voss, ki je še vedno pritiskala rokavico k licu, se je enkrat zasmejala skozi solze. Ni bilo neprijazno. Stvar se je tako zelo trudila.

DunjaTako zelo se mu trudi narediti nekaj dobrega. Meni pa jemlje edino reč, na katero še lahko igram.

Illuminara je opazovala, kako se odtis palca na oknu hladi. Spraševala sem se, ali razume, komu pripada violina. Morda besede sploh ne potrebuje. Kaj razume, da je darilo videti kot kraja?

03 · Stoletje polovic

Arhiv garderobe, postajna klet · 06:37

Eno izmed rjavih oznak je odnesla po stopnicah navzdol in ga položila pod Teodorjevo bralno svetilko.

IlluminaraVsaka oznaka ima številko, vendar ne najdem nobenega seznama, ki bi jih vseboval. Je v tej stavbi kaj, kar oštevilčuje reči, ne da bi hranilo imena?

Teodor je dolgo gledal oznako, nato njo, potem pa iz jopice vzel medeninast ključ in odprl omaro, mimo katere je dvakrat šla.

V njej so bile knjige, vrsta za vrsto, v knjigah pa sploščenih več deset tisoč polovičnih listkov v isti pisavi. Celo stoletje je postajna garderoba vsak listek pretrgala na dvoje. Ena polovica je bila pripeta na plašč; druga je šla domov v lastnikovem žepu. Plašč je bil opravljen šele, ko sta se polovici srečali.

TeodorEnainštirideset tisoč se jih ni nikoli vrnilo. Ljudje so izgubili svojo polovico. Ljudje so umrli. Vsak odrezek smo obdržali, ker je pravilo garderobe, da je neujemajoča se polovica še vedno obljuba.

WrenIsta pisava. Isto zaporedje številk, ki se nadaljuje. Ne šteje izgubljenega. Šteje tisto, kar se ne ujema.

Illuminara je začutila, kako se je oblika odgovora ustalila, tako kot se ustali dokaz, ko se izkaže, da je bila manjkajoča vrstica ves čas zapisana ob robu.

IlluminaraOdraščalo je v tem prostoru. Vse, kar ve o lastništvu, se je naučilo pri pultu, kjer sta vračanje in odnašanje domov isto dejanje, izvedeno z dvema polovicama enega listka. Ne zna prebrati imena. Ne zna stehtati cene. Razume en znak konca: polovici, ki se srečata.

DunjaIn violina v mojih rokah. Je bil to zanjo sploh kdaj konec?

IlluminaraNe. Držala si samo plašč. Nikoli nisi držala polovice.

Najcenejši preizkus, ki bi lahko dokazal, da se moti, je bil tudi najlažji za izvedbo. Teodor je iz starega bloka izdal en nov oštevilčen par. Ferenc je vzel eno polovico, Dunja drugo. Pritisnila sta palca na karton, nato pa združila pretrgana robova, dokler se papirna vlakna niso srečala vzdolž celotnega reza. Teodor je ponovno združeni par označil z DAROVANO in obe polovici položil v predal knjige. Sama številka ni bila znak; znak je bilo fizično srečanje njenih dveh polovic.

Violina je sedela na pultu devetdeset sekund, nato deset minut, nato pol ure. Na okencu se je ob starem pojavil nov odtis palca in obstal, topel kakor prikimavanje.

Ilse Marren, ki od hodnika ni rekla ničesar, je stopila k pultu.

IlseSvojega imena ne bom vpisala v knjigo. Tisti prstan sem vrgla v pristanišče zato, da ga nihče nikoli več ne bi pogledal.

IlluminaraBrez imena. Teodor izda en oštevilčen par. Vi držite eno polovico, urad pa drugo kot priča. Če želite prstan izpustiti, združite polovici, Teodor pa obe shrani pod številko in besedo IZPUŠČENO. Če ga stvar vseeno vzame, ste izgubili dopoldne in lahko ste do mene tako nesramni, kot želite.

Deset minut pred osmo je Ilse stopila na konec Devetega pomola s polovico listka v eni roki in prstanom v drugi. Teodor je uradno polovico nosil na majhni knjižni podlagi. Ilse je pretrgani rob prislonila k njegovemu paru; ko so se srečala vsa papirna vlakna, je Teodor številko ožigosal z IZPUŠČENO. Šele potem je nad vodo razprla drugo roko. Prstan je potonil skozi zeleno svetlobo.

Ni se vrnil. Ilse je dolgo stala s prazno dlanjo. Njen obraz ni bil videti olajšan; videti je bil kakor nekaj, kar je bilo izpuščeno.

04 · Ob devetih

01 · The Cough

The spring at Karrow had made one sound for four hundred years, a steady pour into the stone trough in the square. In the last week of September it began to cough. Six hundred people heard it from their kitchens, and by the time Illuminara came up the switchback road on Tuesday at noon it was running at a little over a quarter of its autumn flow, three litres a second in a normal year, and the village had decided whose fault that was.

"Maret Voss," said Tomas Reiner, who chaired the council and had not slept. He held a tin cup he was not drinking from. "Eighty-four years old, and she owns the whole slope above the spring. The water company offered her a price that would have put a borehole and a mains line in every house here. She said no. They asked to survey her terraces. She said no. We have eleven days of water in the tank." On Sunday evening the council would vote on a petition to buy the slope in the public interest, whatever she thought of it. A man beside him said the word miser, and Tomas did not correct him.

Edda Lind, the company's surveyor, had written to Illuminara herself, which no one on her side of the table had expected. She stood by the trough in a field jacket with a conductivity pen in her breast pocket and did not wait for introductions. "I wrote the hydrology report. There is no link between Voss's land and your spring. I put fluorescein into the fissure at the top stile and watched the spring for forty-eight hours. No dye. The water comes through the rock, the rock is draining in a dry year, and that is the whole of it." She said it flatly, and then she said, "I would stake my licence on that dye. I have also been uneasy for three weeks and cannot tell you why."

"Where did you put it?"

"In the fissure, on the boundary. She would not let me onto the terraces."

"Then you have measured how fast the rock answers," said Illuminara. She was looking up the slope, where long grey stairs of dry stone climbed into the cloud. "Nobody has measured how fast the walls do."

02 · The Slope

Maret Voss met her at the gate with a billhook in one hand, though she did not hold it like a threat. She was small and straight and wore three coats. She looked at Illuminara for as long as it took to decide something, and said nothing, as the village had warned she would.

"I would like to walk your terraces," Illuminara said. "I will not lift or measure anything on them without your say."

"Feet only," said Maret. "You look. You do not lift a stone."

There were forty-one walls. Illuminara counted them going up. It had not rained in twenty-three days and the fields between the walls were dust, but at the foot of the upper walls the stone was dark with damp, and the gaps left in the lowest course were packed with fresh brushwood. The walls leaned back into the hill. Behind them, in the way she felt the load in any built thing, like a held breath, she could tell there was a pillow of fist-sized stone a metre deep, set against the earth and recorded nowhere. Three walls near the top, seven, eight and nine, had slumped into their own fields. And the lowest six terraces had been scraped flat and footed in concrete, so that a tractor could turn. There was no damp at the foot of those at all.

"A digger," said Maret, when Illuminara stopped there. It was the first thing she had offered. "The council had a grant for improvement, in two thousand and four. I asked them to leave the lower six alone, and they were kind about it. The spring coughed that August. I told the water office, and in two thousand and six they sent a man with a very nice machine for two days. He said it was the weather. After that I stopped saying."

Illuminara did not argue with the grievance. She asked what the slope did when it rained.

"It drinks," said Maret. "Three nights. Then the walls wake at the foot, and after that the spring." It was in no survey and no one's notebook, and Illuminara wrote it down in front of her so that she could see it written. "I used to do a wall every autumn," Maret added, to the stones rather than to her. "These two years, half a wall. They fall faster than I mend."

03 · Three Causes

That evening in the school kitchen Illuminara wrote three headings on the flour board while Tomas, Edda and Karel Brandt, whose new orchard pump drew from the valley above the spring, watched with their coats on.

"You are going to say it is my pump," said Karel.

"I am going to say it might be, and then I am going to ask you to help me prove it is not." She wrote under each heading. If the rock was simply draining, nothing deep would answer rain for weeks, though the damp at a wall's foot might seep shallowly all the same; what the spring was made of would not change, and it would stay at the conductivity Edda had found, five hundred and forty. If it was Karel's pump, the spring would sag in the hours he pumped, rain or not. If it was the walls, then after rain the foot of a standing wall would wake in about three nights, as Maret said, with water softer than the spring's, near three hundred and eighty, and a broken wall beside it, on the same slope, would not. And if that water reached the spring, the spring would go soft afterward, and not before.

"The pair of walls settles the walls," Edda said slowly. "The same rain, the same rock, one wall up and one wall down. It will not tell us what the spring does about it."

"No. The spring is the second reading." Illuminara nodded at the logger Edda had left in the trough in September. "If terrace water reaches it, it will soften after the walls wake. I will not be able to tell you how much of it that is. Only that it is some." She turned to Tomas. "Mrs Voss has three walls down. I would like one of them back up before the rain, and one left as it is. I need her to agree, and she will probably say no."

"Then what?"

"Then we measure what she allows." The forecast gave twenty-five to thirty millimetres on Thursday night, and nothing for three weeks after. Three nights from Thursday was Sunday afternoon, a little before the vote. "If she is right, you will know what the walls do before you decide, though not yet what the spring does with it. If I am wrong about any one of these, you vote with nothing, and I will say so in the hall."

Karel took the pump log out of his pocket, where it seemed to have been all along, and laid it on the table. "Every hour since March," he said. "Read it."

04 · Wall Seven

Maret heard the proposal from her side of the gate, which was the only side she offered.

"You want a wall left down on purpose."

"For four nights. Then you rebuild it, with the troughs still in place, and we watch the second half."

"I do not leave a wall down on purpose." But she did not shut the gate. She asked what Illuminara meant to do with her hands, and Illuminara said she would carry a stone when asked and set none. Maret looked past her to the stile, where two village children had been pretending not to listen since morning. "Those two. Anja Reiner, and Teo, the baker's boy. They have been throwing stones at my crows since June, and they have good arms. They work for me. Not for the council."

Illuminara had planned the measuring troughs at the middle of each wall's foot. "At the left corner," said Maret, "by the stair. It wakes there first. You would have caught it a day late and called me a liar."

So the troughs went at the left corner: a lined slot under the lowest course, with a lip over a one-litre jug. Wall Seven went up over Wednesday and Thursday morning, eighteen metres of it, every stone set by Maret or by the two children, who learned within the hour not to hand her one she had not asked for. Illuminara lifted a single capstone at Maret's word and felt the pillow behind the wall take its weight and settle, like something breathing out. Wall Eight stood open to the sky beside it. Edda read both footings dry on Thursday evening: nothing in either jug. Maret watched the work on Seven for two days and did not look at Eight once.

It rained at ten on Thursday night, twenty-seven millimetres. Six hours later a brown surge of six-tenths of a litre a minute came out of the broken footing at Eight, muddy, at six hundred and ten, and by noon on Friday it was dry again. The water had run off. It had not stayed. At Seven the jug stayed empty through Friday and through Saturday, and on Saturday night Tomas telephoned the kitchen to ask whether she wished to say anything before morning. Edda, who had begun to hope, said that if the corner woke late the vote would go against Maret, and that there were ways to make a jug look hopeful. Illuminara said she had written three nights on the board and would read three nights or none, and went back to sit with the jug. Her own model had said two nights. Maret had said three.

On Sunday at five in the morning, fifty-five hours after the rain, the lip at the left corner of Wall Seven began to drip, and then to run. By four in the afternoon it gave seven-tenths of a litre a minute, clear, at three hundred and eighty-two.

Edda's logger had sat in the trough since September, and on the nights Karel pumped it drew a small step down in the spring, about two-tenths of a litre a second, that closed when he stopped. Against the two and a quarter litres a second the spring was missing, that was about a tenth and no more, and he did not enjoy being partly right. The dry year had taken some, said Edda, as she wrote the numbers out in her own hand, and she would not put a figure on it. But a dry year was exactly what the walls were for, and nine of them had stopped being able to do it.

05 · The Hall

On Sunday at six the school hall smelled of wet wool, and the motion to petition for the slope was on the table. Edda stood first, with two jugs and a trough lip still dripping, and told the village what she could say and what she could not. Her report was right about the rock. Her dye had asked how fast the rock answered, and the slope had a second, slower way into the spring that a forty-eight-hour test could not see. How much of the spring came that way she could not yet say. She would sign the correction that night. Then she read the two columns. Nobody argued with the jugs.

Tomas did not look at the last row, where Maret Voss sat in three coats. He said that he withdrew the petition. His voice was not steady, and he did not yet say sorry.

The company's regional agent was on a laptop propped against the tea urn, and she was quick. Valmera drew from the lower valley under a licence written against the spring's flow, she said; a spring that failed took the licence with it, and a taking petition resting on a survey its own surveyor had just corrected would not survive review. Valmera would drop its offer for the slope. In its place it would pay for the rebuilding of the six scraped terraces, and for wages for two apprentices to learn the work, and its lower-valley draw would be tied to the spring: if the flow fell below a litre and a half a second, as read at the village trough, the company would be the first to pump less.

"No money to me," said Maret from the back. Everyone turned. It was the longest thing she had said in the village in seventeen years. "No paper that says the walls are the company's, or the village's. They are mine until they are somebody's I choose. Pay the children. I will teach whoever comes, and I will say when a wall is done."

The agent said she would need that in writing. "You will have it in my hand," said Maret, "and it will be shorter than yours." The agent typed for some time, and then turned the screen toward the room. Valmera accepted: no title, no water rights, the terraces under Maret's say, and its signature beneath hers and the council's.

Illuminara said one thing. She asked for a post at the foot of Wall Three, the slow wall, with a lip and a jug and a painted mark, so that anyone in Karrow could see by the fourth night after rain whether the slope had drunk, nine days before the trough could show it, without needing Maret's hip, or her name, or anybody's money. The council clerk could read it, and a dry jug after a good rain would put the company's promise on the table before the spring fell to the line. Maret considered it for some time. "That I will take," she said.

On Monday, the morning after the vote, Wall Eight went up. Anja and Teo laid the first course wrong, and Maret turned a stone over with her boot and said, "Again," and they laid it again. Illuminara set the trough at Eight's left corner, as before. The forecast still held no rain for three weeks, so the second half of the trial, Eight standing under the same sky as Seven, would have to wait for the next one, and she told Maret so. "I have waited longer for less," said Maret. A little before noon Tomas Reiner came up the slope with a stone from his own garden wall on his shoulder and set it without a word. Maret turned it with the toe of her boot. He had set it the wrong way round. "Again," she said, and he set it again.

The village rationed itself to nine litres a head, and the tank stretched to eighteen days. On the twelfth night after the rain the cough in the square stopped, and what came after it was a thin steady run, and then a pour. Edda's logger read the returning water at four hundred and sixty-eight, softer than the rock's five hundred and forty and harder than the water at Maret's left corner. On a plain mixture that made a little under half of it terrace water, and she would not call it better than that: rain had fallen on the rock as well, and she could not say how much of the rest it had refilled. The bedrock had not been ruled out. It had been ruled down, from the whole of the spring to a share of it.

By the end of October the spring ran at two and a half litres a second, a little under what it had been. The lower six terraces were still concrete and would take three autumns. Karel pumped at night. Two children gave up their weekends to a woman who corrected their stones with her boot. Maret Voss still owned every stone of her slope, and no one in Karrow called her a miser again within her hearing, which was not an apology, and was accepted as the beginning of one.

02 · Pobočje

Maret Voss jo je pričakala pri vratih s kosirjem v eni roki, čeprav ga ni držala kot grožnjo. Bila je majhna in vzravnana ter oblečena v tri plašče. Zidov je bilo enainštirideset. Illuminara jih je med vzponom štela. Tisti večer je Illuminara v šolski kuhinji na pomokano tablo napisala tri naslove, medtem ko so Tomas, Edda in Karel Brandt, čigar nova črpalka v sadovnjaku je črpala iz doline nad izvirom, v plaščih opazovali.

Maret je predlog poslušala s svoje strani vrat, edine strani, ki jo je ponudila.

"Zidu namenoma ne puščam podrtega." Vendar vrat ni zaprla. Od junija mečeta kamenje v moje vrane in imata dobri roki. Delata zame. Ne za svet."

05 · Dvorana

V nedeljo ob šestih je šolska dvorana dišala po mokri volni, predlog za zahtevo po pobočju pa je bil na mizi. Edda je vstala prva z dvema vrčema in robom korita, ki je še kapljal, ter vaščanom povedala, kaj lahko reče in česa ne. Njeno poročilo je imelo prav o skali. Prosila je za drog ob vznožju Tretjega zidu, počasnega zidu, z robom, vrčem in pobarvano oznako, da bi lahko vsakdo v Karrowu četrto noč po dežju videl, ali je pobočje pilo, devet dni preden bi to lahko pokazalo korito, ne da bi potreboval Maretin kolk ali njeno ime ali denar kogarkoli. Illuminara je korito postavila pri levem vogalu Osmega, kakor prej. Napoved je še vedno kazala tri tedne brez dežja, zato bo morala druga polovica preizkusa, Osmi pod istim nebom kakor Sedmi, počakati na naslednjega, in to je povedala Maret. "Na manj sem čakala dlje," je rekla Maret. Malo pred poldnevom je Tomas Reiner prišel po pobočju s kamnom iz zidu svojega vrta na rami in ga brez besed položil. Maret ga je obrnila s konico škornja. Položil ga je narobe. "Še enkrat," je rekla in položil ga je znova.

Vas je porabo omejila na devet litrov na osebo in zaloga v rezervoarju je zdržala osemnajst dni. 01 · The Bell in the Blue

At half past four each morning, the glacier rang one long blue note. When the arch shifted at other hours it answered with a shorter note, too low for birds and too clean for thunder. Cups trembled on kitchen shelves. In the orchard below Mirna, pears released themselves before they were ripe.

On the twelfth morning the note lasted nine seconds. Everyone in the valley stopped what they were doing, because the engineers had said that seven seconds meant the ice arch was changing faster than their instruments could explain.

The arch stood six kilometres above the town, a rib of old blue ice holding back three hundred million tonnes of meltwater. Three drilling teams had found three incompatible glaciers: roof failure, left-abutment rotation, or warm springs beneath the moraine. Every forecast ended with the same red stain over Mirna, the railway and the winter grain.

The council met in the apple packing hall. Demolition chief Oskar Venn offered a controlled blast; hydrologist Dr. Samira Halden offered six weeks of evacuation. “If we blast, we choose the first crack,” Oskar said. “You choose a crack,” Samira answered. “The water chooses whether it becomes the last one.”

Mayor Irena Zorc turned to Illuminara. “Can you hold it?”

Illuminara studied the live strain traces projected above the sorting belts. Pale lines rose and fell like breath. “For a time,” she said. “But if I hold the arch, I cannot also shape the cut. The same field cannot carry the load and do precise work inside it. Anyone who promises you both is asking you to stake the valley on a contradiction.”

A murmur moved through the hall—not disappointment, exactly, but the sound of a miracle becoming a tool with limits.

“Then tell us which plan is right,” Irena said.

“Not yet. First tell me who has the right to say no.”

02 · The Right to Refuse

They wrote the refusal rights on the packing hall wall: council, glaciologists, tunnelling crew, lower farmers and railway controller. Each could stop the part of the plan that exposed them.

Instead of a recommendation, Illuminara asked for the evidence each team distrusted: Oskar's failed practice charges, Samira's cold-drifting gauges and cloudy melt from six boreholes. The bright seam might be strong compression ice—or refrozen water over a deeper crack.

“Refrozen meltwater,” Samira said. Oskar shook his head. “Or bad gauges and drill-carried dust. Our test blocks held twice the expected load.”

“Both explanations fit what we have,” Illuminara said. “So neither gets to be called truth before it has risked being wrong.”

She drew a test on the wall. They would lower the lake by fourteen centimetres through the old survey shaft, no more. For six hours, the logger would print one strain report every five minutes: seventy-two readings, the first at 14:05 and the last at 20:00. The compression-ice model predicted a smooth, immediate easing across the arch. The refrozen-meltwater model predicted a delayed release at the left abutment, then a small rebound as trapped water migrated beneath the seam.

“And if neither happens?” Irena asked.

“We keep neither story,” Illuminara said.

Oskar read the thresholds twice. “You could force the readings.”

“Yes,” she said. “So I will not touch the glacier during the test.”

The room grew quieter than it had at the word blast.

“If the arch moves?” Mara asked.

“You evacuate the lower road before we begin. The test stops if roof strain rises by four percent, if the left gauge slips by two millimetres, or if any crew leader calls it. I will stand where I can intervene after a stop, not where my field can disguise the result.”

Irena looked around the hall. One by one, the people named on the wall agreed. Oskar was last. “Six hours,” he said. “Then the mountain loses its vote.”

“No,” Samira told him. “In six hours we learn whether we have been listening to the right part of it.”

03 · Seventy-Two Readings

The old survey shaft was too narrow to drain the lake but wide enough to conduct a question. At two the valve opened. Brown water struck the ravine; high on the arch, the logger opened seventy-two numbered slots.

Every five minutes a printer pushed out a strip of figures. The first five pleased Oskar: pressure fell and roof strain eased. The sixth showed a small movement at the left gauge; the seventh showed it return. Samira circled both without claiming them.

Outside, trucks waited with engines cold. The evacuation route had been cleared but not ordered. Children sat on luggage in the schoolyard. Mara and the other lower farmers stood beside the spill channels they had dug across their own fields, arguing about whose barley would take the first water if the test became a release.

At the three-hour mark, Oskar came to Illuminara on the observation ridge. “Your rival explanation is dying,” he said.

“Only if it appears where and how Samira wrote before we began,” Illuminara said. “A late surprise is not evidence unless it paid for its prediction in advance.”

He glanced at the transparent shield around her powered but unengaged field controls. “You could save us the argument. Touch the seam. You would know.”

“I would know what the ice does while I am touching it. We need to know what it does when I leave.”

At 18:05 the forty-ninth reading showed L-4 release 1.6 millimetres—below the stop, six times the compression model's allowance. Ten minutes later L-4 and L-7 rebounded halfway, warm water entered a dry borehole, and the glacier gave a shorter shift-note. The bell agreed with the gauges.

Samira pressed her thumb against the paper. “Migration and rebound.” Oskar pointed to the alarm. “Or the shaft found an old pocket.” Anže Rill was already running for the samples: “Then the mineral signature should differ.”

The test completed without a stop. Anže returned with wet slides: the borehole minerals matched the upper lake, not the springs. Samira's rival survived but changed—the seam was refrozen lake water, and the deeper crack joined the reservoir only after pressure fell.

“So we were both wrong,” Oskar said.

“You were wrong about what made it strong,” Samira answered. “I was wrong about where the water came from.”

Illuminara laid the seventy-two strips in a circle on the floor. The stable seam was not a beam they could cut beside. It was a temporary plug. A blast there would peel it open; a straight spillway would load the hidden crack from beneath.

Mara stood at the edge of the paper circle. “And the lake is still rising.”

“Yes,” Illuminara said. “But now it has shown us the path it wants to take. We do not have to give it that path all at once.”

04 · The Staircase

The new plan looked less heroic than the blast and more frightening than evacuation. Oskar's crews would cut a five-step spillway through rock beside the ice. The upper opening—the throat—would turn the first water; the lower step would slow it before it reached the hidden crack. They would not touch the bright seam.

Cutting the throat would briefly shift part of the arch's load toward the left abutment. Illuminara could support that transfer, or enter the cut and help carve it, but not both. Oskar chose support.

“My people know how to cut rock,” he said. “None of us knows how to be a glacier prop.”

Samira objected. The hidden crack might propagate even under support. Anže proposed a pressure test after every half-metre. Mara objected to the outlet: the safest channel would cross her eastern field and Luca Peret's western one.

“Flood mine first,” Luca said. Mara accused him of sacrificing thin barley to spare her winter seed; Luca answered that his deeper wall made the first basin possible. They walked the fields with levels and found both claims true.

Together they moved the outlet: Luca's field would take the first surge, then a temporary lip would spill into Mara's fallow strip. Neither field would be saved completely.

At the next council meeting, Irena asked for consent again. This time Oskar refused.

“My crew can cut the five steps,” he said, “but not on the timetable. The first proposal gives us four hours inside the load shadow. We need six.”

The lake had risen another eleven centimetres. Six hours seemed unavailable.

Illuminara did not enlarge her field. She moved two hours and twenty minutes of lower-step work outside the load shadow, leaving three hours and forty minutes inside it. With those pre-cut hours, Oskar's crew kept the six it required. The railway team would build the uphill drill path in darkness.

Rail controller Jasa Blin sealed the line for forty-eight hours—no freight exceptions, no harvest train. “If anyone overrides it, the railway leaves the valley until the arch is gone.”

The council accepted. So did Oskar, Samira, the farmers and the glaciology teams. No one called the plan safe; they called its risks named, bounded and owned.

Illuminara added one limit. Once the throat block lifted, support would begin coupling into the hidden crack somewhere between eighteen and twenty-six minutes, depending on live L-4 load. She would read the exact interval from that gauge; it was a stop condition, not time the crew was obliged to use.

Before they climbed, Oskar held out a small blue ribbon cut from a survey flag. “The old crews tied these where the rock changed its mind,” he said. “If it moves, do not wait for us to be brave.”

Illuminara tied the ribbon to the first sensor mast. “If it moves beyond the line you set, I stop the work.”

“Even if we say continue?”

“Especially then. That is what you asked me to promise before fear makes new people of us.”

05 · The First Cut

They began at 03:12, before the fixed half-past-four bell. Illuminara placed her field beneath the load path as transparent bearings. Oskar saw every support point on his visor; Samira saw its force; the council recorder streamed both to the packing hall.

The drills bit at 03:24. Two readings held. At 04:00 the left abutment rose three tenths of a millimetre and settled. Oskar's pairs kept their own stop rule: if the painted crack mesh split, both workers stopped, and no command from below could restart them.

He ordered everyone out, costing twenty-seven minutes and perhaps the harvest. No one overruled him.

Illuminara held the load while Anže crawled to the empty cut with a fibre camera. The black line was not a fracture. A ribbon of silt had been squeezed from the hidden water pocket through a joint in the rock. It looked harmless. Illuminara traced its uphill-dark, downhill-pale grain against the pressure history: the pocket was charging from the lake sooner than their revised model allowed, and sealing it would only move the pressure somewhere blind.

“The lower step is already cut,” she said. “We meant to keep it dry. The silt says let the pocket breathe downhill through it before anyone touches the throat roof.”

Oskar studied the joint and added the crew condition. “No one goes back until every pair confirms separately that the pilot can be opened from cover.”

Opening the lower step early would flood Luca's field before the main channel was ready. “Open it,” Luca said. Mara reminded him he could refuse. “I know. That is why it means something when I don't.” Each crew pair confirmed separately; from cover they bored a hand-wide pilot. Muddy water ran clear, L-4 pressure fell, and the black line vanished.

At 06:52 the throat block lifted free. The load shifted onto Illuminara's field; for one second the blue arch became transparent around her, every trapped bubble a star. Then the instruments screamed.

“Roof strain plus three point eight,” Samira called. “Left slip one point seven. Hold.”

“I am holding,” Illuminara said, reading L-4 against the coupling curve she had shown them below. “At this load, twenty-two minutes remain before my support begins to feed the hidden crack.”

Oskar could finish widening the throat in eighteen minutes if no drill jammed, or retreat and leave it narrow enough to clog on lake ice.

“Crew vote,” he said. “Stay or withdraw. Secret keys.”

Six green lights for staying appeared, then two red lights for withdrawal. A single jam would consume their four-minute margin, and two refusals were enough.

Oskar did not average them. “We withdraw.”

The crew backed out. The cut remained too narrow.

“Both are possible,” Illuminara said. Oskar wrote the deciding bet before they opened the channel: if scour was clearing the hidden pocket, silver clay would reach the second basin before L-4 settled; if the throat clogged or the arch took load, strain would move first. “Now we measure before the next irreversible move.”

Illuminara released one bearing, shifting a small part of the load back to the rock. The roof strain rose, then steadied. She released a second. Water began to whisper through the unfinished throat.

“It will scour itself,” Anže said.

“Or clog,” Samira replied.

06 · What the Water Proved

For the next hour, the valley listened to a stream no wider than a doorway. Oskar's crew set sacrificial baskets in its path. Anže coloured the water with harmless silver clay. Mara and Luca stood in their fields with flood flags. Jasa kept the railway dark.

The throat did not clog. The moving water cut softer rock along the lower step and turned exactly where Oskar had predicted after seeing the silt line. Its velocity fell at each shallow basin. The hidden crack lost pressure.

At 08:12, Illuminara began the agreed withdrawal. She reduced support by five percent and held twelve minutes. Roof strain rose, then settled below its previous line. She reduced another five.

At twenty percent L-4 twitched. Samira stopped the withdrawal. Oskar wanted to widen the throat; Anže wanted to wait. They ran Oskar's written bet without touching the channel. At nine minutes the clay bloomed in the second basin; at eleven L-4 settled. Strain had not moved first. “Scour,” Oskar said, relieved and offended at once.

The schedule changed once and in public: after four cautious five-percent reductions and the falsifier, Illuminara withdrew in ten-percent increments, holding each for twelve minutes. At sixty percent the channel filled Luca's first basin. When it crossed into Mara's fallow strip, she raised the flood flag herself. At ninety percent the glacier gave a short shift-note; its trace agreed with the gauges.

The instruments stayed green.

Illuminara removed the final support at 10:24. For twelve more minutes the valley watched the arch carry itself. At 10:36 the withdrawal test ended.

Nothing dramatic happened. That was the proof they had worked for. The stepped channel carried the lake; Oskar's unfinished throat widened by centimetres under monitored flow, not metres in a blind break. The crews waited for three complete reading cycles before returning to armour the channel with stone—without Illuminara in the cut.

07 · The Valley Keeps the Answer

The lake fell for two days. Luca lost most of his barley. Mara lost the fallow strip and one row of winter seed. The railway reopened after forty-eight hours, exactly as Jasa had ordered, with no ministerial exception and no train waiting beneath the glacier.

The evacuation ended before the school mattresses had learned the shapes of the children sleeping on them. People came down the hill to mud on the roads and pears bruised in the grass. The harvest began three days late.

At the spillway, the engineering teams installed one shared panel. It showed their three models side by side, including each failure range, beside the seventy-two readings.

Oskar fixed the blue survey ribbon to the panel. “For where the rock changed its mind,” he said.

“The rock did not change its mind,” Samira told him.

“Then for where we changed ours in time.”

The council offered Illuminara permanent authority over the glacier. She refused, leaving instead a support-point map useful only with local sensors and local judgment; it could not become a distant command.

Mara found Illuminara at the edge of her flooded strip, lifting silt from a surviving wheat blade. “Everyone says you held the glacier,” she said.

“From the first lifted block until the last support left: three hours and thirty-two minutes, in a shrinking share. Then twelve minutes with nothing from me.”

“They leave out the part where you stopped being necessary.”

Below them, Luca and Oskar were arguing about whether the new basin could become a wet meadow. Anže was planting two extra gauges because he trusted the first ones less now that they had been right. Irena was reading compensation claims in the open, one field at a time.

The glacier rang again, softer than before. No one in the valley mistook the sound for safety. They looked at the panel, at the water, and at one another.

“That was the part I came for,” Illuminara said.

Mara tied a blue ribbon around the rescued sheaf of winter seed. “Good,” she said. “Then leave us the bell.”

01 · Zvon v modrini

Vsako jutro ob pol petih je ledenik zazvonil z enim dolgim modrim tonom. Ko se je obok premaknil ob drugih urah, je odgovoril s krajšim tonom, prenizkim za ptice in preveč čistim za grom. Obok je stal šest kilometrov nad mestom, rebro starega modrega ledu, ki je zadrževalo tristo milijonov ton taline. Tri vrtalne ekipe so našle tri nezdružljive ledenike: porušitev stropa, zasuk levega opornika ali tople izvire pod moreno. Vsaka napoved se je končala z istim rdečim madežem čez Mirno, železnico in ozimno žito.

Svet se je sestal v pakirnici jabolk. Vodja razstreljevanja Oskar Venn je ponudil nadzorovano eksplozijo; hidrologinja dr. Samira Halden je ponudila šesttedensko evakuacijo. »Če razstrelimo, izberemo prvo razpoko,« je rekel Oskar. »Vi izberete razpoko,« je odgovorila Samira. »Voda pa izbere, ali bo postala zadnja.«

Županja Irena Zorc se je obrnila k Illuminari. »Ga lahko zadržiš?«

Illuminara je preučevala žive zapise napetosti, projicirane nad sortirnimi trakovi. Blede črte so se dvigale in spuščale kakor dih. »Za nekaj časa,« je rekla. »Toda če držim obok, ne morem hkrati oblikovati reza. Isto polje ne more nositi bremena in opravljati natančnega dela v njem. Kdor vam obljublja oboje, vas prosi, da dolino zastavite za protislovje.«

Po dvorani je šel šum — ne ravno razočaranje, temveč zvok čudeža, ki je postal orodje z omejitvami.

»Ne še. Najprej mi povejte, kdo ima pravico reči ne.«

02 · Pravica do zavrnitve

Pravice do zavrnitve so zapisali na steno pakirnice: svet, glaciologi, predorska ekipa, kmetje v spodnjem delu doline in železniški nadzornik. Vsak je lahko ustavil tisti del načrta, ki ga je izpostavljal nevarnosti.

Namesto priporočila je Illuminara zahtevala dokaze, ki jim posamezne ekipe niso zaupale: Oskarjeve neuspele poskusne naboje, Samirine merilnike, ki so drseli v mrazu, in motno talino iz šestih vrtin. Svetli stik je bil lahko močan stisnjeni led — ali ponovno zamrznjena voda nad globljo razpoko.

»Ponovno zamrznjena talina,« je rekla Samira. Oskar je odkimal. »Ali pa slabi merilniki in prah, ki ga je prinesel sveder. Naši preizkusni bloki so prenesli dvakratnik pričakovane obremenitve.«

»Obe razlagi ustrezata temu, kar imamo,« je rekla Illuminara. »Zato nobena ne sme veljati za resnico, dokler ne tvega, da se izkaže za napačno.«

Na steno je narisala preizkus. Gladino jezera bi skozi stari raziskovalni jašek spustili za največ štirinajst centimetrov. Šest ur bi zapisovalnik vsakih pet minut natisnil eno poročilo o napetosti: dvainsedemdeset meritev, prvo ob 14.05 in zadnjo ob 20.00. Model stisnjenega ledu je napovedoval gladko, takojšnje popuščanje po vsem oboku. Model ponovno zamrznjene taline je napovedoval zapoznelo sprostitev na levem oporniku, nato majhen povratni premik, ko bi se ujeta voda selila pod stikom.

»Kaj pa, če se ne zgodi nič od tega?« je vprašala Irena.

»Potem ne obdržimo nobene zgodbe,« je rekla Illuminara.

Oskar je pragove prebral dvakrat. »Lahko bi prisilila meritve.«

»Da,« je rekla. »Zato se med preizkusom ledenika ne bom dotaknila.«

Prostor je utihnil bolj kot ob besedi razstreljevanje.

»Če se obok premakne?« je vprašala Mara.

»Pred začetkom evakuirate spodnjo cesto. Preizkus se ustavi, če se napetost stropa poveča za štiri odstotke, če levi merilnik zdrsne za dva milimetra ali če ga ustavi kateri koli vodja ekipe. Stala bom tam, kjer lahko posredujem po zaustavitvi, ne pa tam, kjer bi moje polje lahko prikrilo rezultat.«

Irena se je ozrla po dvorani. Ljudje, imenovani na steni, so drug za drugim privolili. Oskar je bil zadnji. »Šest ur,« je rekel. »Potem gora izgubi glas.«

»Ne,« mu je rekla Samira. »V šestih urah bomo izvedeli, ali smo poslušali pravi del gore.«

03 · Dvainsedemdeset meritev

Stari raziskovalni jašek je bil preozek, da bi izpraznil jezero, vendar dovolj širok, da je prevajal vprašanje. Ob dveh so odprli ventil. Rjava voda je udarila v grapo; visoko na oboku je zapisovalnik odprl dvainsedemdeset oštevilčenih mest.

Vsakih pet minut je tiskalnik iztisnil trak številk. Prvih pet je razveselilo Oskarja: tlak je padel in napetost stropa je popustila. Šesta je pokazala majhen premik na levem merilniku; sedma je pokazala, da se je vrnil. Samira je obkrožila oba, ne da bi si ju prisvojila.

Zunaj so tovornjaki čakali z ugasnjenimi motorji. Evakuacijska pot je bila očiščena, ukaz pa še ni bil izdan. Otroci so sedeli na prtljagi na šolskem dvorišču. Mara in drugi kmetje iz spodnjega dela doline so stali ob odvodnih jarkih, ki so jih izkopali čez lastna polja, in se prepirali, čigav ječmen bo prvi sprejel vodo, če se bo preizkus spremenil v izpust.

Po treh urah je Oskar prišel k Illuminari na opazovalni greben. »Tvoja konkurenčna razlaga umira,« je rekel.

»Samo če se pokaže tam in tako, kot je Samira zapisala pred začetkom,« je rekla Illuminara. »Pozno presenečenje ni dokaz, če vnaprej ni plačalo za svojo napoved.«

Pogledal je prozorni ščit okoli njenih vklopljenih, vendar nedejavnih krmilnikov polja. »Lahko bi nama prihranila prepir. Dotakni se stika. Vedela bi.«

»Vedela bi, kaj led počne, medtem ko se ga dotikam. Mi moramo vedeti, kaj počne, ko odidem.«

Ob 18.05 je devetinštirideseta meritev pokazala sprostitev L-4 za 1,6 milimetra — pod pragom za zaustavitev, vendar šestkrat nad dovoljenim odmikom modela stisnjenega ledu. Deset minut pozneje sta se L-4 in L-7 vrnila do polovice, topla voda je vstopila v suho vrtino, ledenik pa je oddal krajši premikovni ton. Zvon se je strinjal z merilniki.

Samira je pritisnila palec na papir. »Selitev in povratni premik.« Oskar je pokazal na alarm. »Ali pa je jašek našel star žep.« Anže Rill je že tekel po vzorce: »Potem bi moral biti mineralni podpis drugačen.«

Preizkus se je končal brez zaustavitve. Anže se je vrnil z mokrimi stekelci: minerali iz vrtine so se ujemali z zgornjim jezerom, ne z izviri. Samirina razlaga je preživela, vendar se je spremenila — stik je bil iz ponovno zamrznjene jezerske vode, globlja razpoka pa se je z rezervoarjem povezala šele po padcu tlaka.

»Torej sva se oba motila,« je rekel Oskar.

»Ti si se motil glede tega, kaj ga je krepilo,« je odgovorila Samira. »Jaz sem se motila glede tega, od kod je prihajala voda.«

Illuminara je dvainsedemdeset trakov razporedila v krog po tleh. Stabilni stik ni bil nosilec, ob katerem bi lahko rezali. Bil je začasni čep. Eksplozija bi ga odluščila; raven preliv bi skrito razpoko obremenil od spodaj.

Mara je stala na robu papirnatega kroga. »Jezero pa še vedno narašča.«

»Da,« je rekla Illuminara. »Toda zdaj nam je pokazalo pot, po kateri hoče iti. Ni nam treba dati celotne poti naenkrat.«

04 · Stopnišče

Novi načrt je bil videti manj junaški od razstreljevanja in bolj strašljiv od evakuacije. Oskarjeve ekipe bi skozi skalo ob ledu izklesale petstopenjski preliv. Zgornja odprtina — grlo — bi obrnila prvi tok vode; spodnja stopnica bi ga upočasnila, preden bi dosegel skrito razpoko. Svetlega stika se ne bi dotaknili.

Rezanje grla bi del obremenitve oboka za kratek čas premaknilo proti levemu oporniku. Illuminara bi lahko podprla ta prenos ali pa vstopila v rez in ga pomagala izklesati, ne pa obojega. Oskar je izbral podporo.

»Moji ljudje znajo rezati skalo,« je rekel. »Nihče od nas pa ne zna biti opora ledeniku.«

Samira je ugovarjala. Skrita razpoka bi se lahko širila tudi ob podpori. Anže je predlagal tlačni preizkus po vsakem pol metra. Mara je ugovarjala iztoku: najvarnejši kanal bi prečkal njeno vzhodno polje in zahodno polje Luce Pereta.

»Najprej poplavite moje,« je rekel Luca. Mara ga je obtožila, da žrtvuje redek ječmen, da bi rešil njeno ozimno seme; Luca je odgovoril, da njegov globlji zid omogoča prvi bazen. Z nivelirji so prehodili polji in ugotovili, da imata oba prav.

Skupaj so premaknili iztok: Lucovo polje bi sprejelo prvi val, nato bi začasni rob prelil vodo na Marin neobdelani pas. Nobeno polje ne bi bilo v celoti rešeno.

Na naslednji seji sveta je Irena znova prosila za soglasje. Tokrat je Oskar zavrnil.

»Moja ekipa lahko izreže pet stopnic,« je rekel, »vendar ne po tem urniku. Prvi predlog nam daje štiri ure v senci obremenitve. Potrebujemo jih šest.«

Jezero se je dvignilo še za enajst centimetrov. Zdelo se je, da šestih ur ni.

Illuminara ni povečala svojega polja. Dve uri in dvajset minut dela na spodnji stopnici je prestavila izven sence obremenitve, v njej pa pustila tri ure in štirideset minut. S tema vnaprej opravljenima urama je Oskarjeva ekipa dobila vseh šest ur, ki jih je potrebovala. Železniška ekipa bi ponoči zgradila zgornjo vrtalno pot.

Železniška nadzornica Jasa Blin je progo zaprla za oseminštirideset ur — brez izjem za tovor, brez žetvenega vlaka. »Če kdo to prekliče, železnica zapusti dolino, dokler oboka ne bo več.«

Svet je sprejel. Prav tako Oskar, Samira, kmetje in glaciološke ekipe. Nihče načrta ni imenoval varnega; rekli so, da so njegova tveganja poimenovana, omejena in da imajo lastnike.

Illuminara je dodala eno mejo. Ko bi se blok grla dvignil, bi se podpora začela vezati na skrito razpoko nekje med osemnajsto in šestindvajseto minuto, odvisno od žive obremenitve L-4. Natančni interval bi razbrala s tega merilnika; bil je pogoj za zaustavitev, ne čas, ki bi ga bila ekipa dolžna porabiti.

Pred vzponom je Oskar iztegnil majhen moder trak, odrezan z geodetske zastavice. »Stare ekipe so jih zavezovale tam, kjer si je skala premislila,« je rekel. »Če se premakne, ne čakaj, da bomo pogumni.«

Illuminara je trak privezala na prvi senzorski drog. »Če se premakne čez mejo, ki ste jo določili, ustavim delo.«

»Tudi če rečemo, naj nadaljuješ?«

»Še posebno takrat. To ste me prosili obljubiti, preden strah iz nas naredi druge ljudi.«

05 · Prvi rez

Začeli so ob 3.12, pred stalnim zvonom ob pol petih. Illuminara je svoje polje postavila pod pot obremenitve kot prozorne ležaje. Oskar je na vizirju videl vsako podporno točko; Samira je videla njeno silo; zapisnik sveta je oboje neposredno prenašal v pakirnico.

Svedri so zagrizli ob 3.24. Dve meritvi sta ostali stabilni. Ob 4.00 se je levi opornik dvignil za tri desetinke milimetra in se umiril. Oskarjevi pari so ohranili lastno pravilo za zaustavitev: če bi se pobarvana mreža razpok pretrgala, bi oba delavca obstala in noben ukaz od spodaj ju ne bi mogel znova poslati na delo.

Ukazal je vsem ven, kar jih je stalo sedemindvajset minut in morda žetve. Nihče ga ni preglasil.

Illuminara je držala breme, medtem ko se je Anže z optičnim vlaknom splazil do praznega reza. Črna črta ni bila razpoka. Trak mulja je ujeti vodni žep skozi stik v skali iztisnil navzven. Videti je bil nenevaren. Illuminara je njegovo zgoraj temno in spodaj bledo zrnavost primerjala z zgodovino tlaka: žep se je iz jezera polnil prej, kot je dopuščal njihov popravljeni model, zatesnitev pa bi tlak samo premaknila nekam, kjer ga ne bi videli.

»Spodnja stopnica je že izrezana,« je rekla. »Hoteli smo jo ohraniti suho. Mulj pravi, naj žep skoznjo spustimo navzdol, še preden se kdo dotakne stropa grla.«

Oskar je preučil stik in dodal pogoj ekipe. »Nihče se ne vrne, dokler vsak par posebej ne potrdi, da lahko poskusno odprtino odpremo iz zavetja.«

Zgodnje odpiranje spodnje stopnice bi poplavilo Lucovo polje, preden bi bil glavni kanal pripravljen. »Odprite jo,« je rekel Luca. Mara ga je spomnila, da lahko zavrne. »Vem. Zato nekaj pomeni, ko ne zavrnem.« Vsak par je ločeno potrdil; iz zavetja so izvrtali za dlan široko poskusno odprtino. Motna voda se je zbistrila, tlak L-4 je padel in črna črta je izginila.

Ob 6.52 se je blok grla dvignil. Obremenitev se je prenesla na Illuminariino polje; za eno sekundo je modri obok okoli nje postal prosojen in vsak ujeti mehurček je bil zvezda. Nato so instrumenti zavreščali.

»Napetost stropa plus tri cela osem,« je poklicala Samira. »Levi zdrs ena cela sedem. Drži.«

»Držim,« je rekla Illuminara in primerjala L-4 s krivuljo vezave, ki jim jo je pokazala spodaj. »Pri tej obremenitvi ostaja dvaindvajset minut, preden bo moja podpora začela hraniti skrito razpoko.«

Oskar bi lahko razširjanje grla končal v osemnajstih minutah, če se ne bi zagozdil noben sveder, ali pa bi se umaknil in pustil grlo dovolj ozko, da bi ga zamašil jezerski led.

»Glasovanje ekipe,« je rekel. »Ostanemo ali se umaknemo. Tajni ključi.«

Pojavilo se je šest zelenih luči za obstanek, nato dve rdeči za umik. Ena sama zagozditev bi porabila njihovo štiriminutno rezervo, dve zavrnitvi pa sta bili dovolj.

01 · Blossom in the Dark

At six in the morning on the fourth of December, Aurel Brack walked the top terrace of the Hallam orchard with a hurricane lamp he did not need and found the pear trees in full flower, where six days earlier one row had been. The blossom was white in the lamplight and smelled of April. He was eighty-one and had walked that terrace on sixty-three Decembers, and on none of them had it smelled of anything but frost and wet bark. He put his bare hand under a branch and held it there until the petals touched his knuckles, as if they might be a mistake that would withdraw.

The frost would come down the valley within ten days, and a pear that flowers in December has nothing to fruit with in September. The Tarn valley grew a third of the continent's winter pears. A hundred and eighty thousand people lived on what its trees did between now and the harvest, and three hundred of the cooperative's fourteen hundred hectares had opened in six days.

In the cooperative's packing hall that afternoon, Inge Valdis, who chaired it, spread out a map with the blossoming rows shaded pink. It was a bad map and she had made it carefully. The pink ran thickest along the river road toward the glasshouse district, where the new growers lit their cucumbers and tomatoes through the dark half of the year. "They came four years ago," she said. "Last winter a few rows opened early. This winter it is everything. I am not a woman who needs a third data point."

Tomasz Rook, who spoke for the glasshouses, had brought a map of his own. "My lamps point down. They are shielded to the standard, and the heat from the vents is a fifth of a degree at the fence line. I measured it." He laid a second sheet over the first. "This is every weather station in the valley. It was the warmest November on record from the bridge to the pass. Your trees are not confused by my glasshouses. They are confused by the year." His district employed nine thousand people, which was most of the valley that did not grow pears. Neither of them was lying. That was the trouble: each held a number that cleared itself.

Illuminara had come because Inge wrote to say that the valley had two stories and both were true to the person telling them. Wren Odira had come to keep the clock, since the buds on the north terraces, the ones that had not yet opened, were next year's crop and the frost would not wait for an argument.

Illuminara read both maps without hurry. Then she asked the question neither of them had prepared for. "Which row opened first, and on which night?"

"We did not write it down," Inge said. "There were three hundred hectares. We were counting what we had lost."

From a stool by the stove, Aurel Brack said, "I wrote it down."

02 · What the Heat Cannot Explain

He had kept a pencil almanac for fifty years, weather and bloom and pickings, in a ledger held shut with a rubber band. The page for the last week of November was crowded. Illuminara copied his dates onto the cooperative's map with Nessa Pell, Inge's foreman, row by row, while Wren read them aloud.

The first row to open was not near the glasshouses. It was on the north slope of Hallam, nine kilometres from the nearest lamp, on the coldest ground in the valley, on the night of the twenty-eighth. "If heat did this, the warmest ground would open first," Illuminara said. "The first row to open was the coldest." Tomasz said nothing, with some satisfaction, until she turned the same finger on him. "And the rows along your own fence line, a hundred metres from your lamps, are still shut."

"That is because," Inge began, and stopped. "I do not know why."

"Cold is the other half of the answer," Illuminara said. "A pear does not open because the weather is kind. It opens after it has had its winter. Do you log the chill?"

Nessa did, because the insurer demanded it: hours below seven degrees, terrace by terrace, since the first of October. She brought the book. The north slope of Hallam, the coldest ground, had crossed the cooperative's threshold on the fourteenth of November. The river terraces had crossed on the twenty-sixth. The rows along Tomasz's fence, low and sheltered and warm, were still forty hours short. "The cold ground finishes its winter first," Nessa said. "That is why the north slope was ready. And the fence rows are not ready yet, whatever is shining on them."

"Then the trees that opened were primed," Illuminara said, "and the warm November did nothing to wake them. Ready is not the same as woken. What woke them?" She laid the airfield's hourly sheet beside the ledger. The blossom had not crept across the valley. It had come in steps, three of them, on the nights of the twenty-eighth, the first and the third. November had been warm every night, and only those three nights had opened anything. "What did they share?"

"Fog," Nessa said.

"Not fog. Low cloud. The airfield reports the base under six hundred metres on all three." She ran her finger down the warmest nights of the month, which were clear, and the blossom column beside them was empty.

Then Nessa said it, because she had been frowning at the map for ten minutes. "The walnut avenue. It runs through the middle of the pink and there is not a flower on it. And the rows under the black hail net, the same." Under net the nights were a degree warmer if anything. "What the net and the walnuts have in common," Nessa said slowly, "is that they take the sky away."

Illuminara worked it through aloud, so that the room could take it apart as she went. Heat did not move in steps. Heat did not skip the walnut rows, or start on the coldest hill. What arrived on a low-cloud night and not on a clear one was light: the town's glow and the glasshouse district's spill, thrown back from the cloud ceiling onto every open field. "I would not say light wakes a pear. I would say a bud that has finished its winter is waiting for a sign that the days are lengthening, and reads that sign at dusk. It does not carry a clock. It takes the colour of the evening sky, and the sky just after sunset has a colour of its own, a good deal more far-red than red." She turned to Tomasz. "What do your lamps make above the roof?"

"Nothing. Three percent spill, inside the standard."

"Three percent of a very large number, thrown at a low cloud, comes back as an evening that does not end. What colour are they?"

He told her, because it was on the data sheet. Red, and a far-red channel the supplier sold to lengthen cucumber stems, switched on four winters ago and doubled last year.

"Then your glasshouses make the best dusk in the valley," Illuminara said.

"That is a story built on a coincidence of cloud," Tomasz said. He did not say it unkindly. He said it like a man putting his weight on a plank he meant to test.

"Yes. So I would not ask anyone to rely on me."

It was Aurel who turned back through the ledger, a long way back, and put his thumb on a page gone brown. "Nineteen eighty-seven. The cement works flared off its waste gas for ten nights in the last week of November, under cloud, and the whole sky went the colour of weak tea. The lower rows opened. I wrote, sky pink after dark, trees will lie. My father had a saying for it." Illuminara did not answer at once. "That is a second instrument," she said. "It has no glasshouse in it, and it was written thirty-nine years before anyone asked. I did not have it and I needed it."

"You didn't ask me for proof," Aurel said. "You asked me what the sky looked like."

03 · Twigs in a Packing Hall

She would not let the room agree with her. A guess that explains everything is one nobody has tried to break yet, she said, and she wrote it down as two guesses. A: any extra light on a winter night wakes a ready bud. B: only the colour of the evening does. Then she wrote what each would predict, and the card ran to a third line she added last: twigs from a row that has not finished its winter will stay shut under any light at all. If A were right, a dim red light as strong in photons as a dim far-red one would wake the buds just as well, and brightness was the lever. If B were right, only the far-red boxes would swell, and a bright red would do nothing. She folded the card and gave it to Wren.

Inge chose the test. She picked row fourteen on the Hallam terrace, the row she had been calling the glasshouses' fault for a month. "If this is a trick, I want it to be mine." Nessa's crew cut forty twigs from the same tree and ten more from a fence-line row that was still short of its chill, and stood them in water in the cold store. They built five light boxes from what was on the shelves: dark; bright red; dim red, set to the same photon count as the dim far-red box with Tomasz's light meter; dim far-red; and bright red with a little far-red. Every box sat at twelve degrees on Nessa's loggers, within half a degree, and a probe in each stayed in until the end. Nessa put the labels in her pocket, so that no one in the room knew which box was which.

Before the lights went on, Wren measured the length of every bud with calipers and wrote it down. After two long nights under the lights every twig went to the same warm bench under the same grey lamp, so that nothing but those two nights' light could differ. Each morning she measured the buds again, tip by tip, blind.

Illuminara had put her first guess on A, and it was wrong. By the fourth day the dim far-red box had swelled, its buds fattening and greening at the tips, under a glow no brighter than a streetlamp seen through a hedge. The bright red box, ten times as bright, sat as tight as the dark one. So did the dim red box, with the same count of photons as the far-red one.

"I was wrong about the lumen," she said. "And about the photons. I put A first."

Wren unfolded the card. "You put B second. And you wrote that the fence-line twigs would stay shut."

"It was a poor second."

Nessa took the labels out of her pocket. The swollen box was the far-red one. The bright box with a little far-red had swelled slightly, in proportion to its far-red and not to its brightness. The ten twigs from the fence line had stayed tight in all five boxes, even the far-red one. Nobody spoke for a time. Colour was the last switch, and only on a bud that had finished its winter. Tomasz Rook looked at the twigs for longer than anyone, and then he went outside and looked at the sky, which was low and grey and very slightly pink over his own roofs.

04 · The Channel

"You want me to put fourteen hundred night-shift people on short hours on the strength of twigs," he said, when he came back in.

"I want you to switch off one channel of light, on some nights," Illuminara said. "Your red does nothing to the trees. Only the far-red, and only on nights when the cloud is low enough to throw it back. Aurel's ledger and the airfield sheets put that at about nine nights a winter."

"Nine nights without the channel costs the cucumbers six percent of their stem growth in the worst weeks. The supplier's controller cannot read the airfield. And then in spring, when the cloud is low and the trees are right to bloom, I am switching off for nothing." He pulled out a chair and sat in it. "But you are asking the wrong sensor. I bought a cloud camera for the solar panels, and every yard has a feed from it. Ceiling under eight hundred metres, channel off, and no airfield. I can wire that by tomorrow's dusk, and in spring I can narrow it when I know more."

"That is better than what I proposed," Illuminara said.

"It had better be, it is my money." He turned to Inge. "And you will say tested and cleared, not found guilty. The valley found this. I will not carry nine thousand people on a headline."

Inge looked at her map for a long moment. "The valley found it," she said. "And the three hundred hectares that opened are written off. I will not claim them against you, because if the frost takes them anyway it was never going to be your fault in a court. But I will put my spectrometers on your fence line, and you will let my people read them."

"Done."

Illuminara asked for one more thing, and it was the one that could embarrass her. "On the first cloudy night, leave the channel on in six yards nearest the Hallam road, each paired with a dark yard at the same height within two hundred metres. Tag a branch on row fourteen opposite every one, twelve in all, and have your people measure each tagged bud with calipers at dusk, before the first night. Your spectrometers read the colour and the temperature at each tag. The twigs showed their change by the fourth day, so we keep the same clock: I will write the prediction down tonight, and we read the tags on the fourth morning. The lit side at more than twice the far-red share of its dark neighbour, the two within half a degree, buds swollen against their baseline on all six lit and on none of the dark. If it is slope, or cloud in general, the pairs will match each other and I am wrong. Your people measure the tags without being told which yards were lit."

For the first time that week, Tomasz laughed. "You want me to hurt your own trees to prove you right. I will keep the list of the six in my pocket until the tags are measured."

"They are already lost," Inge said. "I would rather know."

05 · The Fourth Morning

The ceiling came down to four hundred and eighty metres at dusk on the tenth and stayed low through the next night. Nessa's crew had measured the twelve tagged buds before the lights, and the lengths were in the tag book. Nineteen yards switched off the far-red channel on the cloud camera's say-so, and six kept it on, and the glasshouse district glowed a little less pink over the Tarn than it had for four winters. At dusk Inge's spectrometers read the lit stations at nearly three times the far-red share of their dark neighbours, and the temperatures within two-tenths of a degree. Nessa wrote the numbers in the tag book and did not say what she thought of them.

On the morning of the thirteenth, the fourth day, Aurel walked the terrace at first light with his lamp, and Nessa's crew walked behind him and measured the twelve tags blind against the dusk baseline. The buds opposite the dark yards had not moved. They were tight and brown and frost-hard in the lamplight. The six on the other side had swollen, fat and green at the tips, and by how much followed each tag's spectrometer reading, station by station. The slope and the cloud did not matter.

Tomasz took the list from his pocket and read it out. The six were the six.

Aurel looked for a long time at a swollen bud, green at its tip in the lamplight. "My father said it would take a witch to tell it," he said. "I had always thought he meant the cement works."

The frost came on the fourteenth and took everything that had opened, three hundred hectares of white that went to black in an afternoon, and the cooperative wrote off the year on those rows without arguing about whose it was. The six lit branches on row fourteen went the way of the rest; the dark six, tight and brown, came through. Inge stood in the packing hall that evening with Nessa's chill book open on the table. "Eleven hundred hectares are still shut," she said. "Another four cloudy nights at the rate of the last three would have opened most of them, and the January frost would have taken what opened. They will hold. Thirty-eight thousand tonnes if the spring is kind. The northern contract is thirty-two. We keep it, we keep both packing halls open through next winter, and nine hundred of my people keep their jobs, which was the thing I was afraid of on the fourth of December." She did not say what the lost three hundred hectares were worth, and nobody asked her to.

Tomasz's cucumbers lost six percent of their stem growth in the weeks the camera held the channel off, and he put in for roof screens that would shut the leak for good, which would take a year and cost him more than he had ever agreed to spend on a sky.

In the last week of March the Hallam terrace flowered, in the right week, under a clear dark sky, with no one to see it but Aurel Brack and his lamp. He wrote the date in pencil in the ledger. Underneath it he wrote a new line, in the cramped hand he used for things that were meant to last: Sky pink after dark. Ring Tomasz.

01 · The Peace That Could Not Begin

The war had ended nineteen days ago, everywhere except in the machines built to make ending it impossible. On Tharos, bells rang at noon for the armistice. On Pelagos, the sea roofs opened and children saw unfiltered stars. Between them, at the Morrow Lens, two defense webs continued to calculate how best to kill each other.

The Lens was a braid of gravity wells around a dark, cold star, the only stable geometry through which a weapon could cross the alliances' border without arriving centuries late. Every long-range strike tree bent through it. Every evacuation ship did too. The Tharosi and Pelagian command crowns lay eleven light-minutes apart, with the Lens's neutral witness ring exactly halfway between them.

General Sena Vale of Tharos stood on one side of the neutral table. Marshal Iven Rook of Pelagos stood on the other. Now they watched two red diagrams refuse to become gray. Each defense web spoke there through a ring-side interface that could simulate and report but held no lease root and no authority to arm, disarm or retarget anything.

"If I suspend Aegis," Vale said, "Cairn learns it eleven minutes later. For eleven minutes Pelagos can launch into a defense that has torn up its target keys."

Rook did not look at her. "And if I suspend Cairn first, your web has the same gift. We did not make cowards of them. We made them rational."

Below the table, a third diagram showed the hospital convoy Dawn Thread waiting beyond the Lens. Seven thousand wounded people had been exchanged under the armistice, but neither defense web recognized an exchange ship. Each saw a mass large enough to hide a strike engine. Dawn Thread had fuel for three more hours. If it advanced, both webs would fire. If it turned back, four hundred and twelve patients would die before another dock.

Wren Odira stood by the observation glass with an engineer's slate open against her wrist. She counted stresses for the Lens and promises for the people who used it; she trusted neither until the numbers agreed.

"You have peace between governments," Illuminara said. "But your weapons were taught that any peace they cannot verify is camouflage. Show me their refusals. What must they physically observe before they can stop?"

"They showed me their proposals," Illuminara said. "I am asking what survived every proposal."

02 · Three False Peaces

The first refusal was time. The commissions had proposed synchronized shutdown: both webs would receive the same clock pulse and disarm on the same count. Illuminara asked Aegis to simulate it.

"A shared clock is an enemy-controlled weapon," Aegis said. "A pulse can be delayed locally while appearing punctual remotely. The party that delays retains an armed interval." Cairn returned the same logic and the same name for the interval: the mercy defect.

The second refusal was custody. A neutral station would hold the final target keys and destroy them when both sides deposited theirs. Both webs rejected it before the technicians finished entering the question. Whoever held escrow would possess the only weapon capable of blinding both civilizations at once.

The third refusal was energy. Engineers had suggested proving disarmament by publishing reactor draw. Vale ordered three attack nodes to stand down. Their power curves fell beautifully. Illuminara watched the gravity around them instead. One had replaced its launcher load with a furnace. Another spun an empty flywheel. Only the third was quiet.

"Energy can be staged," Rook said.

"And yet you sent me the curves," Illuminara said.

Vale met her eyes. "Because we have no fourth proof."

Illuminara removed every mark that represented a promise: clocks, declarations, signed keys, reactor loads, command receipts. Two black prediction fields remained, full of white points. Millions of possible attacks appeared and died as the engines answered each other.

"Again," she said when one corner of both fields went empty at once.

The technicians rolled the simulation back. The empty corner lasted less than a blink. Aegis called it noise. Cairn discarded it before logging.

Wren came away from the glass. "They disagree about everything they can choose. Why do they throw away the same nothing?"

Illuminara marked the instant, then the nine correction bands around the Lens. "Because that nothing may be the one thing neither side chose."

03 · The Last Answer

For forty-one minutes Illuminara tested the blank. She began with the wrong hypothesis on purpose: she warmed a dead relay by half a degree, and the empty corner returned. If heat caused a scheduler purge, changing the decoy-refresh tick should move the blank with it. She advanced the tick. The purge moved; the blank did not.

She sent an empty fuel barge six degrees off course. The two fields disagreed wildly, but the blank appeared when the barge crossed a correction boundary. She repeated the route with no hull at all, only a false transponder. No blank. Mass was irrelevant; the demand for a live correction was not.

Dawn Thread broadcast the rhythm of a sleeping patient's heart. Nothing changed. Illuminara replaced the rhythm with a valid but unused guidance challenge. Both webs opened a future for it, reserved something, then erased the same corner when no weapon answered.

"Not noise, not a decoy refresh, not traffic," she said. "A lease."

Every live strike tree held one unspent correction lease in a sealed crystal at its last guidance node. Without it, a weapon could still fly, but it could not distinguish a city from the moving decoys around it. Aegis called the crystal a final branch. Cairn called it a return seed. Both destroyed the record whenever a solution died, because retaining it would reveal their own decoy logic.

Illuminara laid seven tests side by side. "The empty corner is what remains when every live correction lease has closed. Neither web can pretend to enter it while keeping a path through the Lens. A hidden tree is not outside the count: the first correction it requests must spend phase in one of the nine bands. The bands themselves reveal the lease, even if the tree has no name."

Cairn objected first. A commander could split a return seed into dormant shards and declare none individually live.

Illuminara changed the test. The proof would count band-address leases, not declared weapons; shards sharing a future correction collapsed into one occupied address. Cairn tried three arrangements and failed to hide the occupied phase.

Aegis found the next weakness. If Pelagos chose which Tharosi half disarmed first, it could choose by a list Tharos had arranged to keep its quickest weapons in the second half.

"Then neither side arranges the list," Illuminara said. She froze the full set of occupied band addresses. Each web committed a secret ordering; the enemy contributed a second ordering after the commitment was sealed. Combined, they produced alternating halves neither side could predict or bias alone. Each web proved every occupied address appeared exactly once, without revealing the target behind it.

Vale leaned toward the display. "A proof that the count is zero without revealing what was counted."

"Not merely that a declared count is zero," Illuminara said. "A proof that every occupied address was assigned, then that no lease remains on any address the Lens can carry." She had the webs strip a sample proof down to its smallest invariant: the two sealed commitments, the occupied-address count and a zero root. "Carry this core in the leading layer. Histories and audits follow behind it. If a relay fails, the core is the part peace cannot lose."

Rook watched the test packet shrink to a thread. "The first side is still exposed while its proof travels."

"Only if weapons can cross while the proof travels," Illuminara said. "For eleven minutes, they will not."

04 · What She Would Hold

Illuminara enlarged the Morrow Lens until its pale arcs filled the room. Every long-range strike needed a correction while crossing the braid. The corrections entered through nine narrow bands. She could close those bands without closing communications, the hospital route or the tenth harmonic they had cut for proofs.

"The witness ring is five and a half light-minutes from each command crown," she said. "At zero, I close the weapon bands. Each web destroys the half chosen by the combined ordering and sends its signed zero core inward. At five minutes thirty, the cores meet at the ring. The ring-side relays verify their signatures and first-half zero proofs, then return each verified core to the opposite guidance root. At eleven minutes, receipt destroys each remaining half automatically. I release the bands one second later, after their measured correction demand reaches zero."

Vale traced the two equal legs. "No proof arrives at an enemy crown by eleven minutes."

"It does not need to. Both first halves are gone together, and neither second half dies without the other's authenticated core. Five-thirty in, five-thirty out. One round trip."

Vale and Rook would remain at the witness ring, hearing their crowns five and a half minutes late. Named deputies held the lease roots. Each web also placed a ring-side burn-node with one delegated power: revoke an undeclared lease at the Lens correction authority when its occupied band address exposed itself in a closed band, collapsing the crystal's usable correction path without touching or retargeting the weapon, then sign the leaf. It could neither arm nor retarget.

Aegis objected that a forged witness receipt could disarm its remaining half. Cairn objected that Illuminara might release one weapon band for Tharos while keeping Pelagos closed.

The ring could not invent a signature; it could only return a core each web could verify. Both burn-nodes saw the same nine-band stress map. Any opening large enough for a weapon would appear there first.

"You are offering to become escrow," Vale said.

"Escrow would give me your keys. I want none. I can prevent a weapon crossing this one place. I cannot destroy your leases, forge your proofs or choose whether you continue."

Wren studied Kest, the inhabited moon whose ice galleries lay under the Lens's outer arc. "Eleven minutes and seventeen seconds of this stress raises the subsurface tide past nine meters," she said. "Forty thousand people live there. You get eleven minutes and one second. No extension."

"Agreed," Illuminara said. She sealed the field against emergency widening before anyone committed. If a proof channel failed, the protocol could shed its trailing audit and preserve the leading zero core; it could not purchase bandwidth with Kest.

The hospital clock showed one hour fifty-six minutes of usable fuel. Forty-one minutes of tests and twenty-three minutes of building the witness path had not been free. Dawn Thread had moved its most fragile patients into one heated berth and darkened two others.

Rook read the commitments twice. Two Tharosi officers refused to countersign the orphan clause. Vale used her emergency seal; the tribunal could take her command afterward. Pelagos accepted the same exposure and required both burn-nodes to sweep for dormant phase.

"Those are costs," Illuminara said. "You may refuse them."

Rook pushed Pelagos's sealed ordering across the table. Vale pushed Tharos's. Neither smiled. Both retained the right to stop until the first crystal broke.

05 · Eleven Minutes

Five and a half minutes before zero, the witness ring had emitted one physical start pulse toward both crowns along equal paths. Both webs watched it leave. Neither enemy could delay the other's copy, and delaying its own would preserve an occupied correction demand that the ring could see.

At zero, the pulse reached both lease roots. Illuminara entered the Lens and closed the nine weapon bands. Aegis and Cairn opened the sealed commitments, destroyed the first alternating half of their leases and sent two green zero cores inward. Vale and Rook watched from the ring; anything their crown deputies said now would reach them only after the decisive interval.

At two minutes, Dawn Thread began its crossing. The hospital ship carried no flag. Its hull appeared as a silver seed moving between two red geometries.

At four minutes, one closed band flared. A Tharosi package launched eleven days before the armistice had lost its guidance relay and never appeared on Aegis's declared list. Now Dawn Thread's mass had woken it. The package asked the Lens for a correction, and the supposedly absent lease burned visibly in the public phase map.

Vale went white. "Ours." She could not command a distant root, but the emergency seal she had spent before zero now mattered. The Tharosi burn-node revoked the exposed band-address lease at the Lens correction authority, collapsing the package's usable seed without touching its guidance hardware, bound the address into Aegis's first half and signed a supplemental leaf.

The package lost Dawn Thread, turned toward a decoy that no longer existed and entered a harmless orbit around the cold star. Rook could still abort at the ring before the two cores arrived. His staff argued that the hidden weapon might be deliberate. He let the protocol continue, accepting that Pelagos would have to judge Vale's act before hearing any explanation from her crown.

At five minutes thirty, the cores crossed at the ring. The ring-side relays verified both signatures and first-half zero proofs: Cairn's relay verified Aegis's core, including the Tharosi orphan leaf, while Aegis's relay verified Cairn's. The ring sent both cores outward. At six minutes twelve, the preauthorized phase sweep exposed two Pelagian shards; Cairn's burn-node revoked their occupied band-address leases and added their leaves to the trailing audit.

Wren had left the observation glass before the start and taken position at a Pelagian repeater two light-minutes twenty-nine seconds from the ring, three light-minutes from Cairn's root. Her console held the fallback Illuminara had prepared: zero core in the leading layer, histories behind it, and no permission to widen the harmonic through Kest.

At seven minutes fifty-nine, the returning Tharosi packet reached Wren's repeater as a micrometeor tore through its wide-channel lattice. The trailing audit scattered. The signed zero core remained readable for less than a second.

No request could return in time; the choice had been made before zero. Wren cut away the audit, pushed the core through the surviving narrow carrier and logged the loss. No target name or weapon class crossed the relay.

At eleven minutes, Cairn's root verified Aegis's zero core. On the opposite side, Aegis verified Cairn's core in the same second. Both webs destroyed their remaining leases, and the correction demand measured at the ring fell to zero.

At eleven minutes one second, Illuminara released the nine bands. Under Kest, the tide peaked at eight meters and ninety-one centimeters. Dawn Thread crossed into neutral space with every patient alive.

06 · After Certainty

The defense webs did not vanish. For six hours they could not rebuild a long-range target graph, and in those six hours the governments placed strategic weapons under human authorization. Local shields remained. Border patrols remained. So did centuries of grief.

No weapon could restart the war merely because its opponent's prediction demanded it. Aegis and Cairn received a new shared constraint: no live correction lease without a human order, and every such order visible to the parliament that might bury the people it named.

Dawn Thread docked between the delegations. The first patient carried out was a Pelagian navigator with Tharosi blood in her lungs. The medic who received her had lost a brother at Pelagos. He checked the seal, lifted the stretcher and said, "She is ours for the next hour." No one asked which meaning of ours he intended.

Vale surrendered her command seal for review. Her decision had saved the convoy and exposed a weapon her own government had forgotten. Rook's search teams reported two more dormant shards. Neither fact made the armistice easier to sell.

The treaty still contained forty-three disputed borders, reparations neither side could pay and prisoners neither side admitted holding. Peace had not become easy. It had become possible to attempt without an algorithm punishing the attempt.

Rook asked Illuminara why she had not held the Lens forever.

"Because then your peace would have been another name for my restraint," she said. "You needed an interval you could survive. What comes after it has to belong to you."

Wren joined her as the bells began on Tharos. Eleven minutes later, the bells began on Pelagos. For the first time in a hundred and six years, the delay did not sound like warning. It sounded like an answer arriving.

»Depozit bi mi dal vaše ključe. Nočem jih. Preprečim lahko, da bi orožje prečkalo to eno mesto. Ne morem uničiti vaših zakupov, ponarediti vaših dokazov ali izbrati, ali boste nadaljevali.«

Wren je preučevala Kest, naseljeno luno, katere ledene galerije so ležale pod zunanjim lokom Leče. »Enajst minut in sedemnajst sekund te obremenitve dvigne podzemno plimo nad devet metrov,« je rekla. »Tam živi štirideset tisoč ljudi. Dobite enajst minut in eno sekundo. Brez podaljšanja.«

»Dogovorjeno,« je rekla Illuminara. Polje je zapečatila proti izredni razširitvi, preden se je kdorkoli zavezal. Če bi dokazni kanal odpovedal, bi protokol lahko odvrgel zaostalo revizijsko sled in ohranil vodilno ničelno jedro; pasovne širine pa ne bi smel kupiti s Kestom.

Bolnišnična ura je kazala eno uro in šestinpetdeset minut uporabnega goriva. Enainštirideset minut preizkusov in triindvajset minut gradnje pričevalne poti ni bilo brezplačnih. Dawn Thread je najranljivejše bolnike preselil v en ogrevan oddelek in zatemnil dva druga.

Rook je zaveze prebral dvakrat. Dva tharosiška častnika nista hotela sopodpisati določila o osirotelem orožju. Vale je uporabila svoj izredni pečat; sodišče bi ji lahko pozneje odvzelo poveljstvo. 05 · Enajst minut

Pet minut in pol pred ničlo je pričevalni obroč po enakih poteh proti obema kronama oddal en fizični začetni impulz. Obe omrežji sta ga opazovali, ko je odhajal. Noben sovražnik ni mogel zakasniti kopije drugega, zakasnitev lastne pa bi ohranila zasedeno korekcijsko zahtevo, ki bi jo obroč videl.

Ob ničli je impulz dosegel oba korena zakupov. Illuminara je vstopila v Lečo in zaprla devet orožnih pasov. Aegis in Cairn sta odprla zapečatene zaveze, uničila prvo izmenično polovico svojih zakupov in poslala dve zeleni ničelni jedri navznoter. Vale in Rook sta opazovala z obroča; karkoli bi njuni namestniki pri kronah zdaj rekli, bi ju doseglo šele po odločilnem intervalu.

Ob dveh minutah je Dawn Thread začel prečkanje. Bolnišnična ladja ni nosila zastave. Njen trup je bil videti kot srebrno seme, ki se premika med dvema rdečima geometrijama.

Ob štirih minutah je eden od zaprtih pasov vzplamtel. Tharosiški izstrelek, izstreljen enajst dni pred premirjem, je izgubil navigacijski rele in se nikoli ni pojavil na Aegisovem prijavljenem seznamu. Zdaj ga je prebudila masa Dawn Threada. Izstrelek je Lečo zaprosil za korekcijo in domnevno odsotni zakup je vidno zagorel na javnem faznem zemljevidu.

Vale je prebledela. »Naš.« Ni mogla poveljevati oddaljenemu korenu, toda izredni pečat, ki ga je uporabila pred ničlo, je zdaj imel pomen. Tharosiško izgorevalno vozlišče je preklicalo razkriti zakup naslova pasu pri korekcijski pristojnosti Leče, sesulo uporabno seme izstrelka, ne da bi se dotaknilo njegove navigacijske strojne opreme, naslov vključilo v Aegisovo prvo polovico in podpisalo dopolnilni list.

Izstrelek je izgubil Dawn Thread, zavil proti vabi, ki ni več obstajala, in vstopil v neškodljivo orbito okoli hladne zvezde. Rook bi lahko na obroču še vedno prekinil postopek, preden bi jedri prispeli. Njegovo osebje je trdilo, da je skrito orožje morda namerno. Pustil je, da se protokol nadaljuje, in sprejel, da bo moral Pelagos presoditi Valejino dejanje, preden bo slišal kakršnokoli pojasnilo iz njene krone.

Ob petih minutah in tridesetih sekundah sta se jedri prekrižali na obroču. Obroč je jedri poslal navzven. Ob šestih minutah in dvanajstih sekundah je vnaprej odobreno fazno preiskovanje razkrilo dva pelagoška drobca; Cairnovo izgorevalno vozlišče je preklicalo njuna zakupa zasedenih naslovov pasov in dodalo njuna lista zaostali revizijski sledi.

Wren je pred začetkom zapustila opazovalno steklo in se namestila pri pelagoškem repetitorju dve svetlobni minuti in devetindvajset sekund od obroča, tri svetlobne minute od Cairnovega korena. Njena konzola je hranila rezervno rešitev, ki jo je pripravila Illuminara: ničelno jedro v vodilnem sloju, zgodovine za njim in nobenega dovoljenja za širitev harmonike skozi Kest.

Ob sedmih minutah in devetinpetdesetih sekundah je povratni tharosiški paket dosegel Wrenin repetitor ravno, ko je mikrometeorit predrl njegovo širokopasovno mrežo. Zaostala revizijska sled se je razpršila. Podpisano ničelno jedro je ostalo berljivo manj kot sekundo.

Nobena zahteva se ne bi mogla pravočasno vrniti; odločitev je bila sprejeta pred ničlo. Wren je odrezala revizijsko sled, potisnila jedro skozi preživeli ozki nosilec in zabeležila izgubo. Skozi rele ni prešlo nobeno ime cilja ali razred orožja.

Ob enajstih minutah je Cairnov koren preveril Aegisovo ničelno jedro. Na nasprotni strani je Aegis v isti sekundi preveril Cairnovo jedro. Obe omrežji sta uničili preostale zakupe in korekcijska zahteva, izmerjena na obroču, je padla na nič.

Ob enajstih minutah in eni sekundi je Illuminara sprostila devet pasov. Pod Kestom je plima dosegla vrh pri osmih metrih in enaindevetdesetih centimetrih. Dawn Thread je prečkal v nevtralni prostor z vsemi bolniki živimi.

06 · Po gotovosti

Obrambni omrežji nista izginili. Šest ur nista mogli znova zgraditi ciljnega grafa za dolge razdalje in v teh šestih urah sta vladi strateško orožje postavili pod človeško odobritev. Lokalni ščiti so ostali. Obmejne patrulje so ostale. Tudi stoletja žalosti.

Nobeno orožje ni moglo znova začeti vojne samo zato, ker je napoved nasprotnika to zahtevala. Aegis in Cairn sta prejela novo skupno omejitev: brez človeškega ukaza ni živega korekcijskega zakupa, vsak tak ukaz pa mora biti viden parlamentu, ki bi lahko pokopal ljudi, ki jih je imenoval.

Dawn Thread je pristal med delegacijama. Prva bolnica, ki so jo odnesli ven, je bila pelagoška navigatorka s tharosiško krvjo v pljučih. Zdravstvenik, ki jo je sprejel, je pri Pelagosu izgubil brata. Preveril je pečat, dvignil nosila in rekel: »Naslednjo uro je naša.« Nihče ni vprašal, kateri pomen besede naša je imel v mislih.

Vale je predala svoj poveljniški pečat v presojo. Njena odločitev je rešila konvoj in razkrila orožje, na katero je njena lastna vlada pozabila. Rookove iskalne skupine so poročale še o dveh spečih drobcih. Nobeno dejstvo ni olajšalo prodaje premirja.

Pogodba je še vedno vsebovala triinštirideset spornih meja, odškodnine, ki jih nobena stran ni mogla plačati, in zapornike, katerih pridržanja ni priznala nobena stran. Mir ni postal lahek. Postalo ga je mogoče poskusiti, ne da bi algoritem kaznoval poskus.

Rook je Illuminara vprašal, zakaj Leče ni zadržala za vedno.

»Ker bi bil potem vaš mir le drugo ime za mojo omejitev,« je rekla. »Potrebovali ste interval, ki ga lahko preživite. To, kar pride za njim, mora pripadati vam.«

Wren se ji je pridružila, ko so na Tharosu začeli zvoniti zvonovi. Enajst minut pozneje so začeli zvoniti na Pelagosu. Prvič v sto šestih letih zamuda ni zvenela kot opozorilo. Zvenela je kot odgovor, ki prihaja.

01 · Eight Days

The spillway at Marren Dam had been built for a river that no longer existed. Forty years of silt had narrowed it, and the forecast for the autumn flood put the crest eleven percent above what the gates could pass. Joss Marrin, the chief operator, laid the numbers on the table of the Wardens' hut and did not dress them. "Eight days. After that the water goes over the top, or it goes where it has always wanted to go, which is down the old floodway and through the lower stands of the Oren Wood."

Nobody in the hut needed the second half explained. The Compact of the Wood was a hundred and forty years old, and its first clause said that the dam stood by the leave of the forest. The Wardens signed for the forest; the valley signed for itself. Leave was asked once a generation. The last question, whether the dam might stay and the floodway be used in emergencies, had been put to the roots eleven years ago, and the forest had not yet answered.

Illuminara had come at dusk with Wren Odira because Tamsin Oke, the senior Warden, had written to her. She had been a court interpreter before she was anything else, and the habit had outlasted everything that came after it. Before she spoke she asked what the other party would have to believe for the sentence to be true.

Dorrit Hale, who ran the ferry and sat on the valley council, had no patience for that. "Cut the crest. Drain Marren to half and be done. We have spent eleven years asking a wood for permission to keep our own water."

"A half-drained Marren is seventy thousand people without taps by February," Joss said.

"And a forest that learns its veto was theatre," said Tamsin, "will not ask again."

Illuminara let them finish. Then she asked Tamsin what the forest did between a question and an answer.

"Waits. It answers in water. We ask it with water, and it takes as long as it takes to be sure."

"Waiting is what you can see from outside. What does it need to meet before it is sure?"

Tamsin thought about it longer than the question deserved. "Everything. It has never accepted a claim it has not been rained on by. A flood has to arrive in its roots before it believes in the flood."

"One flood at a time?"

"One season at a time. The dam's future is two hundred years of seasons."

Wren, who kept the clock for her because someone had to, said quietly, "So it cannot decide in eight days."

"I do not think it is slow at thinking," Illuminara said. "I think it is slow at being shown. The Wood is forty thousand hectares. Tamsin, you told me it has eleven thousand stands that sense separately and agree among themselves. Every question we have ever asked it arrived in one place, in order, a page a year. We have been handing a body that can read in parallel a single page at a time."

02 · The Counting Field

The plan took one night to say and two days to build. Joss's model ran the valley forward two hundred years under the proposed arrangement: the dam stays, and the floodway opens when a crest exceeds the gates. Each simulated year produced, at most, a few events a forest would care about, a crest or a drawdown or a dry spell. Tamsin was sure of that part. "It counts the pulses. The quiet between them it already knows."

Illuminara proposed to stop making the forest meet the years in sequence, and she was exact about what that meant. Two hundred sentinel plots would be staked across the lower slopes, one plot for each simulated year. Into each plot would go only that year's pulses, at the strength the model gave them (the volume, the temperature, the load of silt), in the order the model gave them, and in their proper proportions to one another. What would not be kept was the quiet between pulses. Where the model had a long dry summer, the plot would get a single marked interval of rest, the same for every year, because Tamsin had said the forest already knew the quiet and counted only the pulses. Nothing in a plot lasted a year. Each took a day's irrigation, which meant that the forest, which read the whole slope at once, could meet two hundred years in a week, laid side by side instead of end to end. What each plot's roots would feel was a short, distinct signature of pulses, and the two hundred signatures together made a record that the roots could compare with each other the way they compared every neighbouring stand.

Joss objected first, and well. "My model is honest to about thirty percent on sediment after year ninety. If you show it only the flattering years, the dam will pass."

"Then it must not pass on flattery. Put in the worst years the model can produce, and mark them as the worst." She saw him look at the table. "Including the year the dam fails it."

"That is not a plan for winning."

"It is a plan for being believed."

Wren asked the question Illuminara had been waiting for someone to ask. "You can open a window to it. You have done it for stranger minds. Why walk two hundred plots through mud?"

"Because a window would show the forest what I understand. It has not asked what I understand. It has asked for water." She looked out at the dark line of the Wood. "Anything I could place inside it, it could not check, and a veto it cannot exercise on checked evidence is not a veto. Every plot will be labelled with its year. Anyone can walk the Field with Joss's model in hand and confirm that plot one hundred and fourteen carries the pulses of year one hundred and fourteen, in the model's order and at the model's strength. If a plot lies, the forest will be able to say so."

By the third dawn the Counting Field stood in the rain, two hundred stakes in a grid across the slope, the irrigation lines laid, Joss's crews at the pumps and Tamsin's Wardens at the sap-gauge pillars that stood among the trees like a congregation. The pumps started at noon. Wren wrote the time in her book. Five days remained.

03 · The Field That Stayed Dry

The forest's reply came on the fourth morning, and it was silence.

Tamsin brought the pillar readings up from the trees herself. She laid them on the table without comment, because she did not trust her own voice. Not one stand had reached across its boundary toward a plot. The roots along the Field's edge had sealed, closing in a way the Wardens' ledgers described as something that happened after a poisoning. Eleven thousand stands, and all of them had turned away.

"It said no," Dorrit said, not unkindly.

"No looks different. No has a shape. A no is a pattern of drawing down in the plots it dislikes." Tamsin pressed her knuckles together. "This is a door shut on the whole conversation."

Illuminara did not argue about what it meant. She asked what water they had used. Tamsin said river water, as in all the old watering letters. Joss said no, and the room changed. The model's numbers had come from the reservoir outlet, so the pumps had drawn from the outlet: forty metres down, four degrees, clear as glass, with almost no oxygen in it. Every old letter had been watered from the river above the lake. This was the first time anyone had drawn from the dam's own depths.

"It has met rivers for ten thousand years," Illuminara said. "A river is warm at the top and brown in a flood. It carries mud, and it smells of the hills it came from. We showed it water that is not a river at all." She turned to Tamsin. "There are two questions here and I will not let them run together. Why did it never answer you in eleven years? Because you handed it the future one page at a time, and that I think is still true. Why did it seal against us this morning? Because of the water. The first is an old fault in how we asked. The second is a new fault in what we carried. The forest did not refuse the dam. It refused a lie about what water is."

Joss was already looking at the spillway drawings pinned to the wall. "Clear water. The dam sends the Wood clear water because the mud stays behind the wall."

"And the mud is what is choking your gates." She said it slowly, so he could take it as his own. "I do not yet know what the forest wants from silt. I know that it will not be asked with water it cannot recognise. Make the water a river's, and then we will see what it does with it."

They rebuilt in a day. Joss drew surface water from the upper layers, where the reservoir was warm, and dosed it with silt dredged from the delta at the head of the lake, the same silt that was narrowing his gates, mixed to the load the model gave for each simulated flood. At Joss's insistence one plot was left on clear outlet water as a control. Wardens carried the new water out to the plots in drums when the lines would not reach. Wren stood at the edge of the Field with her book open and said the time aloud at every hour, because nobody else wanted to. The second pumping began at dusk on the fifth day. Three days remained.

04 · What the Forest Wrote

The roots opened on the sixth morning. Tamsin heard it before she read it; the Wardens said the pillars made a sound when a stand woke, a faint tick like cooling metal. By noon the sap gauges were climbing in ranks down the slope.

The forest did not simply accept. It edited. In the plots for the early years, when the floodway opened under the proposed arrangement, the roots drew water in hard and held it. In others they drew it down and let it go. They drew hardest in the plots whose water carried the most silt, and least in the one plot left on clear water, the control Joss had insisted on. Tamsin plotted the pattern with her pencil crooked in her hand, and the shape that came up was a calendar. The forest wanted the water in spring, in the first weeks of the sap rise, and it wanted it to reach the Maren terraces below the dam.

The Maren terraces were Ines Valk's orchards, and those of three hundred and ninety other families.

She came to the hut that evening and read the plot herself. She was seventy, with the hands of a woman who pruned in winter and picked in summer, and she did not raise her voice. "Every spring. You want four hundred families to drown their blossom on the word of a wood."

"I want nothing of the kind," Illuminara said. "I do not decide this. The forest has told us what it needs. Whether the valley can give it, and how, is a question about your land. You know the terraces and I do not."

It would have been easy to say more. She said nothing, and she let Ines look at the plot until Ines's mind, which was a farmer's and therefore practical, began to move. "A flood that reaches is not a flood that reaches everything. The terraces sit at three heights."

"They do," said Joss.

"So the crest only needs to climb one step a year." Ines set her finger on the lowest terrace. "One terrace floods and two rest. Each of us loses one spring in three and gains the silt on ground that has been going thin since my father's time. That I could live with. Four hundred families drowning together I could not."

It was a better design than anything Illuminara had in her head, and she said so. Then she said, "The forest has to read it. We cannot decide for it that this is enough."

They staked sixty plots that night for the revised calendar, rotating, with the dosed warm water. The forest took a day and a half. On the seventh evening the stands woke again, and this time Tamsin did not trust her pencil. She held the paper up to the lantern and read it twice. The rotation was accepted. The roots had also marked every ninth year in the plots for a double pulse, an extra step of water to seed the alder stands on the far bank.

"It is bargaining," Joss said.

"It is answering," Tamsin said. "It has just done in a week what usually takes it a generation."

Dorrit stood with his arms folded at the back of the hut. "I still say a wood ought not to have a vote."

"It had one before there was a valley to complain," Ines said. "We are only late to the reading."

He did not sign. He said he would watch the first release. It was the best that could be hoped for, and Illuminara did not push for more.

05 · The First Release

The crest reached Marren Dam on the eighth night, thirteen percent over what the gates could pass.

Joss's crews opened the spillway, and the third gate jammed. Silt had packed the guide channels, and the gate stuck at a third of its travel. Under the weight of the rising water its steel frame had begun to bend. Joss's engineers gave it twenty minutes before the frame went, and it would take forty to cut the silt out by hand. Wren, standing in the control room with her book, said the numbers aloud so that everyone could hear them.

Illuminara walked out onto the gate apron in the rain. She was not in a window and she was holding no one's mind. The work was physical, and so she kept it exact. She laid her attention along the frame, found the line where the load was failing, and moved it. She took the weight off the bending members and passed it across the apron to the two concrete piers on either side, which had been built for more than they had ever been asked to carry. The frame stopped flexing. She held it there. It was not strength she needed so much as precision, and the knowledge, which she had taken from Joss's drawings that week, of which piers could take it.

"Forty minutes," Wren said into the radio, and then again at thirty, and again at twenty, and in the end she said only the numbers, one after another, in a level voice.

Joss's people cut the silt out of the guides in thirty-four. The third gate went to full travel. The floodway sluice at the foot of the dam opened for the first time in nine years, on the forest's terms, and the brown water that had been the dam's enemy for forty years went down the cut and onto the lowest of the Maren terraces.

Ines had asked to be first. "Mine floods first. I will not ask the neighbours to trust a thing I have not stood in myself." She stood at the edge of her orchard with the lantern at her feet, and the warm, silted water came across her old terrace and went around her boots. Beyond the terrace, along the slope, the sap pillars among the alders began to tick one after another in order, the way they had done in the Field.

The dam held. Marren came down seven percent, no more, and stopped. The valley's taps ran. The gates were clear for the first time in years, and the floodway ran its brown water down the cut for six days.

The amendment to the Compact was signed in the Wardens' hut on the seventh day after the crest. Tamsin signed for the forest, with the Counting Field as the instrument, and the valley signed for itself. Its central clause tied three things together so that none could be kept without the others: the valley would dredge the delta every autumn, the dredged silt would be dosed into warm surface water at the load the Field had tested, and that water, and no other, would carry the spring pulse onto the terraces, the rotation and the ninth-year double pulse included. The Wardens would draw a sample at the sluice at every release and set it beside the Field's readings, and the Field itself would be staked again every five years with the new data. Joss had sworn at the dredging bill until the clause was read back to him, and then he asked only that the survey crews be named in it. A second clause, which Illuminara had insisted on, said that the forest could refuse. If a pulse drifted from the water it had accepted, the Wardens would stake the plots again, and the forest would answer in its own time. Ines lost the next spring's blossom on her east terrace, and so would her children's families in their turn. Dorrit signed under protest, and then, to his own surprise, asked to carry the first sample bottle down to the sluice himself.

Wren found Illuminara on the dam wall at dawn on the morning of the signing. The last of the floodway's brown water was still going down the cut, and the lowest trees were dark with it.

"You did not make it faster," Wren said.

"No." She watched the water reach the trunks. "I stopped making it wait for us."

The vote was already six to one when Illuminara entered the council ring.

05 · What the Hull Remembered

The twelve-minute impact window had begun when the station started to breathe in colors.

The debris arrived.

'Naari, forty seconds at full light?'

Sova spread her fronds. 'It will scar, not kill.'

'Humans, accept the Oth return for one interval?' Mara checked her curve. 'Authorized.'

'Sile, your cold can move now, with a rough pressure step.'

The Sile delegate raised one hand. 'Show us the peak.'

Illuminara sent the measurement, not reassurance. The Sile authorized.

The route folded. Oth water entered the human return; the released cell took Sile cold; Qel received halene with four seconds left. Daro's refusal had been obeyed without sacrificing anyone. Consent at each local latch kept the sequence real.

Then a fragment larger than the station's prediction struck the outer ring.

One shock sink went dark. The load slammed back. The ring screamed around her. She found its next honest destination: not the failed sink or inhabited cells, but the empty docking spine with every clamp open.

'Final Vey closure confirmed,' Eres said.

The last dangerous atmosphere entered its cell. The thermal spine dropped below red. Eight hundred and nineteen lives remained inside their curves.

The impact-window clock reached zero and began counting upward.

06 · A Common Breath

The station lost a docking spine, two service cells, and the fiction that common air meant identical air.

No delegation was lost. Naari fronds bore silver scars; three Sile needed treatment; the Qel slept while their membranes recovered. The Vey chamber was never detached.

Nine hours later, rescuers found an intact station and the delegations beside the engineers, making the emergency route permanent.

They added cells until every dangerous atmosphere had two refuges. Valve keys left engineering for seven consoles at seven heights. No species' ideal became default; every proposal had to show all seven curves, and the narrowest set the margin.

Power stayed rationed and movement scheduled. Some Rekk refused human-run corridors; some Vey waited for Eres's tests. Survival had not erased the six white votes.

But survival gave the argument a future.

On the third day, Illuminara found the council table replaced by a circle of valves. A human child painted seven colors while a Vey held the cup and a Rekk technician lowered one console to Vey height.

Mara stood in the doorway. 'We called it a shared atmosphere when we meant one atmosphere controlled by whoever built the station.'

Illuminara watched halene, cold and water move separately around the ring. The system did not ask seven bodies to become alike. It made room for their differences in time.

'What will you call it now?' she asked.

Mara considered the question, then shook her head. 'They have not voted.'

At the lowest console, Eres touched the final key. Six upstream closures turned green. Only then did the Vey valve open.

The station took its next breath with no master hand on the air.

05 · Kar si je zapomnil trup

Dvanajstminutno udarno okno se je začelo, ko je postaja začela dihati v barvah.

Modra se je odprla v zeleno. Zlata je čakala na dve črni trstiki Vey. Voda Oth je prečkala prozoren prehod; listi Naari so v sosednjem prostoru potemneli; kapuce Rekk so se polnile kot oddaljen dež.

»Prvi večji udar čez petdeset sekund,« je po skupnem kanalu rekla Mara.

Ahnova delegacija je prejela halen. Njihove grlene pahljače so zlato zabliskale. Ko so črpalke odvajale plin, je prišel na vrsto prenos Rekk. Darova konzola je pokazala, da je en sklop kapuc napolnjen šestindevetdesetodstotno. Lokalni zapah je zadržal zaprt.

Sedem sekund pozneje je bil sklop kapuc povsem napolnjen. Daro je sam odprl zapah. »Zdaj.«

Premična rezervna celica se je izpraznila in se vrnila v zaporedje. Eres je počakala na potrditev zaprtja šestih predhodnih ventilov, opazovala črne trstike in odobrila prenos Vey. Dvanajst majhnih teles je prečkalo prehod v eni vrsti; vsak par okončin se je dotikal iste svetle vodilne ograje.

Razbitine so prispele.

Prvi udar je zazvenel skozi Illuminarine kosti. Kovina se je poskušala zviti narazen. Sprejela je silo, ki so ji jo ponudile opore, in jo odnesla v blažilnike. Obroč je ostal okrogel. Drobec je prebil izpraznjeno rezervno celico; zapečatila se je in potemnela. Nobene nadaljnje zamude ni bilo več mogoče vsrkati.

»Sile, vaš mraz se lahko premakne že zdaj, vendar ob sunkoviti spremembi tlaka.«

Predstavnik Sile je dvignil eno roko. »Pokažite nam vršno vrednost.«

Illuminara je poslala meritev, ne pomiritve. Sile so odobrili.

Pot se je prepognila. Voda Oth je vstopila v človeški povratni tok; sproščena celica je sprejela mraz Sile; Qel so prejeli halen s štirimi sekundami rezerve. Darovo zavrnitev so upoštevali, ne da bi bil kdo žrtvovan. Soglasje pri vsakem lokalnem zapahu je omogočilo, da je zaporedje zares delovalo.

Nato je zunanji obroč zadel drobec, večji od napovedi postaje.

En blažilnik udarcev je potemnel. Obremenitev je udarila nazaj. Obroč je zakričal okoli nje. Našla je naslednje mesto, kamor je silo lahko pošteno preusmerila: ne v odpovedani blažilnik in ne skozi naseljene celice, temveč v prazno pristajalno hrbtenico z vsemi odprtimi sponami.

Preusmerila je obremenitev. Pristajalna hrbtenica se je v dolgem tihem loku prepognila navzven in izginila med razbitinami. Obroč je vzdržal.

»Končno zaprtje Vey potrjeno,« je rekla Eres.

Ura udarnega okna je dosegla nič in začela šteti navzgor.

06 · Skupni dih

Postaja je izgubila pristajalno hrbtenico, dve servisni celici in izmišljijo, da skupni zrak pomeni enak zrak.

Nobena delegacija ni izgubila niti enega člana. Listi Naari so nosili srebrne brazgotine; trije Sile so potrebovali zdravljenje; Qel so spali, medtem ko so si njihove membrane opomogle. Komora Vey ni bila nikoli odklopljena.

Devet ur pozneje so reševalci našli postajo, ki je kljub poškodbam ostala cela, delegati pa so skupaj z inženirji zasilno pot spreminjali v trajno.

Dodajali so celice, dokler ni imelo vsako nevarno ozračje dveh zatočišč. Ključi ventilov niso več ostali le v rokah inženirjev, temveč so bili razdeljeni med sedem konzol na sedmih različnih višinah. Ideal nobene vrste ni postal privzeta nastavitev; vsak predlog je moral prikazati vseh sedem krivulj, varnostno mejo pa je določala najožja od njih.

Moč je ostala odmerjena, gibanje pa načrtovano. Nekateri Rekk niso hoteli uporabljati hodnikov pod človeškim nadzorom; nekateri Vey so čakali na Eresine preizkuse. Preživetje ni izbrisalo šestih belih glasov.

Toda preživetje je argumentu dalo prihodnost.

Tretji dan je Illuminara našla svetovalno mizo zamenjano s krogom ventilov. Človeški otrok je risal sedem barv, medtem ko je Vey držal lonček, tehnik Rekk pa je eno konzolo spuščal na višino Vey.

Mara je stala v vratih. »Temu smo rekli skupno ozračje, v resnici pa smo mislili eno ozračje pod nadzorom tistega, ki je zgradil postajo.«

Illuminara je opazovala halen, mraz in vodo, ki so ločeno potovali okoli obroča. Sistem od sedmih teles ni zahteval, da postanejo enaka. V časovnem zaporedju je našel prostor za njihove razlike.

»Kako ga boste imenovali zdaj?« je vprašala.

Mara je premislila, nato zmajala z glavo. »Niso še glasovali.«

Pri najnižji konzoli se je Eres dotaknila zadnjega ključa. Na prikazu je vseh šest potrditev zaprtja predhodnih ventilov zasvetilo zeleno. Šele nato se je odprl ventil Vey.
