Method

What This Measures

DCC accuracy = the percentage of moves where a player chose the move that DCC's structural analysis ranked #1 among chessdb.cn's top candidates — the largest chess position database in existence (58 billion positions, collective multi-engine intelligence).

Higher DCC = the player more frequently chose the healthiest, most structurally sound move among the top candidates, looking 40 half-moves (20 full moves) into the future.

Convergence & Depth

We tested four depths (D10, D20, D40, D80). Signal converges at D40. D80 adds nothing — r=1.000, average change 0.03pp per player. D40 is the minimum description that captures all the information. Less describes more. All rankings on this page use D40.

Opponent Filter

Only games where the opponent's DCC accuracy was ≥40% ("elite filter"). This removes easy wins against weak opponents and enables fair cross-era comparison. A player can only demonstrate structural quality when the opponent is also playing at elite level.

Findings

What the Data Shows

Finding 1

Polgar is #1 among humans — and still rising

58.6% at D40 — above Carlsen (58.4%), Kasparov (55.8%), Fischer (54.1%). She is the only top player whose DCC rises monotonically across all four tested depths, from 56.6% to 58.6%.

Her moves age like fine wine. The deeper you look, the better they are. She also has the largest positive delta when facing elite opponents (+3.5pp). Every other top player either stays flat or drops against the best. Polgar elevates.

Finding 2

Strategists rise. Tacticians fall.

Players split into two signatures by depth trend:

↑ RISING — Polgar (+2.0pp), Carlsen (+1.0pp), Lasker (+3.4pp), Fischer (+0.7pp), Gukesh (+4.0pp). Their moves optimize for long-term structural health. D10 underestimates them.

↓ FALLING — Nepo (−6.3pp), Anand (−4.8pp), Kramnik (−3.5pp), Firouzja (−3.0pp), Ding (−1.7pp). Brilliant short-term, less robust at depth 40. No previous metric separated these two qualities.

Finding 3

Engines are stable — they don't trade short for long

Stockfish: 70.0% (±0.7pp across all depths). LCZero: 66.1% (±0.2pp). Engine moves are equally good at every horizon. No short-term vs long-term tradeoff. This is what perfect consistency looks like.

The human-engine gap is not a wall. It is a consistency gap.

Finding 4

Fischer–Spassky: largest structural gap in chess history

72.0% vs 49.5% in direct matchups — a gap of +22pp. For context, Stockfish vs LCZero is +7pp. Fischer's structural dominance was 3× the gap between the two strongest engines. Engine-level performance from a human, measured for the first time.

Finding 5

Lasker rises +3.4pp — historical vindication

From 51.0% (shallow) to 54.4% (D40). Lasker was known for "ugly" moves that paid off 15 moves later. Shallow depth doesn't see it. D40 does. First quantitative confirmation of Lasker's strategic depth, measured 130 years after the games were played.

Finding 6

Carlsen's win-loss gap remains tiny

Carlsen plays at similar structural quality whether winning or losing (+6.2pp gap). His opponents make errors, not him. Kasparov and Capablanca elevate dramatically when winning and collapse when losing. Carlsen stays flat. DCC quantifies what commentators call "Carlsen grinding."

Finding 7

Peak human matches engine average

In their best games, elite humans exceed Stockfish's average structural quality:

Stockfish avg: 70.0% — Fischer vs Spassky: 72.0% — Caruana vs Nakamura: 74.5% — Polgar vs Anand: 76.0%

The engine advantage is consistency. Stockfish never drops below 50%. Humans oscillate between 72% brilliance and 39% collapse. The gap is real — but it is not a wall.

Finding 8

Gukesh has the largest depth rise — and is still climbing

+4.0pp from shallow to D40. At 18 years old, he plays for the future more than anyone in the dataset. Shallow depth ranks him #16. D40 ranks him #14. He is still climbing — this number will likely improve as he gains more games at elite level.

Finding 9

130 years of chess: ~5pp total improvement

Pre-war era (~51–54%) → Cold War (~54%) → Computer era (~55–56%) → Modern (~49–58%). About 5pp across 130 years. ELO has inflated by hundreds of points. Structural quality has barely moved. Players are not dramatically better in absolute terms — they are better relative to contemporaries.

Finding 10

DCC separates two independent qualities

By measuring at multiple depths, DCC reveals a clean separation:

Overall move quality — what D10 measures: tactics + strategy combined.

Strategic depth — what Δ(D40−D10) measures: how much better your moves get when you look further ahead. Positive = strategist. Negative = tactician. Both can win. They win differently. No previous metric made this distinction.

Caveats

Honest Limitations

  • Sample sizes vary. Carlsen's 33 elite games is robust. Ding Liren (7), Nepomniachtchi (7) are marginal. Head-to-heads with 2 games are indicative, not definitive.
  • Game selection bias. "Selected" games favor decisive, dramatic, historically important games. Draws are underrepresented. Random sampling would strengthen conclusions.
  • ChessDB coverage. Modern openings have more cached positions. Pre-war players have lower coverage, reducing measurement resolution. Lasker's and Capablanca's true DCC may be higher than measured.
  • DCC ≠ chess strength. DCC measures structural move quality. Chess is also decided by tactics, time management, psychology, endgame technique, opening preparation — none of which DCC measures directly. A player can win consistently with DCC-suboptimal moves.
  • Opponent filter is a proxy. Elite threshold (≥40%) is imperfect. True opponent strength is multi-dimensional. The filter removes the most obvious noise but cannot perfectly level the playing field across 130 years.
