Main things the article gets right

The article correctly presents the “core mechanics”: tokenization → embeddings → positional signal → attention → FFN → residual stream → norm → logits → sampling loop. This really is the basic backbone of most modern LLMs. The article itself also clearly says it covers the “core mechanisms” of transformer-based LLMs and that models differ by weights, configuration, data, and post-training. (0xkato)

It is also right about “lost in the middle”: Liu et al. showed that models often use information at the beginning and end of a context better than information in the middle. (ACL Anthology)

Where it is misleading or too narrow

The biggest weakness is not that it says nonsense. The weakness is the title. “How LLMs Actually Work” sounds like a complete explanation, but in reality it is a well-written transformer explainer.

At minimum, it lacks:

  1. Training data + scaling — why size, data quality, and compute change capabilities so much.
  2. Post-training — SFT, RLHF, DPO, constitutional/safety tuning, reasoning tuning.
  3. Inference systems — batching, KV-cache optimizations, quantization, speculative decoding, routing, server-side orchestration.
  4. Reasoning behavior — how chain-of-thought, scratchpads, self-consistency, verifiers, and agent loops appear.
  5. Tool/RAG/memory wrappers — ChatGPT/Claude/Gemini as products are not only model weights; they often include search, tools, system instructions, memory, and safety classifiers.
  6. Multimodality — today’s frontier models are no longer only text-only decoder-only transformers.

My final score

As an introductory technical article: 88/100. As a complete explanation of “how LLMs actually work”: 72/100. As a source for understanding the basic transformer core: very useful.

BD-style blunt version: it is not bullshit. It is a good article. You just should not read it as “now I understand the entire modern AI system.” You understand its core, not the whole organism.

One-paragraph answer

The article mostly holds. It gives a good, practical explanation of the internal transformer stack: text becomes tokens, tokens become vectors, masked self-attention mixes information from prior context, feed-forward layers transform token representations, and the model generates by predicting the next token. Those points match standard explanations of tokenization, transformer attention, and autoregressive decoding. (OpenAI Help Center) The weak points are that it slightly overstates some intuitions — especially tokenization as the reason for counting failures, embedding-vector analogies, induction heads, and “facts in FFNs” — and it does not give enough space to post-training, hallucinations, tool use, retrieval, safety policies, and product-level systems, which strongly shape ChatGPT/Claude/Gemini-style assistants. (arXiv)

What the article gets right

  • Tokens first, text second. LLMs process token IDs, not raw human text directly. OpenAI’s own docs describe tokens as chunks such as characters, words, punctuation, or partial words, and tiktoken is OpenAI’s BPE tokenizer library. (OpenAI Help Center)

  • Subword tokenization is real and important. The article’s BPE/SentencePiece discussion is broadly correct. SentencePiece is a standard subword tokenizer/detokenizer system used in neural text processing. (ACL Anthology)

  • The transformer explanation is mostly correct. The article’s Q/K/V, scaled dot-product attention, softmax-weighted value mixing, and causal masking are aligned with standard transformer explanations. (nlp.seas.harvard.edu)

  • Multi-head attention is explained well. It correctly says heads use learned projections rather than simply fixed slices of the vector. That is an important detail many beginner explainers get wrong. (nlp.seas.harvard.edu)

  • RoPE is described in the right direction. Rotary positional embeddings encode position through rotations and make relative-position information available inside self-attention. (arXiv)

  • The FFN/MLP section is one of the stronger parts. There is real research support for feed-forward layers acting partly like key-value memories and for mid-layer FF modules playing an important role in factual associations. (ACL Anthology)

  • The article correctly separates architecture from weights. The transformer skeleton is not the intelligence by itself; the learned weights, training data, objective, scale, post-training, and inference setup matter enormously. The article states this in simplified but valid form. (0xkato)

  • GQA, KV cache, MoE, and speculative decoding are real modern inference/architecture ideas. The article’s inclusion of these topics is a strength, not fluff. Mistral 7B, for example, explicitly uses grouped-query attention to speed inference and reduce memory during decoding. (arXiv) Mixtral uses sparse mixture-of-experts routing, where each token is routed to selected experts rather than all FFN experts. (arXiv) Speculative decoding is also a real method for accelerating autoregressive generation. (arXiv)

What needs correction or nuance

  • It is not a full “how LLMs actually work” article. It is mainly an architecture explainer. It does not deeply explain data curation, pretraining loss, scaling, optimizer dynamics, instruction tuning, RLHF/RLAIF/DPO, safety policies, system prompts, retrieval, tool use, evaluation, or deployment.

  • Tokenization is overused as an explanation for mistakes. Tokenization helps explain why letter-level tasks like counting “r” in “strawberry” can be unintuitive, but it is not the whole story. Exact character counting also requires reliable symbolic/algorithmic behavior, not just better tokens. OpenAI’s docs support the token-level framing, but not the stronger claim that tokenization alone explains these failures. (OpenAI Help Center)

  • The embedding analogy is useful but oversold. “king − man + woman ≈ queen” is a classic word-vector intuition, but modern LLM behavior depends far more on contextual hidden states across many layers than on the initial embedding lookup alone. Word-vector research supports semantic vector relationships, but also notes limitations such as insensitivity to word order in simpler embedding systems. (arXiv)

  • RoPE and long context are not magic. RoPE helps with positional representation and relative-position behavior, but long-context models can still fail depending on where relevant information appears. The “Lost in the Middle” paper shows performance can degrade when relevant information is placed in the middle of long contexts. (arXiv)

  • Induction heads are real, but the article overstates certainty. Induction heads are an important mechanistic-interpretability finding, but the strongest causal evidence is in small attention-only models; for larger models with MLPs, evidence is more correlational and incomplete. (arXiv)

  • “Facts live in FFNs” needs nuance. FFNs contain a lot of model capacity and can encode factual associations, but LLM knowledge is distributed across layers, attention, FFNs, activations, and residual streams. ROME-style edits show important evidence, but they do not mean there is a clean database row for every fact. (arXiv)

  • Post-training deserves more weight. Modern assistants are not just pretrained next-token predictors. Instruction tuning and human/preference feedback strongly shape helpfulness, truthfulness, refusal behavior, tone, and task-following. InstructGPT showed that fine-tuning with human feedback improved alignment with user intent and helped reduce toxic/untruthful outputs, while still leaving mistakes. (arXiv)

  • Hallucinations are under-explained. The article mentions generation mechanics, but a serious “how LLMs work” explainer should explain why models can produce plausible but false claims. OpenAI describes hallucinations as plausible but false outputs that persist partly because evaluation setups often reward guessing over calibrated uncertainty. (OpenAI)

Best corrected explanation

A modern text LLM usually starts by tokenizing input text into token IDs. Those IDs are mapped into learned vectors, combined with positional information, and passed through many transformer layers. Inside each layer, masked self-attention lets each token mix information from earlier tokens using learned query, key, and value projections; feed-forward/MLP layers then transform each token representation and provide much of the model’s capacity. Residual connections and normalization keep very deep stacks trainable. (OpenAI Help Center)

During pretraining, the model’s weights are adjusted so it becomes better at predicting missing or next tokens across huge text/code/multimodal corpora. During inference, a decoder-style LLM repeatedly predicts a distribution over the next token, samples or selects one, appends it, and continues. KV caching, grouped-query attention, MoE routing, and speculative decoding are engineering/architecture techniques that make this process cheaper or faster. (arXiv)

But deployed assistants are more than base next-token predictors. They are shaped by instruction tuning, preference/RLHF-style training, system prompts, safety policies, retrieval, tools, and product orchestration. They do not store a clean database of facts; knowledge and skills are distributed across weights and activations. Because they generate plausible continuations rather than directly checking truth by default, they can reason impressively but also hallucinate. (arXiv)

Best corrected explanation

A modern text LLM usually starts by tokenizing input text into token IDs. Those IDs are mapped into learned vectors, combined with positional information, and passed through many transformer layers. Inside each layer, masked self-attention lets each token mix information from earlier tokens using learned query, key, and value projections; feed-forward/MLP layers then transform each token representation and provide much of the model’s capacity. Residual connections and normalization keep very deep stacks trainable. (OpenAI Help Center)

During pretraining, the model’s weights are adjusted so it becomes better at predicting missing or next tokens across huge text/code/multimodal corpora. During inference, a decoder-style LLM repeatedly predicts a distribution over the next token, samples or selects one, appends it, and continues. KV caching, grouped-query attention, MoE routing, and speculative decoding are engineering/architecture techniques that make this process cheaper or faster. (arXiv)

But deployed assistants are more than base next-token predictors. They are shaped by instruction tuning, preference/RLHF-style training, system prompts, safety policies, retrieval, tools, and product orchestration. They do not store a clean database of facts; knowledge and skills are distributed across weights and activations. Because they generate plausible continuations rather than directly checking truth by default, they can reason impressively but also hallucinate. (arXiv)

## Best corrected explanation

A modern text LLM usually starts by **tokenizing** input text into token IDs. Those IDs are mapped into learned vectors, combined with positional information, and passed through many transformer layers. Inside each layer, **masked self-attention** lets each token mix information from earlier tokens using learned query, key, and value projections; **feed-forward/MLP layers** then transform each token representation and provide much of the model’s capacity. Residual connections and normalization keep very deep stacks trainable. ([OpenAI Help Center][2])

During pretraining, the model’s weights are adjusted so it becomes better at predicting missing or next tokens across huge text/code/multimodal corpora. During inference, a decoder-style LLM repeatedly predicts a distribution over the next token, samples or selects one, appends it, and continues. KV caching, grouped-query attention, MoE routing, and speculative decoding are engineering/architecture techniques that make this process cheaper or faster. ([arXiv][8])

But deployed assistants are more than base next-token predictors. They are shaped by instruction tuning, preference/RLHF-style training, system prompts, safety policies, retrieval, tools, and product orchestration. They do not store a clean database of facts; knowledge and skills are distributed across weights and activations. Because they generate plausible continuations rather than directly checking truth by default, they can reason impressively but also hallucinate. ([arXiv][3])

## Best corrected explanation

A modern text LLM usually starts by **tokenizing** input text into token IDs. Those IDs are mapped into learned vectors, combined with positional information, and passed through many transformer layers. Inside each layer, **masked self-attention** lets each token mix information from earlier tokens using learned query, key, and value projections; **feed-forward/MLP layers** then transform each token representation and provide much of the model’s capacity. Residual connections and normalization keep very deep stacks trainable. ([OpenAI Help Center][2])

During pretraining, the model’s weights are adjusted so it becomes better at predicting missing or next tokens across huge text/code/multimodal corpora. During inference, a decoder-style LLM repeatedly predicts a distribution over the next token, samples or selects one, appends it, and continues. KV caching, grouped-query attention, MoE routing, and speculative decoding are engineering/architecture techniques that make this process cheaper or faster. ([arXiv][8])

But deployed assistants are more than base next-token predictors. They are shaped by instruction tuning, preference/RLHF-style training, system prompts, safety policies, retrieval, tools, and product orchestration. They do not store a clean database of facts; knowledge and skills are distributed across weights and activations. Because they generate plausible continuations rather than directly checking truth by default, they can reason impressively but also hallucinate. ([arXiv][3])
