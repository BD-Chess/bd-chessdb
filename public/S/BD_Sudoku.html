Origin Story — one afternoon, one question, one answer ▶
March 23, 2026 · AIM³ Lab · Ljubljana

310 lines of code. That's the entire engine — puzzle generator, backtracking solver with snapshot-based state restoration, live metrics, AI animation, keyboard shortcuts, notes mode, undo history, and the ADSR measurement system. Not 310 lines for the game loop. 310 lines for everything.

The question that started it: does the same MDL kernel that finds optimal TSP tours — the one that navigates 3,496 cities in Nicaragua — also work on constraint satisfaction? In TSP, the founding insight was simple: the shortest route has the most structured search process (Spearman ρ = +0.80, verified on official Waterloo benchmarks). In Sudoku, the question becomes: do easier puzzles have more structured solving processes?

We built a backtracking solver with full process logging. Ran it on 1,000 puzzles across four difficulty levels. Measured ADSR bimodality — whether the solving process cleanly separates into cascading chain-reactions and isolated single-placements.

Result: ρ = −0.50 (p < 10⁻⁶⁴). Easier puzzles score higher. Evil puzzles drop below 0.95. The same principle that guides the TSP solver through a combinatorial space of 101783 possible tours also predicts how hard a Sudoku puzzle is. One kernel. One afternoon. Third domain confirmed.

What the ADSR bar is actually measuring ▶
Live metrics · updated every AI step

Every Sudoku app solves puzzles. This is the only one that watches itself solve and shows you the structure of what it sees.

The ADSR bar measures bimodality — whether the AI's moves cleanly separate into two types: cascades (one placement triggers a chain reaction across multiple cells) and isolated singles (one placement, nothing follows). When the bar is high, the AI is flowing through structured territory. When it drops, the solver is grinding — forced to guess, backtrack, rebuild.

Cascade depth shows how far a single placement ripples. A cascade of 15 means one move resolved 15 more cells by pure logical necessity. Watch for the number spiking on Easy puzzles — the structure is so tight that every move unlocks a cascade. On Evil puzzles, cascades become rare. The AI guesses in the dark.

Candidates tracks total remaining uncertainty across the grid. A smooth descent means the solver is in structured territory. A jagged, stalling descent — flat stretches followed by sudden drops — is the signature of a puzzle that required guessing to break open.

None of this is decorative. This is a research instrument. The ADSR reading you see while the AI solves your puzzle is the same signal that produced ρ = −0.50 across 1,000 puzzles. You're watching the measurement happen in real time.

How to play ▶
Quick guide

Select a cell by clicking or tapping. Highlighted cells share a row, column, or box with your selection.

Place a number using the numpad below the grid, or press 1–9 on your keyboard. Click the same number again to clear it. Arrow keys move the selection.

Notes mode — toggle with the Notes button or press N. Numbers become small pencil marks — candidates you're considering.

Undo with the button or Ctrl+Z. Erase with the button or Backspace.

💡 Hint — highlights the next logical cell the AI would fill, and tells you why (naked single, hidden single, or guess). It doesn't place the number — that's your move.

AI Solve — watch the full solving process animated. Green = naked single (only one candidate). Blue = hidden single (only place for a digit in its group). Orange = guess. Red flash = backtrack. Use the speed slider or Step for one move at a time.

Difficulty: Easy has 36–42 given digits and solves with pure logic. Evil has only 20–23 — the AI will guess and backtrack. Watch the ADSR drop.
