ABSTRACT

HTH is a proposed hydraulic air-compression architecture using current capture, air entrainment, a separator and terrain-adaptive pipes. When inlet and outlet return to the same river, static submergence pressure cancels in the driving-head balance. Available input comes from velocity head and the bed elevation drop, with hydraulic losses and outlet conditions deducted.

The preliminary head-balanced model gives 389.4 W over a 198 m example profile. Five of eight segments fail its bubble-transport check; retaining only the transport-passing segments gives approximately 283 W. These are conditional model estimates, not measurements from a working prototype or a validated power rating.

Ocean, wave and thermal variants are research possibilities with separate energy budgets. Their output, maintenance, lifetime and economics remain unmeasured. The first useful result would be a bench measurement of hydraulic input against pneumatic output.

MDL × DCC could later help allocate flow or compare layouts. This is a control hypothesis, not an independent physical confirmation of a computational kernel.

1Founding Physics▸

1.1 Pressure and driving head

A trompe entrains air in water and delivers separated air under pressure. The water must lose usable hydraulic energy to do this. A deep separator can set a higher air-delivery pressure, but pressure at depth is not a source of continuous power.

1.2 Current and river gradient

  • Velocity head: ideal current capture offers at most v²/(2g) of head. At 3 m/s this is about 0.46 m, or 4.5 kPa for freshwater.
  • Bed elevation drop: a 0.5% gradient over 100 m gives 0.5 m of additional elevation head.
  • Static depth: depth plus burial determines approximate chamber gauge pressure. It cancels between submerged inlet and outlet in the same river and is not added to driving head.
Δp_drive,ideal = ½ρv_capture² + ρgΔz_bed
Δp_static ≈ ρg(depth + burial) · delivery pressure only
P_hydraulic,in = ρgQΔH_boundary

The boundary head must include measured pressure, elevation and velocity terms. Outlet kinetic energy, pipe losses, two-phase flow and compression work reduce what can be recovered.

1.3 Funnel and Venturi

Continuity gives v_throat = Q/A_throat for the actual flow Q. An aperture-to-throat area ratio alone does not establish Q or an attainable throat velocity: the available head, losses, air loading and cavitation constrain the flow. Entrainment therefore needs measurement rather than a prescribed velocity threshold.

1.4 Energy accounting

Hydraulic input must cover compressed-air output and every loss. The proposed geometry is useful only if it transfers a measurable share of available river energy into loaded pneumatic output. Static pressure by itself cannot satisfy that balance.

2River HTH System Architecture▸

2.1 Core Components

  1. Funnel-Venturi Intake: Submerged funnel oriented upstream, with Venturi throat and air ports above waterline.
  2. Downpipe Array: Pipes descending from throat to buried separation chamber. Multiple parallel pipes increase throughput.
  3. Separation Chamber: Buried below river bed. Air separates from water and collects under pressure.
  4. Compressed Air Reservoir & Discharge: Air storage with controlled release. Powers air turbine for electricity.
  5. Water Return: Outflow pipe exits downstream, using river gradient for additional driving force.

2.3 DCC-Style Channel Governance

The multi-channel proposal creates a resource-allocation problem that could be studied with DCC-style methods. A proposed DCC controller would monitor measured channel output and allocate flow. Whether this improves energy recovery needs a controlled comparison.

2.4 Adaptive Polyline Pipe — Reading the River

The distinction between "horizontal" and "vertical" trompe is a false binary. Real rivers have continuously varying profiles. A candidate HTH layout is a polyline that adapts to local topography:

  • Fast shallow section → funnel/Venturi intake: candidate current capture
  • Steep drop or cascade → diagonal/vertical pipe run: classical trompe physics
  • Deep pool (tolmun) → possible separation site; installation requirements remain site-specific
  • Gentle glide → long diagonal run following riverbed gradient
  • Rocky step → vertical trompe segment: concentrated head gain

The pipe "reads" the river — it does not force a single geometry onto varied landscape. This suggests a layout-optimization problem with candidate nodes, boundary head, losses, transport constraints and installation cost.

Depth is a pressure condition, not a layout objective. In the head-balanced profile, the steep drop gives the largest estimated segment output. Layout selection must also satisfy bubble transport and losses.
3Dimensioning: River Applications▸

All outputs here are preliminary estimates from the head-balanced model. They are not prototype measurements, guaranteed yields or validated operating regimes.

3.1 Fast-river scenario

Declared scenario: current 3 m/s, river depth 4 m, gradient 0.3%, chamber burial 3 m. The model estimates 2,068.2 W (about 2.1 kW). At constant operation this would correspond arithmetically to about 18.1 MWh/year; actual duty, entrainment and losses are unverified.

The 7 m separator submergence corresponds to about 68.7 kPa static gauge pressure. That pressure is not driving head. A proposed 8:1 contraction is a geometry choice, not evidence of an attainable high-speed throat flow.

3.2 Personal-use scenario

Declared scenario: current 1 m/s, depth 1.5 m, gradient 1%, chamber burial 1 m. The model estimates 41.7 W, equivalent to about 365 kWh/year only at continuous operation. A useful output rating still depends on measured loaded airflow and pressure.

5Dimensioning: Ocean Applications▸

5.1 Tidal-current model scenario

The existing head-balanced tidal scenario uses 3 m/s current, 10 m depth, zero bed gradient and a 45° pipe angle. It gives 2,322.6 W (about 2.3 kW) under its declared assumptions. Depth supplies chamber pressure, not an extra power source. This is not a measured tidal-device output; current reversal, duty cycle, entrainment, fouling and loss models remain open.

5.2 Wave-column concept

A wave-driven water column could compress air through changing water level and pressure. This is a separate wave-energy input and needs its own hydraulic-to-pneumatic budget. No array output rating or maintenance-free operation has been established for this proposal.

6PV/DGTE Integration▸

PV cooling, low-temperature heat recovery and river-current compression are distinct energy paths. A DGTE/PV/HTH combination would need separate measurements and a shared loss budget before combined output or efficiency can be quoted.

  • PV: solar electrical generation.
  • PV thermal recovery: proposed cooling and additional heat-engine output.
  • HTH: proposed conversion of measured river or tidal hydraulic input.
  • Thermal gradients: require a heat engine and external hot/cold reservoirs.
  • Air storage: shifts energy in time and has charging/discharging losses.

No combined efficiency uplift or weather-independent baseload rating is established by the HTH model.

7Economics: HTH vs. Micro-Hydro▸

7.1 Compare equal useful output

A cost comparison with a turbine requires measured output, operating hours, installation cost, maintenance, fouling and replacement data. Removing rotating underwater parts does not establish zero maintenance or a particular service life.

7.2 Channel selection

Extra channels add cost, controls and failure modes. The proposed MDL-style comparison should include complete lifetime costs per delivered unit of useful energy. Output from a single hydraulic stream must not be counted twice through alternative conversion paths.

7.3 What the bench must establish

  • Usable pressure and airflow under load, not only unloaded chamber pressure.
  • Hydraulic input and pressure drop for the full intake-to-return boundary.
  • Availability under debris, biofouling and changing river conditions.
  • Installation and maintenance assumptions for a like-for-like turbine comparison.

No TCO advantage or return on investment is demonstrated by the present model.

8Cross-Domain Transfer — 8Z Kernel▸

MDL × DCC offers a possible way to compare pipe layouts, instrumentation and flow allocation. The physical experiment must stand on hydraulic and pneumatic measurements independently of that analogy.

9Originality Assessment & Kill Tests▸

9.1 Research contribution and prior art

The proposal combines current capture, air entrainment, terrain-adaptive piping and possible flow governance. Trompes, Venturi entrainment, wave chambers and compressed-air storage already exist. Related prior art includes submerged current-driven compressor concepts such as WO2016046689A1. Novelty of the specific combination requires a separate literature and patent review.

9.2 Three physical gates

  1. Entrainment: measure the onset and amount of air capture for actual flow and geometry, including cavitation.
  2. Bubble transport: compare air entrained with air delivered to the separator under pressure.
  3. Energy: compare hydraulic input across the inlet/outlet boundaries with useful pneumatic output and losses.

9.3 Model status

The head-balanced profile is not an all-gates pass: five of eight segments fail bubble transport. The model estimates 389.4 W before that transport filter and approximately 283 W after it. It neither proves practical operation nor settles thermodynamic closure of a real two-phase device.

9.4 Evidence still needed

Prototype measurements must establish entrainment, stable bubble transport, loaded pressure/flow, losses and repeatability before scale-up, service-life or economic claims.

10Open Questions for AI8 Family▸
  1. Funnel geometry: optimal contraction ratio for maximising entrainment while minimising cavitation?
  2. Bubble dynamics: how do bubbles survive the actually measured throat flow, pressure and cavitation conditions?
  3. Corrosion and biofouling in ocean deployments: anti-fouling coating vs. active CIP?
  4. Separation chamber design: does horizontal input require different geometry? CFD recommended.
  5. DCC governance: can we instrument 15 channels with real-time sensors at useful time-scales?
  6. Regulatory: environmental impact, fish passage, debris management?
  7. Wave column array: resonance tuning — pipe length matched to dominant wave period?
  8. Hybrid PV/DGTE/HTH: optimal site profile — sunny river valley? Coastal thermal gradient?
  9. Init paradox: does deliberately sub-optimal pipe placement lead to better flow discovery?
  10. Can the polyline layout optimiser become a standalone product even if the hardware is weak?
11Proposed Next Steps▸
  1. Bench boundary: define inlet/outlet elevations, pressures, velocities and flow. Record hydraulic input ρgQΔH across those boundaries.
  2. Transparent channel: vary actual flow and geometry; observe entrainment, bubble survival and cavitation.
  3. Loaded separator: measure pressure and air delivery rate, then compare pneumatic output with hydraulic input. Include leakage and pressure losses.
  4. Repeatability: repeat under changing flow and debris conditions before assigning a useful power rating.
  5. Scale and governance: only after that evidence, consider stream, ocean or adaptive-controller studies.
The first decision is whether measurable useful pneumatic output survives the complete energy balance. A pressure reading alone is insufficient.
